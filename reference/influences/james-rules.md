# M. R. James's Rules — Quoted, Then Translated

Full text of the essay: `reference/texts/craft-essays/james-mr__some-remarks-on-ghost-stories.md`
("Some Remarks on Ghost Stories", *The Bookman*, Christmas 1929 — public domain).

James is the most useful single source for this book because he is the only major
practitioner who wrote down what he was doing and why, and because his method — a
dull, careful, credible surface with one intolerable thing coming up through it — is
already the method in `craft/craft-principles.md`. What follows is his language on
the left and what it costs us on the right.

---

## 1. The narrator must be a contemporary, not a costume

> "The seer of ghosts must talk something like me, and be dressed, if not in my
> fashion, yet not too much like a man in a pageant, if he is to enlist my sympathy."

**For us.** Our narrators are not in a pageant either — but our "contemporary" is
1850–1920, so the trap inverts. The risk is not that our people sound too modern; it
is that they sound like *someone doing a nineteenth century voice*. A period narrator
should be unremarkable **to himself**. He does not notice the kerosene lamp. He
notices that the wick wants trimming again. See `style-guide.md` → Diction, and
`period/anachronism-traps.md`.

## 2. Definite time and place, matter-of-fact detail

> "...the greatest successes have been scored by the authors who can make us envisage
> a definite time and place, and give us plenty of clear-cut and matter-of-fact
> detail..."

**For us.** This is `craft-principles.md` §1 in its original form. Note *definite*:
not evocative, not atmospheric — **definite**. A named month. A distance in miles. A
price. The whole `period/` folder exists to make this cheap.

## 3. But be in the dark about the machinery

> "...but who, when the climax is reached, allow us to be just a little in the dark
> as to the working of their machinery. We do not want to see the bones of their
> theory about the supernatural."

**For us.** `craft-principles.md` §3, verbatim in substance. Note that James puts the
darkness **at the climax**, not throughout. The detail is dense right up to the
moment of contact and then goes deliberately thin. That is a *rhythm*, not a policy:
be most concrete in the approach and least concrete in the touch.

## 4. Reticence, but not mildness

> "Reticence may be an elderly doctrine to preach, yet from the artistic point of
> view I am sure it is a sound one. Reticence conduces to effect, blatancy ruins it,
> and there is much blatancy in a lot of recent stories."

And immediately after, so the doctrine is not mistaken for timidity:

> "At the same time don't let us be mild and drab. Malevolence and terror, the glare
> of evil faces, 'the stony grin of unearthly malice', pursuing forms in darkness,
> and 'long-drawn, distant screams', are all in place, and so is a modicum of blood,
> shed with deliberation and carefully husbanded; the weltering and wallowing that I
> too often encounter merely recall the methods of M. G. Lewis."

**For us.** These two paragraphs belong together and are usually quoted apart, which
turns James into a prude. He is not arguing for less; he is arguing for **less,
placed better**. "Carefully husbanded" is the phrase for the card: one withdrawal per
story, as in `craft-principles.md` §2.

The adjustment we need is temperature, not principle. This book's ghosts are patient and
vegetal (`bible/book-bible.md` → Tone), where James's are actively malign. But keep
the operative half: **the ghost must want something, and what it wants must be bad
for the living.** A ghost that is merely sad is scenery. A ghost that is merely
present is a mood.

Also worth transplanting whole: *"sex is tiresome enough in the novels; in a ghost
story, or as the backbone of a ghost story, I have no patience with it."* The
underlying craft point is that any second appetite competes with the first one.

## 5. No occult jargon, no systems

James dismisses Hesketh Prichard's Flaxman Low as "most ingenious and successful, but
rather over-technically 'occult'", and — remarkably — says the same of Blackwood:

> "It seems impertinent to apply the same criticism to Algernon Blackwood, but 'John
> Silence' is surely open to it."

**For us.** Already in the bible ("No shared monster with a name and a mythology", "No
epigraphs from invented grimoires"). James's point is sharper than a ban on grimoires:
*any* system is fatal, including a home-made one. The moment a story implies there are
rules that a competent person could learn, the haunting becomes a profession and the
fear becomes a procedure. Both John Silence volumes are in `texts/canon/` as a
worked example of the failure — read them for what to avoid.

## 6. Setting: the familiar, seen slightly wrong

James's own settings are a cathedral library, a golf links, a rented seaside room, a
provincial hotel with a room numbered 13. Nothing gothic. The horror arrives in
places where a reasonable man has already booked a bed.

**For us.** The Gulf-South equivalents are the equivalents of a golf links, not of a
castle: a rice field, a levee road, a general store, a ferry landing, a schoolhouse, a
dentist's parlour, a moss shed. `period/material-culture.md` is stocked accordingly.
Nothing in this book should happen in a mansion unless the mansion is being sold.

## 7. Pace: the slow start is not optional

James's characteristic shape, which is worth stating explicitly because it is
imitable:

1. A dry, faintly humorous frame — an antiquary, a purchase, a document, a holiday.
2. Ordinary business conducted at length, with jokes.
3. One detail that is wrong and is not remarked on.
4. The same detail, closer.
5. Contact — short, physical, tactile, and over almost before it is described.
6. A flat closing paragraph that declines to explain, often with a small,
   deliberately inadequate piece of afterward information.

Step 5 is nearly always **touch**, not sight: the hand in the dark, the thing in the
bed, the mouth. James's ghosts are felt before they are seen and are usually
described in terms of *texture* — hairy, damp, linen, thin. This is directly usable
for a book whose ghosts are wet.

## 8. His own aim, stated plainly

> "...here you have a story written with the sole object of inspiring a pleasing
> terror in the reader; and as I think, that is the true aim of the ghost story."

**For us.** *Pleasing* is doing real work in that sentence. The reader has to enjoy
being frightened, which means the frightening thing must be interesting to look at
and the prose must be good company on the way to it. A story that is only unpleasant
has failed his test even if it succeeds at being unpleasant.

## The single line to keep

> "We do not want to see the bones of their theory about the supernatural."

If a draft ever needs one test, that is it.

## Who he rated, in his own words

Worth recording because it is a reading list from the only person whose taste we are
deliberately borrowing:

> "Upon mature consideration, I do not think that there are better ghost stories
> anywhere than the best of Le Fanu's; and among these I should give the first place
> to 'The Familiar' (alias 'The Watcher')."

Also named: Le Fanu's "Mr Justice Harbottle", "Carmilla", "Schalken the Painter",
"Squire Toby's Will", "Dickon the Devil", "Madam Crowl's Ghost" — most of which are
in `texts/canon/lefanu__ghostly-tales-v1.txt` and `-v2.txt`. Then Mrs Oliphant's "The
Open Door" and *A Beleaguered City* (downloaded) as "the religious ghost story...
never done better"; Rhoda Broughton, Mrs Riddell, Mrs Henry Wood; and
Erckmann-Chatrian among the French.

On why Le Fanu works, James offers the most concrete piece of praise in the essay —
that he is "an artist in words", citing this as the proof:

> "The aerial image of the old house for a moment stood before her, with its peculiar
> malign, scared and skulking aspect."

*Scared* is the word to notice. The house is frightened. That is a whole technique in
one adjective: give the setting the emotion, and you never have to name the
character's. Compare `style-guide.md` → Emotion.

## Caveat on one famous quotation

The much-repeated formula that a ghost story should leave "a loophole for a natural
explanation... so narrow as not to be quite practicable" is **not in the 1929 essay**
and is not verified here. It is normally attributed to James's introduction to
*Ghosts and Marvels* (Oxford, 1924), which I could not retrieve — see
`reference/00-ACCESS-GAPS.md`. Treat the idea as sound and the wording as unconfirmed;
do not quote it as James's until someone has seen it on the page.
