# Reading Map — What to Read For What

**Read `../influences.md` first** — Mark's own list of what the book is reaching for.
It outranks this file, and it names several influences that are absent below purely
because they are in copyright (Aickman, Jackson, Waters, Hill, O'Connor, Welty,
McCarthy). This file covers what could actually be downloaded.

Every entry marked **[local]** is downloaded in full under `reference/texts/`.
This is not a syllabus. It is a lookup table: when a draft has a specific problem,
go to the writer who solved it.

---

## If the problem is: the mundane isn't buying the impossible

**M. R. James** — *Ghost Stories of an Antiquary* (1904), *More Ghost Stories* (1911),
*A Thin Ghost and Others* (1919). **[local, all three]**
The whole method. Start with **"The Mezzotint"** — a picture that changes between
viewings, which is this book's plate problem exactly, and which James solves by making
the observers a set of bored academics comparing notes. Then **"Oh, Whistle, and I'll
Come to You, My Lad"** for the tactile ghost, and **"The Ash-tree"** for a haunting
that is inherited property.
See `james-rules.md`.

## If the problem is: the atmosphere is thin

**J. Sheridan Le Fanu** — *Ghostly Tales* vols 1–2 **[local]**, *In a Glass Darkly*
**[local, vol. 2 — contains "Carmilla"]**.
James rated him above everyone, and singled out **"The Familiar"** (a.k.a. "The
Watcher") as the best ghost story in English. Also **"Schalken the Painter"**,
**"Madam Crowl's Ghost"**, **"Squire Toby's Will"**. Read for how ominousness is put
*into the description of ordinary objects and buildings* rather than into events.
**"Green Tea"** is the one to study for a haunting that is a medical case, a moral
case, and a real ghost simultaneously, without ever choosing.

## If the problem is: the haunting doesn't mean anything

**Mary E. Wilkins Freeman** — *The Wind in the Rose-Bush* (1903) **[local]**.
The best model in the canon for `craft-principles.md` §4. Freeman's ghosts are always
about somebody who was failed — a child, a poor relation, a woman with no money of her
own — and the living characters are always busy with domestic work while it happens.
**"The Lost Ghost"** and the title story. Short, plain, American, and devastating.

**Edith Wharton** — *Tales of Men and Ghosts* (1910) **[local]**.
The ghost as an unpaid debt, usually financial and always moral. **"Afterward"** is
the one: a haunting whose rule is that you do not recognise it until long afterward.
Wharton is also the best in the canon at making money concrete.

**Charlotte Riddell** — *The Uninhabited House* (1875) **[local]**.
Riddell's whole subject is property: rents, tenancies, wills, the value of a house
nobody will live in. Directly relevant to a book about inheritance and land. Her
*Weird Stories* ("The Open Door", "Nut Bush Farm") is better but not retrievable —
see `00-ACCESS-GAPS.md`.

## If the problem is: the ghost is too visible

**W. W. Jacobs** — *The Lady of the Barge* (1902) **[local]**, for **"The Monkey's
Paw"**. The most famous unopened door in English. Study the last page for how much can
be done with a knock and a bolt, and for the ruthlessness of ending on the absence.

**Henry James** — *The Turn of the Screw* (1898) **[local]**.
The reference case for ambiguity. Read it for structure, not for prose — the sentences
are the opposite of this book's house style. Note that M. R. James's essay closes with
a joke at its expense.

## If the problem is: the pace

**Margaret Oliphant** — *A Beleaguered City* (1879) **[local]**.
A whole town under supernatural siege, told in bureaucratic register by its mayor.
M. R. James called her and Le Fanu the best at "the religious ghost story". Useful
here for slow, communal, unhurried dread — the book's stated tone.

**Oliver Onions** — *Widdershins* (1911) **[local]**, for **"The Beckoning Fair One"**.
The long slow possession of a man by a house. The best study available of a haunting
that is indistinguishable from a decline, and of how to stretch dread over length.

## If the problem is: the story needs a folk register

**Elizabeth Gaskell** — *The Grey Woman and Other Tales* **[local]**, for **"The Old
Nurse's Story"**. Told aloud, by a servant, to children. The best model for a narrator
whose social position controls what she is allowed to notice.

**E. Nesbit** — *Grim Tales* (1893) **[local]**. Short, nasty, unsentimental.
**"Man-Size in Marble"** for a haunting on a calendar — it happens on a specific night
each year, which is a rule the reader can half-infer (`craft-principles.md` §3).

## If the problem is: American plainness

**Ambrose Bierce** — *Can Such Things Be?* (1893) **[local]**, *Present at a Hanging*
**[local]**.
Rural American ghost stories written as newspaper reports. Very short, very dry,
frequently a single paragraph too long. Read the *openings* — Bierce establishes place,
date and legal situation in two sentences.

## Read as cautionary examples

**Algernon Blackwood** — *Three John Silence Stories* **[local]**.
M. R. James's own diagnosis: "surely open to" the charge of being "over-technically
'occult'". A psychic detective explains the mechanism, and the fear evaporates on
contact with the explanation. This is exactly the failure `craft-principles.md` §3
forbids. Worth reading once, for the sensation of watching a good writer defuse his
own bomb.
(Blackwood's **"The Willows"** is a masterpiece and does none of this. Not in the
downloaded volume; worth finding.)

**Arthur Machen** — *The House of Souls* **[local]**, for "The Great God Pan" and "The
White People".
Machen's prose is the opposite pole from this book's — incantatory, adjectival, pagan.
"The White People" is nonetheless the best thing ever written about a child recording
supernatural events in a diary without understanding them, which is a device worth
having. Take the device, leave the diction.

**H. P. Lovecraft** — *Supernatural Horror in Literature* (1927) **[local, craft-essays]**,
plus six stories in `texts/weird-pulp/`.
`../influences.md` lists Lovecraft under **"Deliberately not"** — no cosmology, no
adjective-stacking — and that stands. The stories are downloaded because they were
asked for, and because "The Colour Out of Space" is a haunting of *land* rendered
through crops, water and livestock, which is structurally the nearest thing in his
work to what this book is doing.
Use as an index of the tradition. Do not use as a prose model — it is a compendium of
everything `ai-tells.md` bans, written by hand. His judgements on Machen, Blackwood,
James and Le Fanu are still worth reading.

## Reading order, if starting cold

1. James, "The Mezzotint" and "Oh, Whistle" — the method, in an hour.
2. Freeman, "The Lost Ghost" — the method with a wound in it.
3. Le Fanu, "The Familiar" — the method at maximum atmosphere.
4. Hearn, *Chita*, first twenty pages — the place.
5. James's essay, `texts/craft-essays/` — the theory, from the practitioner.
