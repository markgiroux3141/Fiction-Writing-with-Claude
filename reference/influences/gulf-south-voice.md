# The Gulf-South Register

Five writers who were on the ground in this book's period and geography, all
downloaded in `reference/texts/gulf-south/`. Read them for **nouns, weather,
distances, work and social gradient** — not for prose style. Only one of them writes
anything like the house voice.

Read the honesty note at the bottom before using any of them.

---

## Lafcadio Hearn — *Chita: A Memory of Last Island* (1889) **[local]**

A novella about the 1856 hurricane that destroyed Isle Dernière. It is the single
best piece of writing about this landscape, and its opening twenty pages are a
guided approach from New Orleans down to the Gulf by bayou steamer.

**Take:** the physical inventory. Luggers, the sugar-landing, canal-mouths, drenched
rice-fields, the "black silhouette of some irrigating machine", cypresses "all hoary
with the parasitic tillandsia", reedy morass, quaggy soil. Hearn names everything
correctly and the names are the whole point.

**Leave:** the sentences. Hearn writes in long, rapturous, dash-strung cadences —

> "...she crosses the river, slips into some canal-mouth, labors along the artificial
> channel awhile, and then leaves it with a scream of joy, to puff her free way down
> many a league of heavily shadowed bayou."

That is beautiful and it is banned by `style-guide.md` twice over (em-dash density,
personification, adjective load). Harvest the nouns; write our own verbs.

**Also relevant:** Hearn is the period's best source on Gulf storm behaviour, and
*Chita* is structurally a ghost story about a place — the island that was there and
then was not. See `period/swamp-and-weather.md`.

## George Washington Cable — *Strange True Stories of Louisiana* (1889) **[local]**, *Old Creole Days* (1879) **[local]**

*Strange True Stories* is the more useful of the two for us: Cable presents seven
narratives as recovered documents — diaries, family papers, a manuscript bought at
auction — with editorial apparatus around them. It is a full working model of the
**document device** in `the-19th-century-feel.md` §6, executed in this exact place and
period, by someone who was doing it forty years before we thought of it.

**Take:** the apparatus. How he introduces a document, how much provenance he gives,
how he handles gaps in a manuscript, how he lets a source be unreliable without
saying so.

**Note:** Cable was a white Southerner who publicly and expensively opposed the racial
order of his own region and was driven out of New Orleans for it. That does not make
his fiction free of the period's condescension, but it is a different position from
the one below.

## Kate Chopin — *Bayou Folk* (1894) **[local]**

Short stories set in Natchitoches Parish. The plainest, most modern prose of the five,
and the closest to the house style — Chopin writes short declarative sentences,
withholds interiority, and lets a scene end before the point is made.

**Take:** the prose model. If any of these five is worth imitating at sentence level,
it is Chopin. Also: her handling of work, money, and small-town obligation, and her
women who are constrained without being described as constrained.

**Also:** Chopin's "At Chênière Caminada" is about the community destroyed by the 1893
hurricane. She was writing near-contemporaneously.

## Grace King — *Balcony Stories* (1893) **[local]**

New Orleans stories framed as things overheard between women on a balcony in the
evening. Useful narrowly and specifically for the **frame of communal telling** — who
tells, who listens, what a story is allowed to be about in company. That frame is a
period artefact and it is exactly the container `the-19th-century-feel.md` §5 says to
borrow a piece of.

**Caution:** King wrote in explicit opposition to Cable's politics and her fiction is
substantially a defence of the antebellum order. Use her for form, watch her content.

## Alice Dunbar-Nelson — *The Goodness of St. Rocque, and Other Stories* (1899) **[local]**

Creole New Orleans by a Black woman writing from inside the city rather than about it.
The title comes from a shrine people actually petitioned; the stories move through
churches, charm-work, street trades and a Catholicism that is lived rather than
described.

**Take:** the religion. Dunbar-Nelson shows Catholic devotional practice as ordinary
domestic behaviour — a candle, a novena, a saint you are annoyed with — which is
precisely the register `period/folklore-and-belief.md` argues for. Nobody in her
stories explains a belief to anybody.

## Honesty note — required reading before you borrow from these

These are period texts about a period society, and four of the five were written by
white authors for a white readership in the decades that produced Jim Crow. The
material carries:

- dialect written phonetically, at length, for racially marked characters;
- racial epithets, in narration as well as dialogue;
- plantation nostalgia, most heavily in King and in parts of *Old Creole Days*;
- Black and Creole characters as texture, comic relief, or moral instrument.

**What this means in practice:**

1. **The phonetic dialect is already banned** by `style-guide.md` ("Regional voice
   through syntax and word choice, never phonetic spelling"). Do not let proximity to
   these texts erode that rule. It is the single most likely contamination.
2. **Take material culture, not social attitude.** A moss pole, a lugger, a novena
   card, the price of a barrel of rice — these are facts. The way these books rank the
   people holding them is not.
3. **This setting had a majority-Black rural population in this period.** A book of
   Louisiana ghost stories in which everyone is white is not neutral; it is a choice,
   and it is the same choice King made. The alternative is not to write dialect. It is
   to write characters with interior lives, work, money, property and grievance —
   which is what `craft-principles.md` §4 requires of *every* character anyway.
4. **The wound is available and it is real.** §4 asks for "a thing done to someone poor
   by someone comfortable". In this place and period that includes debt peonage, the
   crop lien, convict leasing in the logging camps, and land taken from people who
   could not record a title. These are historical facts, not metaphors, and they are
   what the ground here is actually made of. Handle them as facts.
   ⚠ How far the book goes into this is Mark's call and belongs in the bible.
