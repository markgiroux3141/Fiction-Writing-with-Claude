# How the Nineteenth-Century Feel Is Actually Made

The brief is "a 19th century feel", not a pastiche. This file is about the difference.
It is technique, distilled from the texts in `reference/texts/`, and it is subordinate
to `craft/style-guide.md` — where the two disagree, the style guide wins.

The short version: **period is produced by what the narrator takes for granted, not
by what the narrator says.**

---

## 1. The mechanism is friction, and modern readers have forgotten it

Almost everything a nineteenth-century character does costs something a modern reader
never pays. Light has to be made and will go out. Water has to be carried. A letter
takes four days. A doctor takes six hours to arrive and cannot do much when he does.
Meat spoils. Wet clothes stay wet.

This is the single largest source of free period texture, and it is also free
*suspense*, because every one of those frictions is a delay you can put between a
character and safety. A woman who wants to leave the house at night in 1887 has to
decide whether to take the lamp — and if she takes it, the house behind her is dark,
and if she leaves it, she is.

Use the friction. Do not explain it. See `period/material-culture.md`.

## 2. Sentences: longer, but not more ornate

The reliable difference between period prose and modern prose is not vocabulary. It is
**clause length and connective habit**. Nineteenth-century narrators:

- run clauses together with semicolons and *and* where we would use full stops;
- qualify in mid-sentence rather than in a following sentence;
- put circumstantial information first ("Having no further business in the town, I
  walked down to the landing") where we front the subject.

But — and this is where pastiche dies — they are not *florid*. Le Fanu, Gaskell,
Riddell and Freeman all write plainer sentences than their reputation suggests. The
ornament is Victorian *magazine* prose, not Victorian *good* prose.

**Practical setting:** allow the long compound sentence, ban the decorative adjective.
This is compatible with the style guide's "vary length aggressively" — the variation
just happens around a longer mean.

## 3. Understatement was the period's own house style

The most frightening sentences in the canon are the flattest. From Le Fanu, Freeman,
and James alike, the pattern is: the narrator reports an impossibility in the same
register used for the weather, and declines to comment.

This is very good news, because it is also the house style in `style-guide.md`. The
period voice and the anti-AI voice want the same thing. Where the AI default is to
*intensify* ("a wave of dread washed over him"), both the period and the house style
*deflate* ("I did not care for it, and I went back inside").

## 4. Emotion is behaviour, and the period does this better than we do

Nineteenth-century narrators are constrained — by manners, by class, by religion, by
what a person may say aloud to another person — and constraint is exactly the engine
`style-guide.md` asks for under **Emotion: render, do not name**. Period manners give
you a free, built-in reason for every character to under-report their own feelings.

Look at what Mary Wilkins Freeman does in *The Wind in the Rose-Bush*
(`texts/canon/freeman__the-wind-in-the-rose-bush.txt`): two women discussing a
missing child while performing hospitality. Nobody says anything true for pages. The
horror is entirely in what the housekeeping is being used to avoid.

## 5. The frame narrative, and why to use only a piece of it

The period's default container is the frame: a manuscript found, a story told at an
inn, a document from a solicitor, "the following account was given to me by a man I
had no reason to disbelieve."

The frame is not decoration. It does three jobs:
1. It licenses the flat, factual voice.
2. It builds in a **reason the ending cannot be resolved** — the source is gone, the
   document breaks off, the teller died.
3. It puts distance between the reader and the impossible thing, which paradoxically
   makes it easier to accept.

**But**: the bible leans against a full found-album frame (§Premise, option (b)), and
a frame per story would be a tic by story four. Use the *effect* without the
apparatus — a narrator who is visibly writing this down, and who mentions once, early
and briefly, why. That is enough. See also §9 below on endings.

## 6. Documents inside the story

Cheaper than a frame and much more useful: the period is full of paper, and paper is
evidence. A ledger line. A tax notice. A doctor's bill. A letter that says less than
the writer knew. A page of a family Bible with a birth entered and no death.

Documents are the highest-yield period device available to us, because they:
- are *definite* in James's sense — dated, priced, named;
- let the reader know something the character does not (`craft-principles.md` §5);
- can be wrong, and their wrongness needs no explanation.

The plate inventory and a photographer's day-book are the obvious ones for this book.

## 7. Names, titles, and forms of address

Period is carried in address more than in diction. *Mrs* Fontenot, not Marie. Men are
surnamed by other men. Servants and employees are surnamed or first-named on a strict
gradient that the reader will feel without being told. A doctor is "Doctor" to
everyone including his friends.

Get this right and you can write otherwise-modern sentences and still be in 1890.
Get it wrong and no amount of *hither* will save you.

## 8. The supernatural is not yet a genre the characters know

This is the hardest thing to hold, and the most valuable. Our characters have not seen
a horror film. They have no vocabulary of *haunting*, *possession*, *paranormal*, *the
entity*. What they have instead is:

- religion — and two different ones in this setting (`period/folklore-and-belief.md`);
- folk practice, held lightly and denied in company;
- Spiritualism, which in this period is a *respectable, modern, faintly scientific*
  enthusiasm, not a fringe one;
- and plain refusal to think about it.

A character who says "the house is haunted" has stepped out of 1890 and into 1975.
A character who stops using one room, and has a reason ready that nobody asked for,
is in 1890.

## 9. Endings: the period ends worse than we do, and that is correct

Nineteenth-century ghost stories routinely end with an anticlimax, a shrug, a legal
disposal of the property, or a paragraph of afterward-information that does not
resolve anything. Modern instincts want a final image. The period's instinct — and
James's, and the house instinct in `ai-tells.md` §B ("the balanced ending sentence")
— is to stop.

The best period ending is a small administrative fact delivered flatly after the
horror: the house was let again; the plate was sold with the rest of the effects; she
lived another thirty years and did not speak of it.

## 10. What to steal and what to leave

**Steal:** long compound sentences; understatement; documents; forms of address;
friction; anticlimactic endings; the narrator who is scrupulous about small facts.

**Leave:** *ejaculated* as a dialogue tag; phonetic dialect (banned outright by the
style guide); the omniscient moralising aside; comic servants; the coincidence-driven
plot; racial and class assumptions rendered without a modern author's judgement.

That last one is not optional and is discussed in `gulf-south-voice.md` — the local
texts we are borrowing register from are the ones with the worst of it in them.
