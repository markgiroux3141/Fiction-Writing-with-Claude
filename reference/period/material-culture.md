# Material Culture — The Physical World, 1850–1920

A stock of correct nouns and correct frictions. `style-guide.md` says *name things
exactly*; this is the inventory that makes that possible without research mid-draft.

**Use two or three of these per page, not ten.** A list of period objects is a costume.
One object, used, is a period.

---

## Light — the most useful system in the book

There is **no electric light in the rural Gulf South in this entire period.** More
than 90% of rural American homes were still on kerosene at the start of the 1930s.

| Source | Period | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Tallow or wax candle | throughout, cheapest | Gutters, wants snuffing, smells of what it's made of |
| Kerosene lamp | standard from the 1860s | The default. Glass chimney, cotton wick, brass burner |
| Lantern | throughout | The one you carry outdoors; wire bail, glass globe |
| Pine knot / lightwood | throughout, poorest | Resinous, smoky, cheap |
| Gas | towns only, from mid-century, "widespread" 1880s but expensive | Not in a parish house |

**The frictions, which are the point:**
- A lamp must be **filled**, and kerosene is bought by the gallon and smells.
- The **wick must be trimmed** or the flame smokes and blacks the chimney.
- The **chimney must be washed** — a daily chore, usually a woman's or a child's.
- A lamp carried from room to room means every other room is dark, and the light
  moves with the character. A character who sets the lamp down and walks past it is
  a character walking into their own shadow.
- Lamps are **put out at bedtime**. A house at 2 a.m. in 1890 is genuinely, totally
  dark. Nobody flicks anything on.
- Matches: friction "lucifers" from the 1830s, safety matches after 1855. They are
  struck on anything, they fail in damp, and in this climate they fail often.

That last one is free: **a box of matches that will not strike is a period-accurate
delay device.** See `style-guide.md` → "Describe by consequence".

## Water and cold

- Water is **drawn, pumped or caught**. Cisterns are standard on the Gulf Coast —
  a wooden or brick tank catching roof runoff. They are also, it turns out, where the
  mosquitoes breed; covering them is what ended yellow fever in 1905.
- No indoor plumbing in a rural house. A privy, a washstand with a basin and ewer,
  a slop jar, a hip bath filled by hand from the stove.
- **Ice** is available earlier here than a modern reader expects. New Orleans had
  mechanical ice manufacturing from the 1860s, and the practice spread through
  Southern cities in the following decades. But ice is a *town* commodity, bought and
  carried, kept in a sawdust-insulated icebox, and gone by evening.
- Nothing keeps. Milk turns. Meat is salted, smoked, or eaten today.

## Heat and cooking

Wood or coal stove; open hearth in older or poorer houses. The stove is **out** at
night and must be got going in the morning. A cold ring is a period fact and a
behavioural detail at once — `style-guide.md` already uses "she puts the kettle on
the cold ring" as its model of unnamed fear.

## Floors, walls, damp

Cypress boards; brick piers holding the house off wet ground; a gallery or porch
running the front. Wallpaper lifts at the seam. Plaster blooms. Everything made of
iron is rusting, everything made of cloth is mildewing, and the smell of a closed
room in August is a real and specific smell. Milled cypress is the local wood and it
is rot-resistant, which is why it was worth an industry.

## Getting about

- **On foot** and **on horseback**. A wagon or a buggy for a family.
- **Pirogue** — the shallow dugout for bayou water. **Lugger** — a small sailing
  workboat. **Skiff**, **flatboat**, **bateau**.
- **Bayou steamers** — light, narrow, purpose-built for shallow winding water.
- **Ferries** at crossings. A ferryman is a period profession and a good
  ghost-story character: he knows who crossed and when.
- Railroads reach the towns; "dummy lines" run into the logging swamps from the
  early 1890s.
- Distances are in **miles** and journeys in **hours or days**. Six miles is a long
  way at night.

## Work — what a character does all day

The setting's trades, most of which involve water:

**Cypress logging** (1870s–1920s, industrialising after 1889). Fellers working from
platforms above the buttress; a crew cutting 35–40 trees a day; the **pullboat**
dragging 125–150 logs a day out of the swamp on cable; the **skidder**; the dummy
line; the mill. Men in **overalls, hip boots and straw hats**. Trees to 100 feet.

**Spanish moss picking.** Pulled down with a **long cane pole with a wire hook**,
hung on fences to cure and rot off its grey outer bark, then baled and sold for
mattress and upholstery stuffing. A cash crop for poor families, done alongside other
work. The cured fibre is black and springy and looks like horsehair.

**Sugar cane, rice, cotton** — depending on parish. Grinding season for cane is
autumn and is when the ledgers get settled.

**Trapping** (muskrat, later nutria), fishing, oystering, shrimping.

**Sharecropping and tenancy.** In 1880 about a third of Louisiana farmers were
tenants; by 1910, 55%. The **crop lien** is the mechanism: the merchant or landlord
advances seed, tools, cloth, doctor's visits against the coming crop, and at
settlement most tenants owe more than they made. This is the economic ghost of the
region and it is `craft-principles.md` §4 waiting to be used.

**Other useful jobs:** ferryman, store-keeper, levee worker, ice man, itinerant
photographer, midwife, undertaker (a new trade in this period), circuit preacher,
schoolteacher, tax assessor, surveyor.

## Money

Quote it in dollars and cents and be mean about it — a dollar a day is a wage. But
much of this economy is not cash: it is **store credit, scrip, and the ledger**.
A character's debt is a written number in someone else's book, which is a
frighteningly good object for a ghost story.

## Paper — the highest-yield props

Tax notice. Deed. Crop-lien contract. Store ledger. Doctor's bill. Family Bible with
births and deaths entered on the flyleaf. Letter. Newspaper. Almanac. Seed catalogue.
Photographer's day-book. A carte-de-visite or cabinet card with a studio name printed
on the mount.

## Clothing, quickly

Men: cotton or wool trousers, braces, collarless shirt with detachable collar for
town, waistcoat, brogans or boots, hat always outdoors. Women: layered — chemise,
corset, petticoats, dress; apron for work; hat or bonnet outdoors; hair up after
girlhood. **Everything is heavy, everything takes time to get out of, and wet cloth
is genuinely dangerous in cold water.** Mourning dress has its own rules —
`death-and-mourning.md`.

## Sound

Worth its own note, since `style-guide.md` says sound does the most work. The period
soundscape has no engine hum, no refrigerator, no traffic, no radio. Which means:
insects, frogs, dogs at distance, a church bell, a train whistle miles off, wind in
cane, water, the house itself moving. And it means **silence is much deeper and much
more common**, so a single sound carries further and matters more.

## Smell

Kerosene, woodsmoke, tallow, hot iron, coffee, horse, wet cypress, mud at low water,
mildew, lye soap, camphor, lime (which is thrown on graves and privies both).

is no excuse.
