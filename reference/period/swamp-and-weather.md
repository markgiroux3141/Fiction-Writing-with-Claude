# Swamp, Water and Weather

The landscape the plates read as, and the physics of it. `style-guide.md` says *name
things exactly*: this is where the names are.

The governing fact: **this is a place where the ground and the water are not reliably
distinguishable, and where the boundary moves.** That is not atmosphere. It is
hydrology, and it is the book's central image handed over for free.

---

## The trees

**Bald cypress** (*Taxodium distichum*). The tree of the place. Deciduous conifer —
it goes bare in winter, which surprises people. Flared, fluted **buttress** at the
base; **knees** rising out of the water around it, whose function is still argued
over. Rot-resistant heartwood, which is why it was worth an industry. Grows to 100
feet and older than the parish.

**Tupelo gum** — the other swamp tree, swollen at the base, standing in the same
water.

**Live oak** — on the ridges and the *chênières* (oak-covered sandy ridges: the word
is *chêne*, oak). Not a swamp tree. If there are live oaks, the ground is higher and
somebody probably built there.

**Willow, water oak, sweet gum, palmetto** in the understory.

**Spanish moss** (*Tillandsia usneoides*) — an epiphyte, not a parasite, and not a
moss. Hearn calls the cypresses "hoary with the parasitic tillandsia", which is the
period's usual error. It hangs in grey curtains, holds water, and was a **cash crop**:
pulled down with a long cane pole with a wire hook, hung on fences to cure until the
grey outer bark rots off, then baled and sold for mattress and upholstery stuffing.
The cured fibre is black, springy, and looks like horsehair.

> Worth pausing on. Curing Spanish moss is **deliberately rotting a hanging grey mass
> until the black inside comes out**, done in the yard, over weeks, by the whole
> family, as ordinary paid work. If a story needs one job that is both economically
> real and quietly horrible, that is the one.

## The water

- **Bayou** — a slow or still watercourse; often a former river channel. It can flow
  either way with wind and tide.
- **Coulee** — a small drainage channel.
- **Slough**, **borrow pit**, **ditch**, **canal** — the artificial and semi-artificial
  water, and the most useful for us, because it is water that somebody *made* and now
  cannot control.
- **Marsh** — grass, no trees. **Swamp** — trees, standing water.
- **Batture** — the ground between the levee and the river, which floods every year
  and which people nonetheless live on.
- **Chênière** — the oak ridge. **Cheniere Caminada** was a real settlement on one.

**Standing versus running.** The distinction is real, it is visible, and everybody
locally knows which is which. Still water goes anoxic, grows duckweed and a skin,
breeds mosquitoes, and smells. Moving water does not. Given the book's title and its
haunt-logic rule (`craft-principles.md` §3 — "it appears in still water but not
running water" is literally the example given), this is the physical fact the whole
collection is standing on. It should be described with total accuracy and never
remarked upon.

**Water levels move.** Spring high water from upriver snowmelt and rain; low water
in late summer and autumn, when things that were under water are not. **A drought is
as good a plot engine as a flood, and less used.**

**Levees.** Earthen, built and maintained by hand and by contract labour, walked and
inspected during high water. A **crevasse** is a levee breach and is a named,
remembered, dated local event. Levee work is seasonal paid labour
(`material-culture.md`).

## The animals — names, because "a bird" is banned

Alligator. Nutria (**not before the 1930s** — it is an introduced species; use
muskrat). Cottonmouth (water moccasin), which does not flee. Snapping turtle.
Crawfish, and crawfish chimneys in a wet field. Gar. Catfish.

Birds: **grackle** (the style guide's own example), great blue heron, egret, ibis,
anhinga (drying its wings on a snag with them spread), barred owl — whose call is the
one that sounds like a question. Chuck-will's-widow at dusk, the southern equivalent
of the whip-poor-will, and traditionally a death omen.

Insects: mosquitoes above all — and after 1900 the reader knows what they are doing
and the characters do not (`medicine-and-disease.md`). Cicadas in late summer, loud
enough to have to speak over. Deerflies. Fireflies over water at dusk, which is what
the feu follet is not.

## Weather

**Summer.** Hot and wet from May to September. The afternoon thunderstorm is a daily
event, arrives fast, and ends. Everything is damp indoors: paper cockles, matches
fail, salt cakes, leather greens, cloth mildews in a closed press.

**Winter.** Mild, grey, and wetter than people expect. Hard freezes happen and are
remembered. Fog on still water on a cold morning — thick, low, and local.

**Hurricanes.** The regional catastrophe, and the period has three that are worth
knowing by name:

| Date | Storm | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| 10 Aug 1856 | **Last Island (Isle Dernière)** | A resort island split in two; at least 198 dead. The subject of Hearn's *Chita*. |
| 1–2 Oct 1893 | **Chênière Caminada** | Landfall near 130 mph; around 2,000 dead. **Still the deadliest storm in Louisiana history.** Kate Chopin was writing about the community within months. |
| 1915 | — | Struck the rebuilt Chênière Caminada and ended the recovery. The graves of the 1893 dead are still out there, going into the marsh. |

**What to take.** Not the storm — a hurricane is too big and too fast for the tone in
`book-bible.md`. Take the **afterward**: a landscape rearranged, a settlement that is
now a name and some brick, bodies recovered from trees and from other people's
property, and a whole community's dead buried at once in ground that will not keep
them. The 1893 storm is the historical event best matched to this book's subject.

## The cypress boom — the industry that ate the setting

1870s to the 1920s, peaking around 1890–1915.

- **1883** — the steam **skidder**: a winch that drags logs to the line.
- **1889** — **pullboats**: barges with steam winches that drag logs out through
  channels cut radially into the swamp, leaving a fan of "pullboat runs" still visible
  from the air today.
- **early 1890s** — **dummy lines**: temporary railroads laid into the swamp.
- **1909** — ten mills on Bayou Teche alone.

The work: a crew felling 35–40 trees a day; a pullboat dragging 125–150 logs a day.
Men working in **overalls, hip boots and straw hats**, standing on springboards above
the buttress, in water, among snakes, for a day wage.

The company town: commissary, scrip, housing owned by the mill, a doctor on the
company's account. Convict leasing and debt peonage in and around this industry are
historical fact.

> **The whole boom is a haunting already.** In fifty years an industry cut down a
> forest that had stood for a thousand, left behind a drained and subsiding
> landscape, and moved on. The ground kept the channels. `craft-principles.md` §4
> asks whether there is a wound under the ghost. In this setting the wound is
> the geography.

## Practical notes

- **Sound carries strangely over flat water**, especially at night and in fog. A voice
  from a mile away can be perfectly clear. This is true, it is verifiable, and it does
  the job that "the darkness seemed to breathe" (banned, `ai-tells.md` §A) is
  reaching for.
- **Nothing dries.** A wet dress stays wet. A wet ghost stays wet
  (`style-guide.md` → Description).
- **Still water is a mirror**, and a very good one in low light. It is also opaque —
  the reflection is the entire visible surface, because there is nothing to see
  through to. That is the physics of the book's title and of Plate III.
- **Distances are deceptive** across flat ground and open water, and everybody local
  knows it, and they are still wrong sometimes.
