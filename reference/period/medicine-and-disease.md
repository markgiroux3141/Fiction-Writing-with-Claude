# Medicine and Disease, 1850–1920

What a body could do to a person, and what could be done about it. The useful thing
for a ghost story is not the disease but the **epistemic situation**: for most of this
period, competent, sober, educated people watched their families die without knowing
why, and had explanations that were confident and wrong.

That is the ground a haunting grows in. A character who says "the fever came off the
water" is not being superstitious. They are stating the medical consensus.

---

## Yellow fever — the regional catastrophe

Called **Yellow Jack**, **Bronze John**, the **saffron scourge**.

| Year | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1853 | Worst Louisiana year. c. 29,120 cases; c. 8,647 dead. |
| 1878 | Lower Mississippi Valley epidemic. >74,000 cases, c. 16,000 dead across five states. Starts in New Orleans, reaches Memphis. |
| 1905 | New Orleans, c. 450 dead. **The last yellow fever epidemic in the United States.** |

**The course of the disease**, because it should be got right if it appears: fever and
chills, then an apparent recovery, then in the severe cases a return with jaundice —
the yellow — and internal haemorrhage. The characteristic sign is **black vomit**,
which is digested blood. It is fast. A person can be well at breakfast and dead within
days.

**What people believed.** That it came from filth, bad air, rotting vegetation, and
the ground itself; that it was carried by goods and people from an infected city.
Hence **quarantine**, which was the main tool from the 1850s and which had real
social consequences: upriver towns and Gulf ports shut trade with New Orleans at the
first rumour, so an epidemic isolates a district economically as well as physically.
Shotgun quarantines — armed men on a road turning back travellers — are period-real.

**The turn.** Finlay proposes the mosquito in 1881 and is ridiculed for nineteen
years. Reed confirms it in 1900. New Orleans acts in 1905: cover the cisterns, screen
the windows, drain the standing water. It works immediately, which must have been an
extraordinary thing to live through.

> **For this book.** The 1900 date splits the period in half. Before it, standing water
> is *known* to be dangerous and *not known* to be dangerous in the way it actually
> is — the belief is right about the water and wrong about the mechanism. That gap is
> almost too neat for a collection called *Standing Water*, and should probably be
> used once, quietly, and never named.

## Malaria

Endemic, chronic, and much less dramatic than yellow fever: "the ague", "chills and
fever", recurring on a cycle the sufferer learns to predict. Treated with quinine —
bitter, widely available, taken in tonics and in whiskey. A character with malaria has
a **fever that comes back on a schedule**, which is a period-accurate way to give a
narrator recurring, unreliable, feverish nights without inventing anything.

## Everything else that killed people

Tuberculosis ("consumption") — slow, wasting, and the great nineteenth-century death,
with a whole aesthetics attached to it. Cholera and typhoid, both waterborne.
Diphtheria and scarlet fever, which took children in clusters — **a family losing
three children in a fortnight is period-normal**, and is the kind of wound
`craft-principles.md` §4 is asking for. Childbed fever. Tetanus from a cut, in a place
full of rusting iron and animal ground. Infections generally, with nothing to treat
them: antibiotics are decades away.

**Infant and child mortality is high enough that it structures family life.** Children
are named after dead siblings. A family Bible flyleaf with five births and three
deaths in it is not gothic; it is ordinary, and it is a document
(`influences/the-19th-century-feel.md` §6).

## What a doctor could actually do

Not much, and this matters for pacing. A country doctor in 1880:

- **arrives hours later**, on horseback, if he was found and if he was sober;
- can set a bone, lance an abscess, stitch, pull a tooth, deliver a baby;
- can give opium or morphine, quinine, calomel (a mercury purgative), whiskey, and
  digitalis;
- can bleed, blister, or purge — declining practices, still around mid-century;
- performs surgery rarely, at home, on a kitchen table. Ether and chloroform are
  available from the late 1840s, so anaesthesia exists; **antisepsis** is the later
  and more consequential arrival, from the 1870s–80s;
- **charges**, and the charge goes on the store ledger or the crop lien.

He cannot: diagnose most infections, treat any of them, take an X-ray (1895 onward,
and not out here), or transfuse.

**Germ theory** wins general acceptance in the 1880s, which means a doctor's
explanatory framework in 1865 and in 1895 are genuinely different, and an older doctor
in 1895 may still be working on the older one.

## Nursing is done by women at home

There is no hospital within reach for most of this population. The sick are kept in a
room in the house, watched in shifts, day and night, for weeks. **Sitting up with the
sick and sitting up with the dead are the same practice at different stages** — see
`death-and-mourning.md`. The exhaustion is real, prolonged, and an entirely
period-accurate reason for a narrator to be unreliable at three in the morning.

## The useful ambiguities

Things that were medically real in this period and that look exactly like a haunting:

- **Fever delirium**, which is common, and which everyone present understands to be
  common, so a delirious person's report is discounted by the household as a matter
  of course.
- **Malarial relapse** on a cycle — the same experience returning on the same days.
- **Laudanum and morphine**, freely available, taken by respectable people, in
  patent medicines with unlisted contents.
- **Lead, mercury and arsenic**: calomel is mercury; arsenic is in some pigments and
  some wallpapers; both produce neurological effects.
- **Carbon monoxide** from a badly drawing flue or a closed stove — headache,
  confusion, and the classic haunted-house symptomatology.
- **Ergotism** from spoiled grain. Rare, real, and the standard sceptic's explanation.

`craft-principles.md` §2 says one impossible thing and everything else obeys physics.
This list is how you obey physics while still frightening people — and per
`james-rules.md`, the natural explanation should be *available and unsatisfying*,
never adjudicated on the page.
