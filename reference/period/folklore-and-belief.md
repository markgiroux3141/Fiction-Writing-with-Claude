# Belief, 1850–1920 — What People Actually Thought Was Out There

The mistake to avoid is a single "folk belief" layer. In this place and period there
are at least four systems running at once in the same household, and educated people
hold several of them simultaneously without embarrassment.

`craft-principles.md` §3 forbids explaining the haunting. This file is not an
explanation kit. It is a guide to **what a character reaches for instead of an
explanation** — which is where the period voice lives.

---

## 1. Catholic — the Acadian and Creole south

Not a background detail; a working technology. Candles lit for named intentions. The
novena — nine days of prayer for a specific request. Saints petitioned for specific
jobs, and reproached when they do not deliver. Mass said for the dead. Holy water in
the house. A blessed palm behind a picture. All Saints' and All Souls' (1 and 2
November) — **the graves are cleaned, whitewashed and dressed with flowers, and the
family spends the day at the tomb.**

That last is the most useful single fact in this file. It is an annual, communal,
entirely ordinary occasion on which everyone in the parish stands at the graves of
their dead and tidies them. Anything that goes wrong on that day goes wrong in public.

Purgatory matters here. A Catholic dead person can be *in transit* — not damned, not
finished, and reachable by the actions of the living. A haunting inside this framework
is a **debt of prayer**, not a monster.

Alice Dunbar-Nelson (`influences/gulf-south-voice.md`) is the model for writing this
without explaining it.

## 2. Protestant — Baptist and Methodist, and the rest of the parishes

A different apparatus and a different relationship to the dead. No purgatory, so no
in-transit soul, so a ghost is theologically awkward — which makes it *more*
frightening to a devout Protestant, not less, because there is no legitimate category
for it. Camp meetings and revivals. The circuit preacher who comes monthly. Providence
as an explanation for illness and disaster. Hymns everyone knows by heart, which are a
free and period-accurate source of language.

**The two systems live in adjacent parishes and intermarry.** A mixed household is a
built-in structural conflict that requires no invention.

## 3. Folk practice — held lightly, denied in company

Not a religion; a set of things people do. Sensible people do them and would deny it.

- Mirrors covered and clocks stopped at a death (`death-and-mourning.md`).
- Bees told of a death; a house animal that must be informed.
- A bird in the house, or one striking a window, as a death sign.
- Salt, a broom laid across a doorway, a horseshoe.
- Naming taboos — not saying what you do not want to attract. Blackwood's "The
  Willows" (`texts/canon/blackwood__the-willows.txt`) states the rule outright:
  *"Do not refer to them by name. To name is to reveal."*
- Dreams read as messages, and dream-books consulted.
- The dead spoken to, at the grave, out loud, as a normal thing.

**Register note:** these are done *briskly*, in passing, by busy people. A character
who performs a folk practice with reverence has become a character in a folk-horror
film. A character who throws salt over her shoulder while talking about the drainage
is in 1890.

## 4. Conjure, hoodoo, and the New Orleans trade

Real, historically documented, African-American in origin, and central to the region's
actual religious life. It has practitioners, a client base across racial lines, and a
material culture — roots, powders, candles, charms, the shrine and the petition.

**Handle with care, and read `influences/gulf-south-voice.md` before writing any of
it.** The period sources are almost all outsider accounts, most of them hostile,
sensationalising, or both, and the whole subject arrives in the twentieth century
pre-distorted by tourism. Three working rules:

1. **It is a religion, not a plot device.** If it functions in the story as a
   mechanism the protagonist can operate, that is `craft-principles.md` §3's
   "solvable mechanism" failure with an added insult on top.
2. **Practitioners are people with businesses, families and reputations.** Not
   oracles, not warnings, not atmosphere.
3. **The safest and best use is peripheral and social:** who consults whom, what the
   neighbours say about it, what a Catholic woman will and will not admit to having
   done. That is all a story needs, and it is true.

⚠ Whether the book goes near this at all is Mark's call. It is defensible either way;
what is not defensible is doing it casually.

## 5. Spiritualism — the modern, respectable option

**The most under-used resource in this file**, because modern readers assume it was
fringe. It was not. At its peak, contemporaries estimated something like six million
American practitioners, and its adherents included the educated, the wealthy and the
progressive. It was associated with abolitionism and with women's rights.

Chronology:
- **1848** — the Fox sisters, Hydesville, New York. Rapping: a set number of knocks
  for yes or no.
- **1850s onward** — table-tipping, spirit rapping, and from 1853 the first Western
  use of automatic writing at a séance. The **planchette** and its descendants develop
  through the second half of the century.
- **1861** — Mumler's first spirit photograph (`photography/spirit-and-postmortem.md`).
- **1861–65** — the Civil War, and 620,000 dead, most of them buried far from home and
  many never identified. This is the engine.
- **1888** — Maggie Fox publicly confesses that she and her sister produced the raps
  by cracking their joints. **The movement continues regardless**, which is itself the
  most interesting fact about it.

**Why it is useful here.** A séance is a period-accurate way to get a group of people
into a dark room with a stated procedure, a set of rules, and a shared expectation —
and to have them be *modern, rational and slightly embarrassed* about it rather than
credulous. The characters are not seeking a ghost; they are trying a new thing that
respectable people in the city are doing. That is a very different scene from the one
the genre usually writes.

The fraud is also useful. Everyone knows some mediums cheat. So does everyone at the
table. The scepticism is period-accurate and it is free ambiguity.

## 6. The lights on the water — feu follet

The regional apparition, and the one that is already half this book's subject.

**Names.** *Feu follet* (French, roughly "fool's fire"); *fifolet* in Louisiana usage.
Cognate with the will-o'-the-wisp.

**What it is.** A light seen over the water at night. It appears as a distant lantern,
a house light, or someone signalling for help. Followed, it leads the traveller into
deep water or into ground he cannot get out of, and he drowns.

**What people said it was**, and the disagreement matters more than any single answer:
- swamp gas;
- the soul of an unbaptised child;
- a soul sent back from the dead to do penance, which attacks the living out of spite
  instead;
- a soul in purgatory — the Catholic reading.

**Why it fits.** It is a light on still water that pretends to be a person, and this is
a book called *Standing Water* whose second section is Reflections. Note also that it
is the rare traditional apparition whose method is **imitation of the ordinary** — it
works precisely by being unremarkable, which is `craft-principles.md` §1 in folk form.

**⚠ Warning.** For that exact reason it is the most over-determined item in this folder
and the easiest to over-use. The bible forbids "a shared monster with a name and a
mythology". Using the feu follet by name in more than one story would build one. The
better move is almost certainly to use the *behaviour* — a light that is a lantern
until you go towards it — and never let the phrase into the prose.

## 7. What nobody has

No vocabulary of *paranormal*, *supernatural entity*, *energy*, *psychic*, *closure*,
*the veil*, *dark forces*, *residual haunting*, *cleansing*. See
`anachronism-traps.md`. What they have instead is scripture, folk practice, medical
language (`medicine-and-disease.md`), law and property language, and — most often —
silence and a changed subject.
