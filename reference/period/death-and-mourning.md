# Death and Mourning, 1850–1920

Death happens at home in this period, and the household handles the body. That is the
central fact, and it is worth more to a ghost story than any amount of graveyard
scenery: **the dead are physically present in the parlour for one to three days, and
the living have jobs to do around them.**

---

## The sequence, in order

**1. The clock is stopped.** At the moment of death, and often the hands are left at
that time. Practically it records the hour; the belief attached is that time has
stopped for the dead person and should not be hurried.

**2. Mirrors are covered.** Sheets, crape, or the glass turned to the wall. The stated
reason varies and is worth noting exactly: in the Southern version, so the departing
spirit does not see its own reflection and become caught in the glass.

> This is directly on the book's **Reflections** motif (`book-bible.md`). It is a
> real, documented custom, it is a rule that people obeyed without being able to
> justify, and it gives an entire household a reason to have covered every reflective
> surface in a house before the story begins. Do not waste it on exposition — a
> narrator who arrives at a house and finds the mirrors sheeted knows exactly what
> that means and will not say so.

**3. The bell is tolled.** The church bell announces a death to the district — in some
communities one stroke per year of the deceased's life, so the neighbours know who
before anyone tells them.

**4. The body is washed, dressed and laid out** — by women of the family and
neighbourhood, at home. Coins on the eyes, the jaw bound up with a cloth until it
sets, the body laid on a board or a door across two chairs before the coffin comes.
In warm weather, ice or a cooling board. In Louisiana in August, speed.

**5. Sitting up with the dead.** A continuous vigil, one to three days, in shifts. The
body is **never left alone**. Neighbours bring food. There is talk, and coffee, and
sometimes cards, and the occasion is social as well as solemn. The stated reasons are
practical (animals, and the real historical fear of premature burial) and spiritual at
once.

> The best haunting device in this file. A vigil is a room, a night, two or three
> people who must stay awake, a body, and an absolute prohibition on leaving. It is a
> locked-room story that the period supplies for free, and every rule of it is one the
> characters will follow without discussing.

**6. The funeral**, usually within a day or two, from the house or the church.

**7. The burial**, and then — a documented Louisiana custom worth knowing — **the
post-burial return to the house**, where the household is put back in order and the
family is fed. The Louisiana Folklife Program has an essay specifically on this.

## Burial in wet ground

This landscape does not accept the dead easily, and that fact is the book's subject in
miniature.

- **Water table.** In flood-prone ground a coffin buried in the ordinary way will be
  lifted by rising groundwater and pushed up out of the earth, and will break open.
  This is not folklore; it is why the region's burial practice looks the way it does.
- **Above-ground tombs** — brick or plastered vaults, the Spanish custom, standard in
  New Orleans and in many towns.
- **Copings** — a concrete or brick rectangle built around a grave and filled, raising
  the burial a foot or more above grade. The common rural compromise.
- **Higher ground.** In the flood parishes, families who could afford it buried across
  the river or on the nearest ridge. Families who could not buried beside the church
  and hoped.
- The poor are buried in the ground, in a plain box, in ground that floods.
  **Who is buried where is a statement of class that everyone in the story can read
  at a glance and nobody will explain.**

## Mourning dress and household mourning

The formal English rules were followed in the American South by those with the money
to follow them, and approximated by everyone else.

**Periods** (for a woman, the strictest case): husband, 2 years; parent or child,
1 year; sibling, 6 months; grandparent, 9 months; uncle/aunt/niece/nephew, 3 months;
cousin, 6 weeks.

**First mourning** ("widow's weeds"): black throughout — dress and cloak covered in
**crape**, a stiff, matte, crimped silk that deliberately absorbs light and reflects
nothing; a crape bonnet with a long veil; plain white muslin collar and cuffs. No
shine. No jewellery except jet or hair-work.

**Half-mourning**, later: grey, lavender, mauve, black-and-white.

**The house mourns too.** Windows and mirrors draped in crape. A crape badge or wreath
on the front door announcing the death to the street.

**Details worth stealing:**
- Crape is chosen *because it does not reflect*. In a book about reflections, a woman
  dressed head to foot in a fabric engineered to be unreflective is an image that
  needs no commentary.
- Crape is ruined by rain and by damp, and this is a wet climate. Mourning is
  expensive and it is expensive *repeatedly*.
- Mourning is publicly legible. A stranger's clothing tells you who died and how long
  ago. A character still in first mourning after three years is telling the district
  something.
- Hair jewellery — a lock of the dead person's hair woven into a brooch or ring — is
  ordinary, not gothic, and is worn daily.

## Photographing the dead

See `photography/spirit-and-postmortem.md`. Briefly: photographing a dead child or
relative was common, unembarrassed, and often the only photograph the family had of
that person.

## The undertaker arrives late

Embalming spreads out of the Civil War, and formaldehyde-based practice from the
1890s; the undertaker professionalises through the 1880s–1900s. But the trade reaches
the rural South last. **For most of this period, in a parish, there is no funeral home
— there is a carpenter who makes the box and a neighbour woman who lays out the body.**
A story set in 1910 might have an undertaker; a story set in 1875 almost certainly
does not.

## Notes for use

- The period does not need to be told that death is close. It is close. **Nobody in
  these stories is shocked by a corpse.** They are shocked by a corpse behaving
  incorrectly.
- Every custom above is a **rule that is obeyed without justification**, which makes
  the whole complex an ideal environment for `craft-principles.md` §3: a haunting with
  rules the reader can half-infer, sitting inside a culture already full of rules
  nobody states.
- The obvious trap is the mirror. It is so good that it will be tempting to use it in
  three stories. **Use it once.** Log it in the ledger in `bible/book-bible.md` when
  you do.
