# Spirit Photography and Photographing the Dead

Two separate practices that modern readers run together. One was a fraud with a trial
attached; the other was ordinary family life. The book's premise — that the stories
are what the plates are *of* — sits directly on top of both.

---

## Part 1: Post-mortem photography — the ordinary one

**This was not morbid and it was not a subculture.** It was common, unembarrassed
practice through the second half of the nineteenth century, and the reason is
economic: a photograph cost money and was an occasion. Many families never got round
to it. Then a child died, and there was one last chance to have an image of them, and
they took it.

**For a great many nineteenth-century people, the only photograph that existed of them
was taken after they were dead.** Sit with that before writing any of it.

### What it looked like in practice

- **Children and infants most of all.** Frequently posed in the mother's arms, or in a
  crib, or on a couch, as though asleep.
- Adults laid out in the parlour, in the coffin or on the cooling board, sometimes
  with the family gathered.
- The photographer came **to the house**. This is a domestic scene, not a studio one —
  and with a wet-plate photographer it means a stranger setting up a darkroom tent in
  the yard of a house in mourning (`wet-plate.md`).
- Eyes: usually closed. The often-repeated claims about eyes painted onto the plate,
  or bodies propped upright in stands to look alive, are **largely modern legend** —
  the posing stand was for the living, who had to hold still, not for the dead, who
  had no difficulty. Treat the propped-corpse story as folklore about the Victorians
  rather than as a Victorian fact. **[flagged: contested; do not build a story on it]**
- The image is then kept, framed, carried, put in the album with everyone else.

### The technical irony worth knowing

Long exposures punish movement. **The dead do not move.** A post-mortem plate of a
child in its mother's arms is frequently the sharpest thing in the frame, with the
living mother very slightly blurred around it.

That is a true fact about the physics, it needs no supernatural gloss, and it is
almost unbearable. It is also, precisely, `style-guide.md`'s instruction to anchor the
impossible in the physical — except that here nothing impossible has happened at all.

### For this book

`bible/book-bible.md` names **The Faces Beneath** as a section and lists Plate IV
(above-ground burials) and Plates I and V under it. Post-mortem practice is the
natural material. The restraint required is total: the period treated this as normal,
so **the prose must treat it as normal too.** A narrator who finds it ghoulish is a
modern narrator. A narrator who says the child was photographed on the Tuesday, and
the plate cost two dollars, and it is the only one there is, has done the whole job.

## Part 2: Spirit photography — the fraud

### William H. Mumler

A Boston jewellery engraver. In **1861** he made a self-portrait and found a faint
second figure in it, which he identified as a dead cousin. He went into business.

His clients sat for a portrait and received a plate with a translucent figure standing
behind or beside them — a dead husband, a dead child, a dead soldier. He was doing
**double exposure**, and probably reusing plates and stock negatives. Some of his
"spirits" were later identified as living Boston residents.

**In 1869 he was tried for fraud in New York.** It was a national story. He was
**acquitted** — the prosecution could not demonstrate the method to the court's
satisfaction — and the acquittal did not settle anything for anybody.

### Why it worked

Not credulity. Arithmetic. **620,000 dead in the Civil War**, most of them buried
where they fell, far from home, many unidentified, with no body returned and no grave
to visit. A whole generation of families had a death with no object attached to it.
Mumler sold the object.

The most famous surviving Mumler is a portrait of **Mary Todd Lincoln** with a
shadowy Abraham Lincoln standing behind her, hands on her shoulders. She sat under a
false name.

Spirit photography continued after Mumler — William Hope in England into the 1920s,
with Arthur Conan Doyle among the believers who refused to accept the debunkings.

### What is genuinely useful in it

1. **The mechanism is banal and the belief is not.** Everybody's theory of how the
   photograph got made was wrong, including the sceptics' — the prosecution in 1869
   could not prove the trick. That gap between an obvious fraud and an unprovable one
   is exactly the ambiguity `james-rules.md` §3 asks for.
2. **The sitters were not fools.** They were grieving people paying money for the only
   thing on offer. Writing them as dupes is both bad history and bad character work.
3. **A photograph is evidence in a way a story is not.** A ghost that appears in a
   room can be disbelieved. A ghost on a plate is an object on a table that several
   people are looking at, and it will still be there tomorrow. `craft-principles.md`
   §1 — the mundane buying the impossible — has no better instrument than this.
4. **It gives a period-accurate reason for a character to want a photograph made**,
   which is otherwise hard to motivate.

### The precursor: Hippolyte Bayard, 1840

Twenty years before Mumler, and not a ghost at all. Bayard had developed a
direct-positive photographic process and was persuaded to delay announcing it; when
Daguerre took the credit and the French government's pension, Bayard produced
**"Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man"** — a photograph of himself posed as a corpse,
with a note on the back stating that the body was his, that he had drowned himself in
despair, and that it had been in the morgue several days.

It is arguably the first staged photograph and the first faked photographic document,
and it was made in **protest**, by the one man who best understood what the medium
could be made to say. Worth keeping in mind whenever a story in this book asks the
reader to treat a plate as evidence.

### The trap

**Do not write a spirit photographer as the villain.** It is the first idea, it
resolves into a con-man plot, and a con-man plot has a solution — which
`craft-principles.md` §3 forbids. The interesting version is the honest photographer
who finds something on a plate he did not put there, cannot explain, does not
publicise, and quietly does not destroy.

And note the harder inversion, which is probably the best story available here:
**a man who knows exactly how the trick is done, and finds one he cannot account for.**
