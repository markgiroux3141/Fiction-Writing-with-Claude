# Wet Plate Collodion — How the Plates Were Actually Made

The five photographs are wet plates, and the bible says the stories are what the
photographs are *of*. So the process is not background: it is a set of physical
constraints that determine **who could be photographed, where, for how long, and
what could go wrong.** Every constraint below is a plot device.

---

## The chronology

| Period | Dominant process |
|---|---|
| 1839 – mid-1850s | Daguerreotype — silvered copper, one-off, mirror-like, no negative |
| **1851 – c. 1880** | **Wet collodion.** Archer publishes 1851; by the end of the 1850s it has almost entirely displaced the daguerreotype |
| 1854 onward | **Ambrotype** — a collodion negative on glass, backed with black, read as a positive |
| 1856 onward | **Tintype (ferrotype)** — the same chemistry on lacquered iron. Cheap, tough, postable. The itinerant's format |
| 1880s onward | **Gelatin dry plates** replace collodion — more sensitive, no darkroom in the field |
| 1888 onward | Kodak. Photography leaves the professional's hands |

**The wet plate era is roughly thirty years, and during it the vast majority of
photographs on earth were made this way.** For our period centre of c. 1850–1920, that
means: a plate photograph is *current technology* before about 1880 and *an old
photograph* after it. A wet plate made in 1905 is somebody being deliberately
old-fashioned, or poor, or both — which is itself a character note.

## The process, in the order it happens

1. **Coat.** A glass plate is flowed with **collodion** — nitrocellulose dissolved in
   ether and alcohol — with potassium iodide in it. The plate is tilted until the
   coating is even and the excess poured off a corner. It sets to a tacky skin.
2. **Sensitise.** In the dark, the plate goes into a bath of **silver nitrate** for
   several minutes. Now it is light-sensitive, and now the clock is running.
3. **Load** into a light-tight plate holder, still wet.
4. **Expose** in the camera. Seconds to minutes depending on light.
5. **Develop** immediately — pyrogallic acid early on, ferrous sulphate later.
6. **Fix** — sodium thiosulphate ("hypo"), or **potassium cyanide**, which is faster,
   commonly used, and lethal.
7. **Wash, dry, varnish.**

**The whole sequence must be finished before the plate dries — ten to fifteen
minutes.** That single fact generates everything else.

## What the constraint means in a story

**The darkroom must be within a few minutes' walk of the camera.** In the field that
means a **portable darkroom**: a tent, a wagon, a converted cart, a box with sleeves.
A photographer working a bayou or a cemetery has physically brought a small dark room
with him and set it up nearby.

> A man arrives at a house with a wagon, sets up a tent, and disappears into it
> repeatedly for a quarter of an hour at a time, and comes out with images. That is
> what people actually saw. It is worth remembering how that looked to them.

**He is handling poisons.** Ether (volatile, flammable, an anaesthetic in its own
right), silver nitrate (stains skin black — a wet-plate photographer's hands are
marked, permanently and visibly, which is a superb physical detail), and potentially
**potassium cyanide**. Photographers were sometimes ill. The tent smells of ether.

**Exposures are long.** Seconds at best, tens of seconds in poor light. Which means:

- **Nobody smiles**, and the stiffness in period portraits is a mechanical fact.
- **Sitters are braced.** A **posing stand** — an iron neck-and-back clamp on a stand
  behind the sitter — holds the head still. It is out of frame. It is in the room.
- **Anything that moves is not recorded, or is recorded as a blur.** Leaves. Water.
  Someone who walked through the frame and out again leaves a faint smear or nothing
  at all.
- **Something that stood still for the whole exposure is recorded sharply**, whether
  or not anyone saw it standing there.

That last pair is the entire technical basis of nineteenth-century spirit photography
and of every genuine "figure in the background" a Victorian family ever puzzled over.
See `spirit-and-postmortem.md`. **It is also, for our purposes, a rule the reader can
half-infer** in exactly the way `craft-principles.md` §3 wants: *the plate keeps what
was still.*

**The plate is unique.** A wet-plate negative is one object. There is no other copy
unless prints were made and kept. Break it and the image is gone. This makes a plate a
**thing that can be stolen, hidden, sold with the household effects, or dropped** —
see the bible's premise.

## What a plate looks and behaves like

- Glass, heavy, roughly the size of the final image — contact-printed, so an
  8×10 print needs an 8×10 plate and an 8×10 camera.
- The image sits in a thin skin of collodion on one surface, and that skin can
  **lift, flake, craze, and peel at the edges.**
- Characteristic flaws, all period-authentic and all useful: pour lines and comet-tails
  from the coating; a dark thumbprint at the corner where it was held; uneven edges;
  **fogging**; oyster-shell flaking; streaks where the developer ran.
- Collodion is **orthochromatic** — sensitive to blue and ultraviolet, insensitive to
  red. Consequences you can see: skies blow out to blank white; blue eyes go pale and
  strange; freckles, sunburn and red lips go dark; a red dress photographs black.
- Varnished plates yellow with age.

**Do not describe a plate flaw as ominous.** Describe it accurately and let the reader
do it. `style-guide.md` → "Anchor the supernatural in the physical."

## Who takes the photograph

**Studio photographers** in towns: a skylit gallery, painted backdrops, props, headrest,
a mounted card with the studio's name and address printed on it.

**Itinerants** working the country: a wagon, a tent, tintypes at a low price, from
house to house and at fairs, camp meetings and markets. **An itinerant photographer is
a stranger who arrives, makes images of your family, and leaves** — and who keeps a
day-book of where he was and whom he photographed. That day-book is one of the best
period documents available to this book
(`influences/the-19th-century-feel.md` §6).

## Formats to name correctly

- **Carte de visite** (CDV) — a small albumen print on a card mount, c. 2½ × 4 in.,
  collected in albums. The 1860s craze.
- **Cabinet card** — the larger successor, c. 4¼ × 6½ in., from the 1870s.
- **Tintype** — on iron, often in a paper sleeve or a small case.
- **Ambrotype** — on glass, in a hinged case with a velvet lining and a brass mat.
- **Stereograph** — a double image viewed through a stereoscope, for depth. A parlour
  entertainment.

## A caution for the drafting

The temptation, in a book illustrated with wet plates, is to write a story *about
photography* in which a character explains the process. Do not. **A period character
who has his portrait made does not know how it works and is not interested.** The
process belongs to the writer, not to the dialogue.
