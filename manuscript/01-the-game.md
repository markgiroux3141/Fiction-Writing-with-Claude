On the fourth day of the heat Alma shelled peas on the gallery with the pan between her
knees.

The shade came round to that side of the house at about ten and held until two, and that
was the only reason to sit there. Hollis had gone down to the cane before light. He had
thirty acres of it and one hand, a man called Cobb, who came at grinding, so he would not
be up before dinner and there was no sense keeping anything hot for him. She had put his
plate in the press with a cloth over it and let the fire go out.

The children had got into the linen. She had heard the door of the press go and had let
it go, because seeing to it meant standing, and she had decided at breakfast to stand as
few times as the day would allow.

From the gallery she could see the whole yard and the fig tree in the middle of it. They
were going round the tree. Two under sheets, three not. The ones under sheets went slow
with their arms out, because that was the rule.

Mattie was twelve and too old for it and was playing anyway, which she managed by playing
badly on purpose, so that nobody could say she had tried. Nan got caught almost at once
and did not mind and went under a sheet. Wick was arguing about something. The Ledoux girl
was the quick one; she had been in the house since the end of June, on account of her
mother's confinement, and she could run.

The yard was in full sun by then and they kept the game inside the ring of the tree's
shade, all of them but Sam, who would tear out across the open and come back holding the
top of his head and saying it was nothing.

She shelled peas. She got through most of the pan.

After a while a third sheet came round the far side of the tree.

Her hands went on for another pod and then stopped. The thought she had was that there
would not be enough peas, which was not true and had nothing to do with anything, and it
sat in her head and would not go.

It was taller.

It went the same slow way, arms out, keeping to the rule, and the children screamed at it
properly. It followed the Ledoux girl twice round and let her get away, which was correct.
The yard was dry enough that the children raised dust to their knees. It did not.

She thought Hollis had come up from the field to play. Hollis was not a man who played.

It came round the tree a fourth time and stopped out in the open with its arms down at its
sides, in the full sun, and stood there while the game went on without it. Nan ran into
the back of it and bounced off and got up and kept running.

Alma set the pan on the boards and went down the steps.

By the time she was in the yard the game had moved to the far side of the tree and there
were five of them, and the sheets were on Nan and on Mattie, and the trunk of the fig was
not wide enough to hide a grown man behind. She walked round it anyway. She looked up into
it. There was a good deal of light coming through and nothing in it but figs, most of them
still green.

"Who was that," she said. "Just now. The tall one."

"You were it," Wick said to Mattie.

"I was not it."

"You were, you touched the tree."

She asked them again at noon, when she called them in, and got the same nothing. Four came
up the steps and then the fifth, pulling the sheet off her head as she came, and they were
loud in the hall the way children are when they have been shouting outside and have not
finished. Wick said the grace because it was Wick's week and he went too fast, and she
made him do it again. She counted them at the table and got five, and counted again and
got five.

Hollis came up and ate and said the heat would break by Sunday. She told him about the
tall one and he said one of the Ledoux boys, likely, come up to see his sister and got
pulled into it. Alma said the Ledoux boys were both in Franklin. Hollis said then it was
somebody walking through, and it was a hot day, and there was nothing under a sheet but
whoever was under it, and he took his hat and went back down.

The linen went back in the press that evening, all of it, and she did not count it, and
she thought about not counting it while she was closing the door.

She went out on the gallery after supper. The heat came up off the yard the way it does
when the ground has had all day of it, and the fig stood in the middle of the yard, and
the yard was empty.

The sun had not come off it yet. It would not be off it for an hour.

---

The heat broke on the Sunday, as Hollis had said, and it broke hard. It rained through
Sunday night and most of Monday and the yard stood in water to the depth of a thumb, and
the ditch behind the cane ran brown and loud, and the children were kept in and were a
plague about it.

Nan did not want her dinner on the Monday. Alma made her sit with it until it went cold,
because that was what you did with a child who had decided against a thing, and Nan sat
with it, and in the end Alma scraped the plate out for the chickens and thought no more
about it that day.

On Tuesday the water was off the yard and they went out again.

She saw it once that week, from the kitchen window, with a knife in her hand and half an
onion. It was not doing anything. She put the knife down and straightened up and went out
to the step, and the yard was the yard.

She got so that she knew where all five of them were at any hour of the day. She had not
decided to do that.

Nan's throat came on Wednesday. She said it was sore and then she said it was not, and by
evening she would not say either way, and she lay on the settee in the hall where it was
coolest and watched the ceiling and answered when she was spoken to. Alma sat up with her
that night, and Mattie took the small hours, and in the morning there was a smell in the
hall that Alma knew and did not have a name for.

Hollis went for the doctor on Thursday and got back with him at four. He was not drunk.
He looked at Nan's throat with a spoon and the window behind him, and he said it was the
fever that was in the parish, and he left a paper of something and said to keep her
taking water, and he wrote the visit in his book and said he would put it against the
crop. Then he went on to the Thibaults, who had two down.

Emmy Ledoux stayed. Her mother was lying-in with a four-week-old and there was nowhere in
the parish worse to send a child out of a sick house than into that one, and Alma wrote
and said so. She put in that the fever was at the Thibaults' and that the baby wanted
quiet, and she asked the woman what she thought best, and every word of it was true. The
answer came back on the Saturday and agreed with her.

Nan died on the Friday, early, before it was light.

Mrs Rey came up from the landing and laid her out, which she had done for most of the
district and did without any fuss at all. She tied the jaw and washed her and did her
hair and put her in the blue, and when she was done she folded Nan's arms across her
front and went out to the pot to see about the water.

Alma came in at eleven with the coffee for her.

She was in the doorway a while. She knew that afterward from the cup, which had gone cool
in her hand before she moved.

The arms were out to the sides.

She put the cup on the floor, which was not where cups went, and folded them back across
the front and held them down for a second the way you hold something that might go
somewhere. Then she went out to the pot.

"Did you have her arms out?"

Mrs Rey said she had folded them across the way she always did, and asked was there a way
Alma wanted them, because some families did prefer the other.

"No," Alma said.

When she came back in, the arms were out to the sides again.

She left them. Mrs Rey came in later to do the flowers and stood over Nan a second longer
than the flowers needed, and did not say anything about it either way.

After the burying Alma stopped thinking about it, because there was a good deal else.

They were playing again by the Tuesday. There were four of them and no sheets, because
the press was shut, and they went round the tree in the ordinary way, shrieking, and the
Ledoux girl was still the quick one.

Alma stood at the window with the pan and watched them and did not call them in.

---

Nan was buried on the Saturday.

Father Hébert came out from the Chêne and was late, having had the Thibault boy at eleven.
He said what he says. The plot and the fee went in a ledger. He had not seen Hollis at
Mass since the spring and said so to him and not to her, the way you would mention a
fence.

On the Sunday afternoon Alma saw it from the kitchen. It had a sheet, though the press had
been shut a week, and it was going round the fig with the four of them at the slow pace,
arms out, keeping to the rule.

She went out through the door and down the steps and across the yard at it.

She did not know what she had said. She knew she had said it at the top of her voice,
because her throat was raw with it that night and still raw in the morning.

There was nothing at the tree. There was the tree. There was Wick a few feet off with a
sheet over his arm, looking up at her, and Emmy Ledoux behind him with her hand over her
mouth, and Sam out by the ditch, not coming any closer.

She had all four of them in the house by four o'clock and she put the bar across.

She kept them in on the Monday and on the Tuesday. A house in this parish in August with
the doors shut and the shutters to is nearer ninety-five inside than ninety, and by
Tuesday evening the four of them were lying on the boards of the hall where the draft
would have been if there had been one. Wick was sick on the floor from the heat. She
cleaned it up and kept them in. Somebody had to go out to the pot and the henhouse twice a
day and she sent Emmy, because Emmy was the quick one.

Hollis said it that night, in the dark. Shut up hot like that was how children took the
fever. Everybody knew it. Air was the whole of it — you kept a sickroom open, you kept the
shutters back, you did not hold four children in a closed hall in August, and if she
wanted to know what had got Nan then it was the ditch and the cistern and the closeness of
that back room, and she could ask any man in the parish.

Mrs Rey said the same on the Wednesday, at the pot, without being asked. She said also
that the Bertrand place had sold its gin and got less for it than they had given, and Alma
said she had not heard. The doctor had told the Thibaults to get their two out into the
air and had said it in front of six people. Father Hébert had said it at the grave, in
among the other things he said, and had not stopped to see whether she took it.

What Alma had against all that was that there was a tall thing in the yard that played
with her children, and she had spent that on the Sunday, at the top of her voice, in front
of them. Hollis had taken the lamp and the gun round the yard and the cane edge that
night and had been the better part of an hour about it, and had come in and been kind to
her. He was kind to her all week. She would have taken almost anything else.

So she argued the fever, which she did not believe, against four people who did, and she
lost it.

On the Thursday morning she took the bar down and opened the house and let them out.

She wrote to the Ledoux woman again the same day. The answer came back on the Tuesday and
was the same answer, and she read it twice and put it away. Emmy's boots had gone into the
press in July with the good linen, and the press had been shut since Nan was buried.

It was at the tree on the Saturday, and Hollis was at the house, because the mule had
thrown a shoe and he had brought her up to be seen to. He had got her off a man at the
landing in the spring and she had been trouble about her feet ever since.

Alma was at the top of the steps with a pan of water. She got most of the way across the
yard with the pan still in her hands and she knew what she said this time, because she
said the same thing over: get away from them. Get away from them.

There was nothing at the tree.

When she turned round, Hollis was at the bottom of the steps and he was not looking at the
tree. He had not looked at the tree once. He was looking at her, and he had Sam by the
shoulder, and he had put him behind him.

He came and took the pan off her and said let's go in.

He said nothing about it that day. On the Sunday he said that Ada had written and had
room, and that the boys might go up to her a few weeks until the fever was out of the
parish, and that it would be easier on Alma as well, and he said all of it in the voice he
used for the weather.

He said the boys. Neither of them said Emmy Ledoux.

Alma said no. He let it go, and she knew he would raise it again, because it was a
sensible thing and he was a sensible man.

She was awake most of that night with it. Either there was a thing in the yard or there
was not.

She got up before light and did the fires.

She moved the wash to the bench at the north end of the gallery after that. It is a worse
place for it; the light comes in under the roof there and gets in your eyes.

She saw it on the Sunday.

What she noticed first was her hands. She had a piece of Wick's shirt in them and she had
wrung it past wringing, so that the twist of it had gone dry, and her fingers had set and
had to be got open one at a time. The water in the tub had stopped moving. She did not
know how long she had been standing there.

She put the shirt in the basket without rinsing it, and took out another thing, and
started on that.

She did not go down. If she went down there would be nothing at the tree, and Hollis would
be up at dinner, and Ada had room.

It went after the Ledoux girl mostly, and the thought she had was that Emmy Ledoux was not
hers, and she finished pinning up the sheet she was holding before she moved.

Emmy asked most days when she was going home. Alma said when your mother is up.

She did not write to the Ledoux woman again.

She asked Hollis once more to cut the fig down. He said he would get to it. The tree was
bearing, and he was sixteen hours in the cane with a great deal of it left, and he would
see to it when the crop was in. Alma said that would be November. He said it would.

Neither of them said anything more about the tree, then or after.

Mattie went off her food on the Monday.

Alma did not make her sit with it.

It went quicker with Mattie than it had with Nan — six days, and four of them bad — and
there was no sending for the doctor, because he was at the Thibaults' until the Wednesday
and after that he was at his own house, where he had two of his own down and one of them
already gone. Hollis came up out of the cane twice a day. Emmy took the two nights in the
middle that Alma could not take. She was nine and she was good at it. Sam carried water and
did not get sick and never did, all that summer, and nobody ever said why.

Mattie died on the Sunday, in the afternoon, with the whole house awake for once.

Mrs Rey came up and laid her out in the grey. Nobody touched the arms.

One of the Ledoux boys came up from Franklin on the Tuesday to see how his sister did.
Alma met him out in the yard. She said the child was asleep and there was fever in the
house, and both of those were true, and he stood there a minute with his hat and then went
back down the road.

There were three of them left after that, and the game does not work with three. They
argued about it in the yard most of a morning.

By the afternoon they had it working again.

Alma was at the bench with her hands in the water when Wick came up the steps for the
dipper, and he told Sam, going back down, that it was better this way. The tall one never
got tired.

---

Wick told her himself, on the Thursday. He came up for the dipper and drank most of it and
said he had been it nearly the whole morning and had not minded, and then he said his
throat hurt.

She heard herself start to ask him who had been it before him. She stopped in the middle
of the question and sent him in to lie down in the hall where it was cooler.

It took Wick nine days, which was longer than Mattie and longer than Nan, and there was a
night in the middle of them when he was better and sat up and wanted figs, and because he
had asked for a thing, having asked for nothing in days, she took the pan out and went
down and picked them in the dark with her back to the house, not hurrying, because
hurrying would have meant she had a reason to. Nothing happened. She brought them in and
he ate two and gave the rest to Emmy, and in the morning he was worse than he had been.

He died on the Sunday, early.

Mrs Rey came up for the third time that summer and she did not fold his arms across. She
had them straight out from the start and worked round them, and when she was finished she
stood back from the bed and looked at him for a while.

"I meant to ask. Is that a way in your people? Laying them out so."

Alma said her mother's people were from Alabama.

"I wondered, was all." Mrs Rey picked up the basin and then set it down again. "I've done
a few and I never saw it."

She had learned the work from a woman called Delphine on the Bertrand place, who did that
place and two others besides, and who would not let a girl put a hand on a body before she
was fourteen. Mrs Rey was eleven when she started watching from the door, and she watched
for three years. Delphine was particular about the hair over everything else. Most people
got the hair wrong, she said, and thought nobody could tell.

Alma asked how many she had done.

Mrs Rey said she did not keep a count. Then she said better than a hundred. Then she said
it was more than that, on account of the year of the water, when she did eleven in one
week, and she could still say the eleven in order.

She took her apron off and folded it.

"I'll come if you send for me again," she said. "I'd rather you didn't send."

She was down off the steps and gone before Alma had the door open for her.

Hollis raised Ada again on the Monday and Alma did not argue it. Sam went up on the
Wednesday in the wagon with a box and his hat and did not want to go, and stood up in the
back of it to see the house until the road turned. There was room on the seat beside him.

That left Emmy Ledoux.

Her mother was up by then, but the baby was not thriving and was not christened yet, and
the letter said another few weeks would be a kindness if Alma could manage it. Alma wrote
back the same day that she could manage it. It went down to the landing that evening and
she did not put it to Hollis first. She had not put the burials in any of the letters.
There had not been a natural place for it, and then there were three of them and no place
at all.

Emmy asked once more that week when she was going home. Alma said when your mother is up.

There is no playing tag with one. Alma sent her out in the afternoons for the air, because
that was what you did with a child, and from the bench she could see her going round the
fig at the slow pace with her arms held out from her sides, and stopping, and waiting where
she was, and then going again.

---

Emmy stopped going out in the afternoons on the second of September.

Alma nursed her by herself. Hollis was getting the place ready for the cutting and was
gone before light and back after dark, and Mrs Rey came up twice with broth and did not
come into the room. Alma sat up in the chair beside the bed with the shutters back,
because you kept a sickroom open, and she had the window and the yard in front of her the
whole of every night. Nothing in it but the tree.

On the Friday night the girl was as low as Alma had seen any of them get.

On the Saturday morning she was sitting up.

She wanted the shutters wider and she wanted to know whether Sam was back yet and she
talked about her mother's baby and what they were going to call it, all of it clear and in
order, and she took a little bread in milk and kept it down. Alma had seen this twice
already that summer and once with her own mother. She knew what it was.

She sat in the chair and let herself have it anyway, for about an hour.

The girl slept in the middle of the morning and Alma stayed where she was.

She was out of the chair when the girl came off the bed. She went to the window first.

The yard was in full sun.

Emmy was running. She was barefoot and in her shift and she ran the way she had run in
July, and she went round the fig, and the tall one came round the other side of it going
slow with its arms out, because that was the rule.

It followed her round. It did not hurry. It did not have to.

Alma got to the bottom of the steps. She found out about her hand later in the day — a cut
along the base of the thumb, from the door latch, that wanted a stitch and did not get one.

Emmy went round behind the trunk. The tall one went round after her.

Alma was twelve feet away and neither of them came out the other side.

She walked round the tree. She walked round it again. It is about two feet through at the
base.

Hollis came up out of the field because she did not stop calling.

They went through the cane to the Thibault line that afternoon and dragged the ditch on
the Monday, and men came up from the landing who had never been up to the house before,
and it went on the better part of a week. Alma went out with them the first two days.
There is no stone for Emmy Ledoux.

Alma wrote to her mother on the Tuesday. It took her most of the day. What she put in the
letter was that Emmy had been very ill, and had got out of the house, and that they had not
been able to find her, and that she had written twice in the summer to ask whether the girl
should come home. She put in the dates. Every word of it was true, and she never said the
rest of it to anybody as long as she lived.

They gave over looking on the Saturday. On the Sunday she took a sheet out of the press.

She did it standing at the kitchen table with the cloth scissors, and she did not lay the
sheet out and she did not mark it. She cut the one hole and then the other and they came
out ragged and they came out wrong, too far apart and the one of them higher than the
other, and she did not go back and cut them again. She left the pieces where they fell and
she did not put the scissors away.

Hollis came in while she was at it and stood in the door. He did not ask her what she was
making. He was thirty-one years old, in the door of his own kitchen, waiting to be told,
and she did not tell him.

She went out at two in the afternoon, which was the hour they had used. The yard was in
full sun. She went down the steps and across in the sheet and started round the fig the
slow way with her arms held out from her sides.

Hollis came off the gallery after her before she had got round it once.

He walked along beside her and said her name. He said it four or five times and then he
said come in now, Alma, and then he got hold of her arm above the elbow and pulled.

She did not stop and she did not take the arm back. She came along with the arm and kept

He got in front of her and put both hands flat against her and she came into them and went
on coming, and he gave ground backward the whole way round the north side of the tree. He
was shouting at her by then. He used her name and her mother's name and he used the names
of the three that were buried, all three of them, out loud in the middle of the afternoon
where the road could hear him, and she came on at the same pace and did not answer any of
it.

Then he got hold of the sheet.

She brought both hands up and held it down over herself, and that was the only thing she
fought him for, and she fought him for it hard.

He let go and stood out of the way and let her past.

He was blown by then and had to get his hands on his knees. She had not changed her pace
once.

There was a smell in the yard that he knew and did not have a name for.

Through the holes she had cut wrong he had got a look at one of her eyes, and it was open,
and it was not on him.

He looked up into it.

The sun was over the top of the tree by two and came down through it, and there was a good
deal of light coming through.

Emmy Ledoux was up where the trunk goes into three, about level with the gallery roof. The
figs were all round her and most of them were still green. She was not holding on.

He got off his knees and went as far as the trunk and put his hand on it and left it
there.

In August he had gone round that yard the better part of an hour with a lamp and the gun.
He did not go round it now.

He went back up the steps and sat down on the gallery, because that was as far as he got,
and he did not go out to her again.

Alma went round, and she stopped, and she stood where she had stopped, and then she went
round again.

The sun came off the yard a little after five. She did not come in with it. The grass was
going down in a ring by then where she had been walking.

He sat there until it was too dark to make her out.

She never hurried. She never put her arms down. She waited to be got, because that was the
rule.
