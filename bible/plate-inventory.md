# Plate Inventory

The photographs, and what each one is a story of. **Rewritten 2026-09-09**: every file
in `wet plate photographs/` was looked at, renamed from its generator filename to a
descriptive slug, and given a story idea. **Forty-three photographs, plus the cover** — the
four in § VII plus `the-scratched-face` were added by Mark on 2026-09-11 and are the only
pictures in the set that were made for a story instead of found.

`the tall one.png` keeps the name it already had — it is story 01's plate and is in the
render pipeline under that name.

**Read `story-seed-pool.md` alongside this.** That file holds 82 premises generated from
the *period* rather than from the pictures, organised by axis, with an eighteen-item modal
band burned at the head. Where an entry below leans on one of those axes it says so
(`pool C1`, `pool M5`). The two files are meant to cross-pollinate: a plate suggests an
image, the pool suggests the machinery, and neither is a story until Mark picks.

⚠ **How to read an entry (D-007, 2026-09-14).** Every `What's frightening` line below is
meant to be **one clause of essence** — the thing the picture does to you — and several of
them overreach into physical description of the subject. Take the clause and discard the
inventory. These images were generated; their detail is noise rather than authorship, so
there is no correct reading of a surface to be had, and any premise resting on root fibre,
grain, damage, exposure or anatomy rests on nothing. Test every reading by asking **would it
survive if the same subject were a pencil sketch?** The entries were written before this was
understood and are being left in place rather than rewritten, because the descriptive
sentences are still a usable way to find a picture in the folder. They are not evidence.

**The story ideas here are premises, not events**, and none of them has been scored
against `invention-protocol.md`'s four tests. A chosen premise still needs its own
twenty-candidate event pool in the story folder, the way `stories/02-georgie/` got one. No
ending shapes are assigned (L-015). No plate is assigned to a story except story 01's.
Flags: `[near: …]` names a collision with a spent event (`invention-protocol.md` Part 3) or
a modal event (`ai-tells.md` §F); `[flag: …]` names something doctrine bars or a problem
with the picture itself.

# I. The five originals

The first four are the plates the book bible was written around. Their entries are kept
close to what they said before, with a story idea added.

## The Sheet on the Stairs
`the-sheet-on-the-stairs.png` — *Faces Beneath* — was Plate I

A figure under a white sheet on the landing of a ruined staircase. Two cut eyeholes, black
and perfectly round. Peeling plaster, cobwebbed banister. The costume is a child's idea of
a ghost, worn by something the size of an adult, and the drape falls as though over a real
body.

**What's frightening:** the deliberateness. Somebody *put this on*, which means there was
a reason to be disguised.

**Story.** A mourning household's photographic hoax. Spirit pictures are a known trade and
everybody knows some operators cheat — so a widow arranges her own: she cuts the eyeholes
herself, out of a sheet she has, and stands her surviving son on the landing for the
exposure, because there was never a photograph taken of the other one and she can no longer
call his face up. The sitting goes off. The impossible thing is not on the plate: it is
that the sheet is now in use in the house — brought out, worn thin, damp when it should be
dry, folded by somebody who folds it wrong. What she pays is laundry: one sheet, washed
oftener than any other thing she owns, for as long as she keeps the house.
[near: story 01 owns sheets-with-eyeholes and arms-out; this one must stay indoors, in the
trade, and must never put the arms out]

## The Wrong Reflection
`the-wrong-reflection.png` — *Reflections* — the title plate

A veiled woman in a pale dress at the edge of a flooded wood. Cypress, saw grass, flat
brown water. Her face behind the veil is a blank. Below her the reflection hangs lower and
longer than she does, and the water shows a shape the bank does not.

**What's frightening:** the reflection is the true body and the figure on the bank is what
it is projecting.

**Story.** Mourning has an arithmetic — husband two years, parent one, sibling six months
(pool L5) — and a woman in a parish with bad luck in it can be in black continuously for a
decade without anything unusual having happened. This one is, and crape is chosen because
it reflects nothing, and it is destroyed by damp, and she cannot afford it and buys it
anyway. She does the wash at the ditch behind the house because it is nearer than the ford.
The impossible thing is that the still water carries one more figure on the bank than the
bank does. She wants it to be who she thinks it is. It is patient about not being.
[flag: `death-and-mourning.md` says spend the covered-mirror custom **once in the book**;
this story would be the natural place and would then close pool L4]

## The Attitude of Grief
`the-attitude-of-grief.png` — *Faces Beneath* — was Plate IV

**Mark liked this one, 2026-09-09** → `bible/plate-explorations/attitude-of-grief.md`:
22 themes, 12 period seams, 14 protagonists, 30 impossible conditions, 12 wounds and 10
off-page rules, plus what the plate collides with. Note there is already an unscored
vignette on it — `craft/vignettes/batch-02.md` #4 — whose event is logged spent and whose
off-page rule fails D-004. The story idea below is one of the thirty, not the answer.

A winged figure among overgrown graves — Celtic cross, ivy, a flower-carved headstone,
wings fully spread. The face is not carved: it is slack, exhausted, human, hair in damp
ropes. Bare feet on the leaf litter, and the stone drapery has the give of wet cloth.

**What's frightening:** it is not a statue that came alive. It is a woman who has been
standing there long enough to be mistaken for one.

**Story.** The monument trade, on installments. A family buys a coping and pays it off at
the stone-cutter's in a parish where who is buried where is a public statement of class.
The bill that comes has a **figure** on it — cut, delivered, set — which nobody in the
family ordered, itemised as carefully as everything else, and the figure is on the plot.
Paying it is cheaper than the argument, and the argument would be held in front of people.
The physical, late and once and vegetal: ivy through the hem of the drapery, and the hem
gives. What the family pays is the balance, monthly, for eleven years.
[pool M5, D2]

## The Sitter
`the-sitter.png` — *Faces Beneath* — was Plate V

Studio portrait, formal dress — frock coat, cravat, pinned collar. The head is wrong:
hairless mottled scalp, enormous black lensless eyes, and a beard that is not hair but fine
root-fibre, still growing, hanging in threads past the collar. Hands not visible. The pose
is correct and patient.

**What's frightening:** the composure. It sat for the full exposure. It wanted a portrait.

**Story.** A photographer's day-book — date, sitter, format, fee, entered in the
assistant's hand. There is a sitting nobody remembers, correct in every column, and the
assistant remembers the man and describes him and describes the beard. This is the 1880s,
so the operator's twenty years of wet-collodion skill is about to be worth nothing:
gelatin dry plates are arriving, exposures are collapsing, and the best plate of his life
is one he cannot show to anybody. He takes the fee. He goes on taking the work.
**The strongest candidate in the whole set for first person** — the book owes one — and
for a story told as a document (pool O6, H4, H2).

# II. Roots

## The Climb
`the-climb.png`

A root-mass in the rough shape of a long-limbed animal ascends a ruined staircase. Roots
have come up through the boards, sheathed the newel post, and gathered themselves into
something with a spine and forelimbs. Caught mid-motion, going up.

**What's frightening:** direction. It is climbing *toward* the bedrooms, and the house is
feeding it.

**Story.** A family takes a house cheap because the last people left it, which in this
economy means the lien came due. The wife's work includes the stairs — scrubbed weekly,
because that is what you do with stairs — and what she is scrubbing off them is not dirt
and is not moss, though moss is what she calls it when asked. The impossible thing is the
direction. What they pay is that nobody sleeps upstairs any more, and the downstairs rooms
were never meant to hold a family, and the arrangement holds for years.
[near: pool I3; and `ai-tells.md` §F's stairs are well-trodden — the differentiator is that
this is *growth*, on a schedule of seasons, not footsteps]

## The Stooping One
`the-stooping-one.png`

Something with a small pale skull-like head and a fibrous beard bends down through an
interior doorway, its body a hanging mass of roots, more root-matter spread across the
floor in front of it. Tall enough that the doorway is the reason it is bent.

**What's frightening:** it had to stoop. The house's dimensions are a fact it has to work
around, which means it is *in* the house on the house's terms.

**Story.** Period-real work: a house on brick piers has its sills rot, and you jack the
house up and put new sills under it, and it costs more than the family has. The carpenter
comes with jacks and screws and finds the work already half done from underneath — timbers
taken, bearing shifted, the house holding itself up in a way he cannot account for and
would not sign off. The wound is that the crop went on this house. What the family pays is
the carpenter's opinion, which he gives them for nothing, and which they decline.

## The Crouching Root
`the-crouching-root.png`

A crouching figure made of root-fibre — skull for a head, root-hair hanging from the jaw,
thin knotted limbs, hands like torn tap-roots. Photographed close, on soft ground.

**What's frightening:** it is a specimen. Somebody set this down and photographed it, which
means somebody handled it.

**Story.** A pullboat crew cutting radial runs into the swamp, the cable dragging a hundred
and fifty logs a day, men in hip boots on a day wage. Something comes out of a root ball
into the yard and it is not a root, and the winch does not stop, because the crew is paid
by the log and the winch is not theirs. It is photographed because a man in the crew knows
somebody who buys curiosities. **The wound is the industry**: fifty years to cut a forest
that stood a thousand, and the ground kept the channels.
[pool C2; `swamp-and-weather.md` — "the whole boom is a haunting already"]

## The Root Beard
`the-root-beard.png`

A standing tree in bare winter woods. Set into the trunk at head height is a small skull-
like knot, and out of it pours a mass of roots — down the trunk, over the ground, spreading
out of frame. Growing *down* out of a living tree.

**What's frightening:** the direction again, inverted. Roots belong under the ground and
these are coming out of the air.

**Story.** A dooryard tree that the family has always used for something ordinary — the
meat hung in it, the rope swing, the shade where the wash goes. It is the most useful thing
on the place. The impossible thing is that it has begun growing out of itself, downward,
and the growth is at the height of a man's face. The wound is that they need the shade,
literally: there is nowhere else to hang the wash and no money to plant and wait.
[near: story 01 spent a fig tree in a dooryard and a child going behind it — this tree must
be a different tree doing different work, and nobody may go behind it]

## The Undercut Tree
`the-undercut-tree.png`

An immense root system exposed at a water's edge, roots spilling down a cut bank toward a
shallow rocky channel. Hills in the far distance.

**What's frightening:** the water took the ground out from under it and it is still
standing.

**Story.** Low water in late summer, when things that were under water are not (pool B1).
The channel drops and the roots are found to have taken hold of something that was put
into the water, and grown around it, and kept it. The man who finds it is not the man who
put it there, and the man who put it there is alive, local, and helpful about the search.
The story never says why he is helpful.
[flag: **there are hills in this photograph** and the render crop cannot remove them. Usable
only for a story set in the Felicianas, the Natchez bluffs, or the north Louisiana
hills — which `stories/02-georgie/event-options.md` already offers as real cellar country]

## The Old Tree
`the-old-tree.png`

A vast ancient tree, its trunk a mass of fused stems and buttresses, roots draped over a
bank; open hill country behind. A folded paper print, creased in quarters.

**What's frightening:** it is older than anything human in the frame, and it has been
photographed as a landmark — somebody thought it needed recording.

**Story.** A **witness tree**: real surveying practice, a tree named in the field notes and
used to fix a section corner, so that every boundary in the neighbourhood is measured from
it. A surveyor runs a line for a sale and the line will not close; he runs it three times
and it fails by the same amount each time (pool M1). The tree is where the notes say it is
and it is not where it was. Every deed in the district is drawn from that corner. He has to
sign something by Friday, and he signs, and the story is what that costs him
professionally for the rest of his life.
[flag: hills again — same constraint as *The Undercut Tree*. The pairing is convenient:
one story could use both]

# III. Reflections

## The Woman in the Water
`the-woman-in-the-water.png`

A woman in a period work dress stands in shallow flat water, arched hard backward, head
thrown right back, mouth open to the sky, wet hair hanging. One hand trails down and her
fingers touch the surface, making rings. A brush island behind her.

**What's frightening:** the posture is not a fall and not a swoon. It is *attentive* —
somebody listening to something directly overhead.

**Story.** Told by the daughter. The mother does the wash at the ditch, which is the
nearest water, and her head goes back like that when she is standing in it, and she does
not know that it does. The daughter has seen it twice. The impossible thing is the posture
and nothing else: no voice, no figure, no explanation. The wound is inheritance — the
chore descends, the water is the same water, and the daughter is fourteen and already
carrying half the load.

## The Bundle in the Water
`the-bundle-in-the-water.png`

A woman in a pale dress and a straw boater stands at the edge of still water, head bowed,
looking down. At her feet, floating in the shallows among the reeds, is a small wrapped
bundle, swaddled, with a shape at one end.

**What's frightening:** she is looking at it, and she has not called anybody.

**Story.** The regional light on the water is said by some to be the soul of an unbaptised
child, and the belief is never named in the prose (`folklore-and-belief.md` §6). A woman
finds a wrapped bundle in the ditch at the foot of her own place. There is nobody in the
parish missing one — no notice, no search, no name (pool D3). The impossible thing is that
it is in the water and it has not been in the water. **The event is that she takes it
out**, which is test 2 of the four tests answered in a single gesture: she is not a witness
after that, she is the one it belongs to. What she pays is a plot, a fee in a ledger, and a
name she has to give it.
[near: story 01's mother's letter already carries four words of unbaptised pressure — the
register is open, not spent, but this story would close it]

## The Empty House
`the-empty-house.png`

A derelict two-storey clapboard house with a columned porch, dormer above, shutters hanging,
curtains still in the upper windows. Bare trees, fog, no people.

**What's frightening:** the curtains. Somebody hung them and nobody has taken them down.

**Story.** A tax assessor's story, which the genre never writes. The house is on the roll
and in nobody's name; the notice goes out and comes back; the assessment says *occupied* and
the assessment is correct. He rides out to correct the roll and cannot, and the roll has to
be signed and filed by a date. The impossible thing sits entirely in the paper and in one
visit. The wound is that a parish is a set of written numbers in somebody else's book, and
he is the man who writes them.
[pool D2, M1, O3 — the intrusion inverted: the impossible thing is on the side of the law]

## The Gentleman
`the-gentleman.png`

A tintype portrait of an elderly man: bald above, side hair, an enormous white moustache,
frock coat, waistcoat, bow tie, hands folded. Nothing whatever is wrong with him.

**What's frightening:** nothing. This is the most useful photograph in the set, because
`craft-principles.md` §1 says the mundane buys the impossible, and this is a page of
credit.

**Story.** He sits at sixty because his wife wants a portrait before he dies, and the
operator tells him to hold still, and holding still is the one thing he has not been able
to do since a night thirty years ago that the story does not explain. The plate is fine.
The story is what he does not tell her, and the whole thing is told by him — first person,
plain, in the voice of a man giving an account of himself and leaving out the middle.
[flag: no origin (L-016) — the night must not be narrated, only worked around; the story
declines to let him explain himself, which is L-021 pointed at a narrator]

## The Old Man
`the-old-man.png`

A gaunt old man, wild grey hair, deep-set eyes, stubble, high collar and stock. Severe and
entirely ordinary.

**What's frightening:** nothing, again — and the pair with *The Gentleman* is worth keeping
in mind. Two ordinary men in a book of monsters is a resource.

**Story.** A deposition. He is a witness in a boundary suit, giving sworn evidence in 1901
about what he saw on the line in 1868, and he is credible, particular and unhurried about
everything except the one detail everybody in the room lets pass. Pays the **first-person**
debt and the **dialogue-at-length** debt at once, and gives every omission a reason to be
on the page: this is a legal document with an audience and a purpose.
[pool O6, M4]

# IV. The Faces Beneath

## The Standing Dead
`the-standing-dead.png`

A desiccated corpse — skin drawn to the bone, ribs showing, rags at the shoulder and hip —
stands upright in a doorway on a tiled floor, head bowed, arms hanging, feet flat.

**What's frightening:** nobody in this period is shocked by a corpse. They are shocked by
one behaving incorrectly, and this one is standing.

**Story.** The parish's laying-out woman: coins on the eyes, the jaw bound with a cloth
until it sets, the body on a door across two chairs, and she has done ninety of them and is
paid in kind. This one will not lie down. The household is in the next room. She is the
only one who can be sent for, and the parish will go on sending for her, which is the whole
price: she cannot stop doing the work and cannot say why she has begun dreading a job she
has done for thirty years.
[pool F5 — and D-003's ration applies: this is the visceral register, not the vegetal one,
so it goes late and once and needs arguing]

## The One in the Bonnet
`the-one-in-the-bonnet.png`

Close on a skull-faced head in a pale hood or veil, tied with a bow under the chin. The jaw
is dropped wide open, teeth showing, with fine strands hanging from the upper jaw.

**What's frightening:** the bow. Somebody tied that, carefully, the way you would tie a
child's bonnet.

**Story.** The jaw is bound with a cloth at a laying-out so that it sets shut before the
viewing, and the binding is somebody's job, and it is done well or badly and the parish can
tell. This one is bound correctly and the mouth is open anyway. She ties it again. It is
open again. The viewing is at two and the family is in the hall, and what she does is the
story — not what it means.

## The Two in the Tomb
`the-two-in-the-tomb.png`

Two clothed skeletons in a brick chamber, in rotted grave cloth, one leaning forward out of
the dark, the other sitting back among rubble and loose bone.

**What's frightening:** the arrangement. They are sitting rather than lying, and there are
two.

**Story.** A tomb is property — bought, inherited, with room for a set number, and reused,
the older remains moved back into the caveau to make space for the next (pool M5). A family
opens theirs for a burial and there are two in a space sold for one, and the extra one is
dressed for the period and is not on any list the family keeps. Somebody paid for that
space, on purpose, and the payment is findable. The wound is that the household has already
done the arithmetic on who goes in next, out loud, and now there is one place fewer.

## The Face in the Hair
`the-face-in-the-hair.png`

A face behind a curtain of long ropey grey hair — old, heavy-featured, eyes rolled up and
white. No body, no ground, no room.

**What's frightening:** it is *hanging*. Everything in the frame falls, which makes the
face something suspended rather than something standing.

**Story.** Moss-picking, the region's actual cash crop for poor families: grey moss pulled
down with a wire hook on a cane pole, hung along the fences for weeks to rot the outer bark
off, then beaten and baled for mattress stuffing. The cured fibre is black, springy, and
looks like horsehair. There is a face in the hanging moss on the fence, and the fence is
sixty yards of the family's income, and the buyer's wagon comes Friday. They bale it.
[pool C1 — `swamp-and-weather.md` calls this the one job that is both economically real and
quietly horrible]

## The Wrapped Child
`the-wrapped-child.png`

A small child stands against a studio backdrop, wound from throat to ankle in strips of
cloth, hair loose and wild, eyes very pale and wide, mouth in something like a smile.

**What's frightening:** the wrapping is not a costume. That is how a body is bound for
burial, and this one is on its feet, in a studio, being photographed.

**Story.** Photographing the dead was ordinary, unembarrassed, and often the only picture a
family ever had of that child. The mother asks the operator for it **standing**, because
she has none of him standing. The operator does it — a stand, a brace, twenty seconds — and
takes the money, and the parish knows she asked. The impossible thing is what the plate
shows him doing during the exposure. What she pays is that she keeps the picture out, on the
press, where anyone who calls can see it, for the rest of her life.
[near: story 01 spent children sickening after contact and a child's laying-out; this is a
*commission*, and the horror is that the mother chose it — L-021 in the register of grief]

## The Painted Man
`the-painted-man.png`

A tintype head-and-shoulders of a man in whiteface with painted brows and mouth, a conical
cloth cap, frizzed pale hair, a knotted kerchief and a worn coat. One eye is clouded and
blind. The teeth are bad and there are too many of them.

**What's frightening:** the age and the teeth. He is old, and he is not funny, and this is
his working portrait.

**Story.** A show works the river towns by steamer — a circus, a medicine show, a
one-tent outfit on the batture for a night. The tent comes down, the boat goes, and one of
the company is still in the district a week later, in a rented room, behaving impeccably,
owing nothing and asking for nothing. The family that took his rent cannot get him out
because he has done nothing wrong, and a man who has done nothing wrong is very difficult
to remove from a room he has paid for.
[flag: the least *Standing Water* photograph in the set — the bible bans the romp and the
carnival register. The way in is to refuse the clown: he is a man who paints his face for
work, and the story is a **tenancy** story]

# V. The house with the staircase — an interior cycle

Five photographs are demonstrably the same house, and two more are near neighbours. The
collection is standalone (D-001), so these are **different houses that happen to be dying
the same way** — a visual rhyme only, never a shared setting. Deployed deliberately, this
is the strongest section-opening sequence available in the set.

## The Thing on the Floor
`the-thing-on-the-floor.png`

An interior: banister and newel post, stair treads littered with root and moss debris. In
the doorway at left, a small motion-smeared figure, child-height, standing. On the boards in
the foreground, a long-legged spider-like thing made of root fibre and hair.

**What's frightening:** the blur. It moved during the exposure and the thing on the floor
did not.

**Story.** A house where the children are told to keep off the stairs, for the ordinary
reason that the treads are going. The mother sweeps the debris up twice a week and calls it
moss, and it is not moss, and she has known that for a while. **There are two impossible
things in this photograph and a story may have one** (§E item 5) — the thing on the floor is
the better one, because the figure in the doorway is a child and story 01 has spent
children.
[pool I3]

## The One on the Banister
`the-one-on-the-banister.png`

A skeleton in rotted lace and rags leans on the stair rail of a good house, forearms over
the banister, skull tipped, one hand hanging loose — the posture of somebody waiting for
somebody to come down, and in no hurry.

**What's frightening:** the ease. It is comfortable.

**Story.** A boarding house, which is a woman's business and a woman's whole income, and
the landlady's standing rule is that nobody waits in the hall. There is a lodger in the hall
who was there before she bought the house, who is patient, and whose rent is paid. She needs
the rent. What she does is what a landlady does: she works around him, and she stops taking
new lodgers with children, and the business shrinks by a third and stays that size.
[near: vignette A1 spent *a watcher at the top of the stairs* — this one is at the bottom
and is at ease, which is the whole differentiator; if that is not enough, cut it]

## The One Showing Them Up
`the-one-showing-them-up.png`

A grand hall with a long staircase. A dark shaggy hooded figure stands at left, one arm
extended toward the stairs, offering the way up. At the head of the stairs, two faint pale
figures. Everything but the banister is smeared.

**What's frightening:** the gesture is courteous. It is *showing somebody up*.

**Story.** In this period a traveller is taken in — the circuit preacher sleeps in whichever
house will have him, and hospitality is not a kindness but an obligation with rules. In one
house somebody is showing guests up who were never admitted, and the household finds out
because a guest thanks them, warmly and specifically, for the room. The wound is that the
family's own hospitality is the door, and they cannot close it without becoming the sort of
house that does not take people in.
[near: story 01 spent *arms held straight out to the sides*. This is **one** arm, extended
in offering, and it must be written as a gesture rather than a posture, or it is a fourth
recurrence of story 01's image (see D-004)]

## The Hooded One at the Stairs
`2/the-hooded-one-at-the-stairs.png`

A tall hooded, cloaked figure at the foot of a staircase, soft-focused, its face an
unresolvable dark hollow. Stairs going up behind it into the light.

**What's frightening:** it is standing where you would stand to be seen.

**Story.** Invert the concealment (pool O1). Nothing is hidden, there is no discovery plot,
and the household absorbed this years ago: they step round it on the way up, the way you
step round a pail. The story arrives after all of that, and its POV is a child who has never
lived in a house without one and is old enough this year to notice that other houses do not
have theirs. Nothing to find out; the horror is entirely the accommodation, already made,
by people who love each other.
[flag: `lessons-learned.md` L-019 — accommodation must be *earned* by a fight the reader
sees. A story that opens inside the arrangement has to put the fight somewhere else: give it
to the child, who has not made the peace her parents made]

## The Thing on the Stairs
`2/the-thing-on-the-stairs.png`

A crouched animal-shaped thing on the stair treads — hairy or mossy across the back, thin

## The One in the Brush
`the-one-in-the-brush.png`

A shrouded skeleton seated on a fallen log in a wood, veiled in rotted cloth over the skull,

## The One in the Marsh

## The Specimen
`the-specimen.png`

**What's frightening:** the legs. It has been photographed as a natural-history specimen and

# VII. The vessel

**Four photographs, added by Mark 2026-09-11**, generated after the story 04 conversation and
renamed from their generator filenames the same day. They are the first pictures in the set
that were made *for* a story rather than found and then interrogated, and the entries below
are written in that knowledge.

All four read as **paper prints**, cracked, foxed and folded, like *The Attitude of Grief*
and *The Ox* — see § *What the book actually prints*. `the-ones-at-the-rail` has lost a
corner of the emulsion and `the-ship-under-way` has lost the whole upper left of the sheet.
Crease lines run through the picture area in three of the four.

**They are plausibly one vessel** — the same heavy three-masted hull, seen at sea, at sea
again, from alongside, and from her own deck. D-001 does not care, because they would all
serve one story, but nothing obliges them to be the same ship and the catalogue does not
assert it.

**They correct `stories/04-ship/notes.md` §3.1**, which said the set contained no vessel and
argued that the story would therefore have to face a portrait. That was true on 2026-09-11 at
nine in the morning and is not true now, and the argument it was carrying — that a portrait
plate keeps the story on a person and off the ship as spectacle — has to be made on its
merits instead of by default.

**And they cut against §2 of the same file.** Three of the four are **open sea**: a running
swell, foam at the stem, no land anywhere. The finding in `notes.md` was that blue water
breaks the book's register and the passes and the bays do not. These photographs are not
inshore. Either the story goes outside the islands and the register argument was wrong, or
the plate and the prose disagree and one of them gives. That is a real decision and it is
Mark's.

## The Ones at the Rail
`the-ones-at-the-rail.png`

A three-masted ship bows-on and under way in a running sea, bowsprit pointed very nearly at
the lens, a wave breaking white along her stem. Yards crossed, canvas furled or hanging.
Along the head rail and down the port side stand men in hats — six or seven of them clearly,
more further aft — not working. Watching.

**What's frightening:** where the photographer is. To make this picture with a wet plate you
need a tripod, a tent, chemistry, and something like ten seconds of stillness, and you need
all of it in open water, ahead of a ship that is moving, at a range of perhaps thirty yards.
The men are looking at whoever is doing that.

**Story.** The impossible thing is in the making of the plate and not in the scene, which is
motif II (*a plate is a reflection that was made to stay*). One route: the plate is in a
dead operator's effects with his day-book, and the day-book accounts for every plate he
poured that season except this one, which is not entered, is not numbered, and is the only
one of them anybody wants to buy. The wound is the sale. [flag: the found-album frame is
`book-bible.md`'s open ⚠ and this entry leans on it; ban-list #3 is nearby, since a reader

## The Ship Under Way

**What's frightening:** the gear. No crew alive lets sail hang like that, and she is

**Story.** The ban list's first entry is the derelict found with the table laid, and this
picture walks straight into it, so the way in is the law rather than the mystery: **a vessel

## Numbering: catalogue vs. printed

Filenames are **names, not numbers**, deliberately: the catalogue outlives any particular
running order, and a photograph reassigned to a different story would otherwise carry its
old story's numeral with it. The numeral a plate carries **in the printed book** is assigned
in reading order and lives in `render/plates.json`. *The Tall One* prints as `PLATE I`
because *The Game* is the first story.

The section headings above (**Roots**, **Reflections**, **The Faces Beneath**) are the
bible's three motifs and are a **filing suggestion, not an assignment**. Several photographs
sit in two; the interior cycle sits across all three.

## What the book actually prints

**Not the plate. The picture.**

Every photograph here is a photograph *of a physical object*: hand-cut edges, collodion pour
ridges, bare corners, varnish crazing, fold quarters on the paper prints, the whole thing
lying on a copy stand. No 19th-century process could put any of that on a page. The negative
is masked to a rectangle in the printing frame and the physical edges fall outside the image
area before the process begins.

`render/scripts/plate_process.py` crops the artifact away and reproduces the picture. Three
processes exist; the book currently uses **`mounted_print`** — a real photographic print,
trimmed and pasted to the leaf by hand, with the halo its chemistry left in the mount.
`photogravure` and `halftone` are the printed alternatives. See `render/README.md`.

**No photo corners.** Gummed corners are a snapshot-album technology of about 1905 onward —
twenty years late for this book — and they assert the found-album frame rather than a
published edition. Any source image carrying them is cropped inside them.

**Three prints in this set are paper, folded in quarters** — *The Attitude of Grief*, *The
Ox*, *The Old Tree* — and one, *The Ox*, has faded past legibility in half the frame. The
crease lines run through the picture area and cannot be cropped out. That is a decision for
Mark: they read as *handled* rather than as *plates*, which is either exactly the book's
subject or a break in its material logic.
