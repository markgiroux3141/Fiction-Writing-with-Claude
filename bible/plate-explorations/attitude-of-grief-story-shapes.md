## The design problem

A story needs escalation. The figure cannot supply it, because anything it *does* is a fact
about it, and enough facts make a mechanism. So the rule for all four shapes below:

**The figure never acts, never changes, and never responds to anything anybody does.** It is a
constant. What escalates is the narrator's **position relative to it** — how near she has to
be, how often, how responsible she is for it, and how much she would have to say out loud to
get out of it. The pressure is human and administrative and it comes from the ordinary world,
which is L-019's requirement anyway: she fights, and it is the ordinary world that beats her.

**The corollary, and it is the one that is easy to break.** If the parish tries to move it and
something goes wrong, the figure has acted, and the enigma is over. Whatever happens must fail
or succeed for entirely human reasons.

All four keep the voice from `attitude-of-grief-prose-01.md` — first person, a woman
explaining her own behaviour to nobody. Wound and rule-off-page get written after one is
chosen, not before.

## A. The corner

**The pressure: the plot fills up toward it.**

Her brother dies. The family ground holds a set number and everybody has always known which
places are left, because everybody in that parish has done that arithmetic about their own
plot and would not admit to it. The last two places are in the corner nearest the unnamed
ground. Nearest the figure.

The whole story is a decision she cannot discuss. To bury him anywhere else, she would have to
give a reason, in front of her sister-in-law, on the day. So he goes in the corner. And then
the story is the year afterwards, in which the four-times-a-year has become something else,
because there is now somebody she loves lying within reach of it, and going out there is no
longer a chore she performs but a visit she owes.

**The end.** There is one place left and she is the next to want it. She goes into town and
buys the unnamed plot — the one the figure stands on, which three families are said to own and
none of them claims — and it costs very little, and the clerk is mildly surprised that anybody
wants that ground, and says so pleasantly. She is now the owner of it. Nobody will ever be
buried in it.

> He was surprised at me and he was decent about it, and he wrote it out, and he said well,
> it is a piece of ground like any other piece of ground. I said that it was.

**What it costs the enigma:** nothing. The figure is not mentioned in the transaction. It is
now hers in the way ground is, and the story never says what she has taken on.

## B. The minute book

**The pressure: the parish decides to put the ground in order.**

A new priest, or a diocesan visitation, or simply the cemetery association doing what such
bodies do every twenty years — a survey of the ground, plots matched against the book, and a
tidying. The unnamed plot has no owner of record. The figure stands on parish ground with no
paperwork behind it. The resolution is that it be moved to the corner by the gate, where the
old broken stones go, and the ground be made available.

**Nobody objects.** That is the story. Fourteen people in a room, every one of whom has spent
their life not putting a rake against it, and the vote is unanimous, because to object you
would have to say why, and there is no way to say why that does not sound like what it is. She
tries three times. Everything she says is true and none of it is the reason: it is good work,
it has been there since before her mother, the ground under it is low and wet and would not
take a burial anyway.

**The end.** It is moved. Four men and a dray and most of a morning, and no difficulty at all.
It now stands in the corner by the gate, which is the gate everybody comes in by.

> They set it where the broken stones go, which is by the gate, and they set it facing the way
> it had faced before, because that is how you set a thing back down. It faces the path. You
> come in past it now.

**What it costs the enigma:** nothing, provided the move goes smoothly — and it must. The
horror is that the parish's twenty years of unspoken avoidance were undone in one meeting by
people being reasonable, and that the arrangement everyone had made is now impossible to keep.

## C. What you teach a child

**The pressure: the girl is not afraid, and the narrator cannot say why she should be.**

Marguerite is nine and likes the cemetery. It is quiet, there are cats in the wall, the names
are good for reading practice, and nobody bothers her. She goes on her own. She is not
frightened of the figure and she says things about it — *how does anybody know*, *it isn't
watching over him, it's watching at him*, *her hair's just in the way* — in the flat way
children say obvious things, and each one is worse than the last, and the child does not know
that and never will.

The escalation is entirely the narrator's. She can teach the child the fear, which means
saying it out loud, which she has not done in thirty-one years and which would make it real
and hand it on. Or she can let the girl keep going out there.

**The end.** She does neither, because that is what people do. She finds the girl a Saturday
job at the store, which is a good thing for a girl to have and which her sister is pleased
about, and which takes the whole of Saturday.

> I have thought since about what I would have said to her, if I had said it, and I cannot get
> further than the first part of it, which is that you must not go out there, and then I am
> stuck, because she would have asked me why, and she is a child who asks.

**What it costs the enigma:** nothing, and this is the shape that guards it best, because
nothing in the story is ever about the figure — it is about a woman who will not say a
sentence.

## D. The keeping

**The pressure: she joins the arrangement, and then cannot leave it.**

From the sexton fragment: the unnamed plot is kept. He has never been asked to keep it, has
never billed anyone for it, does not know who does it, and has been thinking for nine years
about coming out early on the day before All Saints' to find out.

One February she pulls the ivy off the plinth. There is no moment of decision — she is doing
her own coping, the runners have come across, and it is four feet away, and she does it and
goes home. She does it again before Easter. By August it is part of the visit.

Then the fact that undoes her: **if she stops, it will be visible that somebody stopped.** The
ivy takes three summers to cover an inscription and about one to be noticeable. She has made
herself into the thing that is keeping it, and the only way out is a year of the parish
watching that ground go over, and being able to see, on All Saints', in front of everybody,
that whoever had been keeping it had quit.

**The end.** She does not stop. The story closes on the February visit taking two hours now
instead of one, and the reason she gives her family for that, which is her knees, and her knees
are genuinely bad.

> There is nothing to it. It is ten minutes with the shears and I am out there anyway.

**What it costs the enigma:** nothing. It hands the reader one solved thing — *who keeps it* —
which is a human answer, and a solved human question makes the unsolved one louder rather than
quieter. Note the sexton never finds out, and the reader watches her not tell him.

## Two more that are engines rather than stories

**The outsider with the camera.** A photographer on commission spends a morning working close
to it while the parish watches, breaking rules he cannot see, in front of people who will not
explain them. It is the best available vehicle for *how people behave around it*, because he is
the only person who can observe the behaviour and the only one who cannot read it. It costs the
denial-voice, which is the strongest thing we have, so it is likelier a movement inside another
shape than a story by itself.

**The last one.** She is old, there is nobody to come after her, and the story is her arranging
what happens to the ground when she is gone — which means arranging, without ever saying so,
that the figure goes on being avoided correctly by people she will never meet.

## They combine

**A and D** are the strongest pair: the ground fills toward it, and she has taken on the
keeping of it, so by the end she owns the plot, tends the plot, and has her brother lying in
the corner nearest it — and every one of those is a thing she did for a reason she gave out
loud, and every reason was true.

**B is the only one with an event in it**, which makes it the easiest to write and the most
dangerous: an event invites a cause.
