# The Angel — Twelve Scares

`wet plate photographs/the-attitude-of-grief.png`

Mark, 2026-09-09: *"I'm seeing a lot of minutiae about type of screws... but not a lot about
the things that's scary. When I look at an image, I try to get my mind to a place that sends
a shiver down my spine and form an idea based on that. The details can be ironed out later.
I need to find a way for you to propose ideas that are about 'what is the scary thing'. It
can even be in the form of sample prose."*

So: twelve moments, rendered rather than specified. Each is one scare and nothing else. No
plot, no mechanism, no flags in the body of it — react to whichever ones do something to you
and the machinery gets derived afterward. Names are placeholders.

They are **not** a set. Several contradict each other about what the figure is, deliberately.

---

## 1. The sole

He had photographed it twice before and never gone close. This time the light was behind it,
so he went in under the wings to get the plate holder clear of the ivy, and he was down on
one knee with a hand on the plinth when he saw the underside of the near foot. Statues have
no soles. This one had a sole and it was dirty the way a foot is dirty — grey through the
arch, black in the creases of the toes, a leaf stuck flat to the heel. He was up and four
steps away before he had decided to be. His hand had come off the stone last, and the stone
had been the temperature of the morning, which was the only thing about it that was right.

## 2. The likeness

The wings were what cost. The face came free — *finished to the customer's likeness upon
receipt of a photograph, no charge* — so they sent the one of Aurélie in her confirmation
dress, and the yard cut it from that, and when the figure came off the dray everybody said
it was her to the life. Everybody went on saying so. What nobody said, in the third year or
the fourth, was that a face cut in marble in 1897 ought to have stayed a girl of fourteen.
Her mother stood in front of it on a Sunday in 1903 and made herself look at the jaw. It was
the jaw Aurélie would have had.

## 3. Tired

She had been coming out to the plot for eleven years and had never once thought the figure
looked sad. Sad was what they had paid for — the attitude of grief, two dollars more with
the wings out. What it looked like, close to, was tired. It looked like a woman in the last
hour of a day spent standing, past the point where the standing is work, when it has become
the only thing there is and the thought of sitting has gone out of her. Odile looked at it
for a while. Then she looked at her own hands. She did her weeding and went home, and she
did not come out to the plot again until All Saints', when everyone goes and nobody is
alone in there.

## 4. The long way round

The trouble with it standing down among the graves instead of up on a column was that you
came round the Terrebonne coping and it was simply there, at your own height, close enough
to be introduced to. Everyone in that parish had a way of not doing that. You went up the
far side of the cross, you did your own plot, you went out at the near gate. Boudreaux
watched an old man do it — eighty years old, two wives in that ground, a bad hip — and the
old man went the whole way round the wrong side with his hat already in his hand, the way
you cross a room where somebody has fallen asleep in a chair.

## 5. Dry Thursday

It rained Tuesday and it rained Wednesday. By Thursday noon the cemetery was dry — copings
dry, the cross dry, the leaves gone crisp enough to hear. The figure's drapery was still
dark with water from the knee down, the way a hem goes dark, and it hung. Stone does not
hang.

## 6. Her hair's in the way

The children were made to come on All Saints' the same as everybody and they got bored the
same as everybody, and it was the little Prejean girl, seven years old, who said it. Her
mother had said something about the angel looking down at the grave, which is what the
figure does; the hair comes forward over the face and the chin is down, and looking down at
the grave is the whole of what it is for. The child said no it isn't. She said it the way
children correct you about a thing they cannot believe you have got wrong. *She's looking at
us. Her hair's just in the way.*

## 7. Further off, please

The Ohio people had paid for the photograph of the grave because they could not come down,
and they wrote back in a good hand and thanked him kindly for the trouble he had gone to,
and asked whether he would be so good as to make them another one from further off. Further
off. He read it twice standing in the road with the mail in his hand. He had made that plate
from the road.

## 8. The toes

The flowers go on the stone. That is what the stone is, a flat place at the head of the
plot to put flowers on, and for nine years her mother had put them there. Then she began
putting them at the feet instead, in among the leaves, which meant kneeling, which at her
age meant help getting up. Céline did not say anything about it for a long while. What she
said something about, eventually, was the toes: that the two long ones on the near foot had
gone smooth and slightly yellow, the way a doorknob goes, or a rail, or anything that a hand
takes hold of in the same place for years.

## 9. Two coats

On All Saints' the whole parish is in there from early, whitewashing and sweeping and setting
out flowers, and it is the one day of the year when the ground is full of people and nothing
is quiet. The Landrys came up to their plot at half past eight and the plinth had been
whitewashed. Not the coping — the coping was theirs to do and it was as they had left it.
The plinth under the figure's feet, which nobody whitewashes, because there is no reason to
and it is not a tomb. Two coats, and cut in clean at the ground with a brush, and dry.

## 10. He did not look at it

The man had lost his bearings among the older plots and she pointed him out to the Fontenot
tomb and he was decent about it, and thanked her, and then he laughed and said he had taken
her for the statue at first, the light being what it was. She laughed too. He was a pleasant
man and it was a pleasant thing to say. She was two hundred yards down the road before she
had it: he had been standing an arm's length from the figure the whole time he said it, and
he had not looked at it once, not while he said it and not before, and not when he turned to
go.

## 11. Her arms

She weeded the plot on the Saturday, which is nothing — an hour on her knees, the ivy off the
coping, the runners pulled out of the shell path. On the Sunday she could not get her arms
above her shoulders. Not sore: they went up to a little below the shoulder and then they
would not go, the way a drawer stops. It lasted until the Tuesday. She had done nothing that
week but weed, and she had weeded that plot four times a year for nineteen years, and she
stood in her own kitchen on the Sunday morning working out how to get a cup down off the
shelf.

## 12. The shears

The sexton keeps shears for the ivy, because ivy will take an inscription in three summers
if it is let. Some while ago he began using them on the hair as well. It is not a long job.
He has never mentioned it to the family, or to the priest, or to his wife, and he does not do
it on All Saints' when there are people about, and he could not tell you when he started.

**What to do with this.** Say which ones did something to you, even roughly — *this one, and

shiver went, and the screws and the ledgers get invented afterward, if at all.
