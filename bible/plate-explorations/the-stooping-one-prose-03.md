# The Stooping One — Prose 03: the glance

Mark, 2026-09-14, on D-007 and D-008: *"just what you would get from it with a passive glance
that tweaked your amygdala and sent a shiver down your spine."*

So: one second of looking, and what it leaves.

**It has bent down to be face to face with somebody.** Not a figure standing in a doorway — a
thing the size of the room that has lowered its head to the height a person's head would be,
and is holding there, patient, waiting to be looked at. The posture is the one an adult takes
to speak to a child.

That is a different essence from the one `prose-01.md` worked, and I think it is the better
one. That file read the stoop as a **clearance** problem — it is too tall for the house, the
house's dimensions are a fact it works around. That reading was arrived at by inspecting a
doorway, and it is an architect's reading of the picture. The glance does not say *it didn't
fit.* It says **it came down to your level**, which is prey's reading, and it is the one that
does something to a body.

Everything else in the picture — what it is made of, what is on the floor, what the head is
like — is not in here, and under D-007 is not mine to read.

**Unhooked, deliberately, and flagged.** These twenty are not Verna and Emile Ivey. No fixed
cast, no fixed year, no shared house: a different person each time, because `prose-01` and
`prose-02` both fixed a cast and then generated inside it, and a cast is itself a constraint on
what can happen. Stage 0 wants variance and nothing else (L-022). **The Ivey spine is untouched
and still unscored** — this is a third axis, not a replacement for it.

> **Rule (off-page), provisional and mine (L-008):** It comes down to the height of whatever
> face is in the room, and it waits to be looked at. NEVER STATED. Nothing below depends on it
> being true.

**Burned before generating (invention-protocol rule 5) — the modal answers to *something
enormous bends down to you indoors*, banned:** it leans over the bed while she pretends to
sleep · she wakes with it above her face · it stands in the child's doorway seen from the hall ·
it reaches under the bed · a doorway lit by lightning · it follows her from room to room,
always in the doorway · the child calls it the tall man, or has a new friend · it plays at
peekaboo and the child laughs · pencil marks on the doorframe, a growth chart added to at the
top · glimpsed reflected behind her · three sightings, each closer · it bows, it is courteous,
it is a gentleman · it bends over a coffin at a wake · something the size of the house stands
outside looking in at the window · it gets taller each time.

**Children are not a protagonist here.** Story 01 spent that register hard and the plate
inventory's children row is a warning rather than a resource. The height the thing bends to is
the whole subject, so the temptation is structural, and it is declined on purpose.

## 1 — The good cups

Aurélie had her son at the table for the first time since March and got the good cups down for
it.

She was most of the way through the business about the Bertrand place before she understood she
was looking up at him. To do that she had gone down on her heels by the stove, the way a woman
gets down to a pot, and she did not know how long she had been telling it from there. He was
answering her from up in his chair, patient, not hurrying her.

She got up too fast and had to put a hand on the warming shelf. She asked him something she
already knew the answer to, about the road, and he bent down a little to hear her.

She was out at the cistern before she knew she had left the house.

## 2 — The measure of his own door

Terrell Prudhomme hung the back door himself the year he married and he knew its measure.

He came in with stovewood in both arms and ducked, and the load came through clean, and he was
three steps into the kitchen before the ducking caught up with him. He put the wood in the box.
He went back out and came in again and ducked.

He did it four times, standing off from the door each time and saying the height of it out loud,
and every time his neck went down and his shoulders came up and the top of the frame went past
somewhere above him.

The fifth time he stayed in the yard. His wife called him twice and he answered her from there.

## 3 — What the mother watched

The Sonnier girl had been at it since before dawn and Ozélia had the lamp up on the press where
it would do the most good.

The girl stopped pushing. Her eyes went off Ozélia's face and up to the ceiling boards, and then
they came down — slow, tracking, the way eyes follow a bird crossing a window — and stopped
about a foot above her own face and stayed there.

Ozélia looked where they had stopped. She looked at the lamp, and at the boards, and back at the
girl, whose eyes had not moved.

She put her hand flat over the girl's eyes and told her to push. The girl pushed.

## 4 — At her height

Félicie was on her knees doing the hall floor with the brush, working backwards toward the front
room, and the door to it stood open the way it stood open every day.

The light out of it went and came back.

She was up on her heels with the brush in her fist before she had worked out why. It had not
gone dark above her. The wall over her head kept its light the whole time and so did the
ceiling, and whatever crossed that doorway crossed it low, at the level of a woman on her knees,
and took the light off her hands and off the bucket and off nothing else.

She finished the hall standing up, with a mop she had to go out to the shed for.

## 5 — So that it would have been a prayer

Old Kyle was two hours at the Deshotels wake and had not said much, which was usual for him.

He came out of it bent double with his mouth by the dead man's ear. He did not know what he had
said. He knew he had said it, the way you know you have spoken in a church, and every face in
the room had come around to him, and the two women by the window had stopped their fans.

He stayed down where he was. Then he said it again, louder, with the *amen* on the end of it so
that it would have been a prayer, and straightened up, and got out past the chairs without
touching anybody, and nobody in that room ever asked him.

## 6 — Both of them looking up

Eulalie had the wash half on the line when Ardoin came over the fence for the scythe stone,
which he did twice a summer.

He talked to her about the dry weather with his chin up and his face angled, looking up at her,
and he is a head and a half taller than she is. She got the stone out of the crock and gave it
to him and he thanked her looking up.

She had the crock back on the shelf before she understood that she had been doing it too — that
the two of them had stood in the yard in the middle of a bright morning with both their faces
tipped up, and that whatever they had been looking up at had been between them the whole time,
and neither had said one word about it.

She gave him the scythe as well. He took it.

## 7 — Sir

Théodule had been blind twenty years and knew his sister's hall the way he knew his own hands.
Six steps, the chair, the turn.

He was waiting on dinner with his back to the plaster when the hall in front of his face got
smaller. Not a sound in it. The room came in and came down and stopped close, the way a room
does when somebody large leans in to say something to you, and he said *sir*, because he is a
polite man and it was the only thing there was to say.

His sister came out of the kitchen and asked him who he was talking to. He said nobody.

He sat down in the hall chair and said he was not hungry, and he was not moved from it.

## 8 — It had not done it for him

Alcée Trahan was the tallest man in that parish and had ducked through the shop door on Front
Street every working day for thirty-one years.

He went through it with the brick hod up on his shoulder and did not duck and nothing took his
hat.

He stood inside with the hod still up and worked at it. The door was the door. The step was the
step. He had gone under that header so long that his neck did it without him, and this time
there had been nothing to go under, which meant the house had made room.

It had not done it for him. He set the hod down in the middle of the floor where it was in
everybody's way and went and sat on the mounting block until the foreman came for him.

## 9 — She went back in for the hen

The speckled hen had got under the house again and Bertine went in after her on her stomach with
the lamp pushed out ahead in one hand.

Down there the dirt is a foot from your face and you go by your elbows. She had the hen cornered
against the third pier when a face came level with hers at the far end, down in the dirt, low,
because down there a face has to be.

She came out backwards faster than she went in and stood in the yard in the sun with her skirt
to her knees in dust and both hands full of nothing.

The hen was still under there. After a while she picked the lamp up out of the grass and went
back in for the hen.

## 10 — The landing

Two of the Guillory boys were getting a wardrobe up the stair for their mother, the big one with
the mirror in the door.

Sévère had the top end and swung it to clear the newel and called *under*, the way you do, and
his brother down on the fourth step ducked — went right down, hands off the load, so the whole
weight of it came onto Sévère's arms and the mirror went into the wall.

The wardrobe was nowhere near his brother. It was nowhere near that side of the stair at all.

They set it down on the landing to get their breath, and their mother came out and asked them
what they were doing, and neither one of them answered her.

It is on the landing yet.

## 11 — The sums

Miss Beasley kept the school at Pitre's Cut, twenty-two on the roll and benches for eleven.

She got the children out at three and went to wash the board and her knees would not take her
up. She got as far as a hand on the desk and had to stay. She had been down at the children's
height — on her heels between the benches, hearing them read — and she could not have told you
whether it was that hour or that week, because her stockings were through at both knees and she
had not noticed them going.

She put the next day's sums on the board from the floor. It reaches the bottom third, which is
where the little ones can see it anyway.

## 12 — The legs

Hollis Wicks came in off the boat on a Thursday and his bed was low.

The legs were off it. They were stood against the wall in a bundle with the twine around them
and the cuts were square and the sawdust had been swept and carried out, and his own saw was up
on its nail, oiled, the way he leaves it.

He went through the whole house after that and there was nothing else touched. Nobody had been
in it. He stood in the doorway of his own room a good while with his hat still on.

Then he lay down on it to see if it was right.

It was right.

## 13 — The rest of the decade

Odile said hers on her knees at the foot of the bed, which she had done since she was nine.

She was partway into the third decade when something came down in front of her face and stopped
there. She kept her head where it was. She had the beads going and she kept them going, and she
worked out, without once looking up, that if she stood she would put her face somewhere it would
have to come down to again.

So she said the rest of the decade. Then she said the whole thing over from the start. She was
still down there when the window went grey, and her husband stepped over her to get at his
trousers and said nothing, and that is the part she has never got past.

## 14 — He finished the room

Norbert had the ceiling of the front room to whitewash and did it off a plank on two barrels,
face a hand's width from the boards.

He was working along the beam when the brush went soft. It did not go into plaster. It went into
something that gave and came back, and it was between him and the ceiling, and it was the same
colour as the ceiling, and it ran further along that beam than he could see from where he was
lying.

He went on. He had four foot of the beam left and he did it, the brush going soft and firm and
soft again under his hand, and he finished the room, because he had the barrels set and the lime
mixed.

## 15 — About your height

The timber man came to supper and they put him on the good side of the table.

He never once lifted his head. He ate with his chin down and answered everything pleasantly and
left at nine, and Judson walked him to the gate and came back in and found his own chin was on
his chest.

He asked his wife, later, with the lamp out, to say what the man had looked like.

She was a long time about it. She said he had a grey coat. She said he was a polite kind of man.
Then she said he was about your height when he sat down, which is not an answer to what a man
looks like, and Judson lay there and did not ask her anything else.

## 16 — The broken thread

Ozéma took in piecework and did it on the treadle by the north window, eleven hours some days,
head down.

The thread broke.

She looked up, which she had not done since the light changed, and her face came up into a place
that was already occupied, and she took it away fast and got the back of her head against the
window frame.

She sat with the broken end between her fingers. She did not rethread it. She had the machine in
front of her and the work on her knees and the light going, and she sat with her chin down on her
chest where it had been all day, which was the one place in that room she could be sure of, and
she stayed until dark and then longer.

## 17 — The doorway she stands in

Léda Fuselier had two rooms of her own house she did not go in any more and no account of why.

Her husband cut one door in that house higher than the rest, years back, for a press he was
bringing in, and never trimmed the header down after.

She was standing in it. She came to with the dishrag still in her hand and no notion how long,
and the children going round her both ways, and she got out of the doorway and went back to the
sink.

Before supper she had her chair in it. She ate in the doorway with the plate on her knees and the
family reached around her for the bread, and not one of them asked her, which was worse than if
they had.

## 18 — Ceilings

The house on Poydras had seven-foot ceilings and the agent apologised for them twice.

He said *watch your head* going into the back bedroom and Carol did not have to. She is five foot
one. She had never had to.

She was writing the deposit check on the kitchen counter when it came to her that the house in
Slidell had been the same, and the apartment before it, and the place with the sloped roof in
Baton Rouge where you could not stand up on the far side of the bed — and that she had chosen
every one of them fast, and been happy in all of them, and had never in her life lived under a
high ceiling.

She finished the check.

## 19 — He doesn't have to

Marguerite's mother had been six weeks flat on her back. That put her face lower than any other
face in the house, so all day long everybody bent down to it: the doctor, Father Lejeune, the
girl who came for the wash, Marguerite forty times a day.

Her mother's eyes went to the door of the room.

Her mother said it clear, in her own ordinary voice, the voice she used for the price of things:
*He doesn't have to.*

Marguerite looked at the door. She looked at it a while. Then she went and woke the doctor, who
was asleep in the front room with his boots on and had been for an hour, and made him come in,
and stood behind him the whole time he was with her mother.

## 20 — The voice he uses

Emma was at the kitchen table stringing beans with the door to the front room open, and her
husband was in there talking to somebody.

He was using the voice he uses for children. She has heard it on their grandchildren, and on the
Ardoin baby, and on a dog once — that slow bending voice, pitched down, the words gone round and
simple.

There is no child in that house. There has not been one in it in eleven years.

She sat with her hands in the beans and listened to the whole of it. It went nine or ten minutes
and it was about the rain, and the price of shingles, and whether the road would hold, and he was
perfectly polite the entire time, and he never once got an answer.

She did not go in. She washed what was already washed.

## Notes

**Pre-flight item 0, answered in writing (L-027).**

**(a) What happens to somebody?** #1 she comes to on her heels talking up at her own son, and he
bends to hear her; #2 his neck ducks five times under a door he built and he ends up answering
his wife from the yard; #3 a labouring woman's eyes track something down to a foot above her face
and stop, and the midwife covers them and carries on; #5 he has spoken into a dead man's ear and
does not know what he said, so he says it again with an *amen* on it; #6 two people stand in a
bright yard with both faces tipped up at what is between them; #8 a man passes under a header he
has ducked under for thirty-one years and understands the room made way for something that was
not him; #9 a face comes level with hers in the dirt under her own house, and she goes back in;
#10 a man drops a wardrobe to duck under nothing; #11 her stockings are through at both knees and
she cannot say when; #12 he lies down on the bed he does not remember cutting the legs off, to see

already occupied; #19 her mother says *he doesn't have to* in the voice she used for the price of
