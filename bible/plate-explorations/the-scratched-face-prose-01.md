# The Scratched Face — Five Passages

Plate: `the-scratched-face.png`. The riff and the doctrine argument are in
`the-scratched-face.md`; this file is the prose it was describing.

Five passages. They contradict each other and none of them is the story. Each takes a
different **relation to the act**: doing it as paid work, having done it and found it
useless, having been there before it existed, finding it done and knowing whose face it was,
and being required to reproduce the pose that is in it.

Nobody sings. Nobody has a name for it. Nobody explains anything.

---

## 1 — Eleven a day

Retouching is done at a desk with a glass top and a mirror set under it so the light comes up
through the work, and Delphine Cobb had the one at the window of the back room and the use of
it from nine until three, which is as long as the light was worth anything. Mr. Rey paid her a
dollar sixty a day and expected eleven prints off her, twelve if nothing went wrong. Most of
what she did was nothing: a spot, a crease, a collar put back, a man's bad ear.

Perhaps one in thirty wanted a face taken out altogether. A sitter who moved and came out
with two mouths. A flare. A family that did not pay and does not get to keep a likeness. You
work the face out with the knife in short strokes across the grain, turning the print as you
go, and then you put the ground back over it with a brush and stipple it into what is behind
it, and done properly a person looking at it reads nothing at all — only that the operator
framed it that way.

They had bought a dead operator's stock out of the estate in July. Four hundred and six plates in
a crate packed with newspaper, and prints off the sixty that were worth printing, and she had
been three weeks going through them.

She came to this one after dinner and started it at ten past one.

At a quarter to three she set the knife down, because her hand had shut and she could not
open it, and the work was finished.

Not the face. The face had been out for a long while by then. The **ground was back over it** —
brushed, stippled, carried off into the sky at the edges so that the join did not show, which
is twenty minutes with a different brush and a different white, and the brush was dry and in
its place on the rail where it lives.

Delphine sat with her hand in her lap until it opened.

Then she held the print up to the glass and went looking for the place where she had run off
the edge of the face and kept going, because the knifing went up past the hairline into the
hair and down the shoulder as far as the light, and that is not taking a face out and it is
not what anybody pays for. There is always a place. Every piece of work has one — the stroke
where you can see the hand change its mind. She had been looking at her own hands across that
desk for eleven years.

Mr. Rey held it to the window a good while.

He said it was the best knifing she had done for him. He said he would like the rest of the
crate finished to the same standard, and he put her up to a dollar seventy-five.

## 2 — Eleven dollars

Aristide Dell paid two dollars for the print and one more to the man who mounted it. The pin
he used was his own, out of his wife's workbox, and so was the afternoon.

It did not work.

He had not expected it to and he did it anyway, which is a distinction he made to himself a
great many times afterward and never once out loud.

What he had not thought about, and there is no reason a man would, is that a negative is
stock. The glass belongs to the studio. It lives in a numbered paper sleeve in a rack in a
back room, and a print off it costs a dollar, and can be had by anybody who walks in off Camp
Street with a dollar in his hand.

The clerk was obliging about it. He wanted the number, which Aristide had, and then he wanted
a reason, which Aristide did not, because a studio that agrees to stop printing a negative on
a customer's word has to write something in the book beside it, and the clerk had the book
open and the pen already wet.

Aristide stood in front of him and worked at that for a while.

He gave them eleven dollars instead. That was the price of the glass, and it was two weeks,
and it was money he had told Léonie was going toward the boat.

He had it home and unwrapped on the kitchen table before he looked at it properly.

The scratches were in the glass. The whole radiating field of them, the run up into the hair,
the length of shoulder as far as the light — every stroke he had put into a sheet of paper
with a pin on a Tuesday afternoon, cut into a negative that had been in its sleeve in the rack
the entire time.

He put it back in the sleeve and the sleeve in the bottom of a trunk.

What Aristide Dell pays is eleven dollars, every year at settlement, in the form of the
difference between a boat and no boat, and Léonie has stopped asking about it, which is worse
than when she asked.

## 3 — The men on the rock

They were laying stone that summer for a dollar and a quarter a day, barged down the river in
scows and tipped over the side and then set by hand, which is done by men on their knees in
the wet with a bar. There is no stone on that coast. Not a pebble. Every piece of what they
were standing on came down the river on somebody's scow and got put where it was by somebody's
back.

A photographer came out to the works in July with a tent, a woman, and a costume in a trunk.

Ovide Fontenot was on the low course thirty feet under them and had the whole of it in front
of him for two hours, which is the only reason his word was worth having in August.

She sat where she was put. She held her arms up while the man counted and put them down
between plates and worked at her shoulders with her thumbs, and she asked twice for water and
got it the second time. At noon she ate with the gang out of the same pot and was civil and
dull and said she was from Algiers, and two of them knew the street she named.

The photographer was taken out of the water at the works on the eleventh of August, and there
was an inquest, because there is one when a man is found in the water and nobody saw him go
in.

Ovide gave his testimony and every word of it was true.

What he did not give them — because they did not ask it, and because he would have had to say
it out loud in a room with a clerk writing it down — was that she came up out of the water to
get onto the rock. Nobody rowed her out there. The tender lay off the works from six in the
morning and never lowered a boat.

He finished the season.

The course he set that week is under about four feet of water now and it is holding, and it
will go on holding, and it is the only thing Ovide Fontenot ever made that will outlast the
parish he made it for.

## 4 — Card eleven

A travelling man came through in the spring with views, twenty-four to the set, sixty cents
for the set and a dollar for the set and the album, and Eulalie Thibault bought both because
the Reys had both.

An album lives on the parlour table and gets turned by whoever is sitting nearest it. That is
what an album is for. A woman who buys one is buying a thing for other people to handle.

Card eleven was a figure on rock at the water's edge in a costume, which was a line they sold
and which nobody thought anything about; there were three like it in the set.

The face on card eleven belonged to her sister Zélie, who went down to the city eleven years
ago and wrote twice.

Eulalie took the pin out of her collar and did it out at the parlour table on a Sunday
afternoon with four people in the house and the door open onto the yard.

It took her until the lamps were lit. She went up into the hair and down onto the shoulder,
because she was not going to leave any part of it that a person could work backward from, and
she had no training at all and it shows — the strokes are long where they should be short and
they cross each other, and she went through the paper in two places and had to stop.

She put the card back at eleven and the album back on the table.

It has been turned by every woman in that parish since. They say it is a shame about the
damage to the one. Eulalie says it came that way in the set, which is the kind of thing that
happens with a set bought off a travelling man, and the man who sold it to her comes through
every spring and always has.

She buys something from him every year. She has never once asked him where the views were
made.

## 5 — The second sitting

Two dollars for the day and the loan of the costume, which hooked up the back, and which
Adele was hooked into behind the tent by the operator's wife, with her own chemise still on
underneath, because she would not do it any other way.

Sixteen plates in July. Ten seconds a plate and four or five minutes between them while he
went into the tent and came out again, and the rock was hot enough through the tail that she
had to be helped up off it at the end.

In July she gave him her arms up and her hands open, because open was what he asked for, and
open is what a woman does with her hands when she has been told to put them up.

They sent for her again in October. Same rock, lower water, and she went, because two dollars
in October is two dollars.

What he wanted was the July pose, and he had the print out of his case to show her, the way
you show a woman the pose so she does not have to be described into it.

In the print her hands are not open.

The fingers are bent. All of them, on both hands, at the middle joint, and held there — which
is not a thing a person does with her hands, and is not a thing Adele did with hers.

She said the plate had got it wrong. He said a plate does not get hands wrong, and he was
right about that, and she knew he was: hands are the one thing a plate is honest about,
because they are nearest the lens and they are the first thing to go if they move, and these
had not moved. Every knuckle on them was sharp.

She did it because it was two dollars.

She held her fingers bent at the middle joint for sixteen plates at ten seconds a plate, and
he was pleased with her, and she was paid at four o'clock and walked back in with the gang.

Adele does it now without being asked. Over a washboard, waiting on a kettle, sitting in
church with her hands in her lap — the fingers go and stay gone, and she does not know they
have gone until she happens to look down at them.

**Where L-006 fires:** properly in #5 (the fingers, involuntary and permanent) and in #4 (the
album left out, which is a price she pays in company every spring). #1 ends on a raise, #2 on
a settlement, #3 on four feet of water over a course of stone that is holding. Three of five
end on money or work, which is the register the plate keeps handing over.

**The mundane explanation dies once, in #5 only**, and it dies the way the beat index wants —
one exchange, with the one person who could have settled it, and he is *right* about the
photography. Nobody else in these five checks anything, because a fact needs no lawyer.

**What I kept refusing.** No song, no lure, no calling. No mechanism: the defacement is a
job in #1, a failure in #2, hasn't happened in #3, is an act of family bookkeeping in #4,
and is beside the point in #5. Nobody in five passages says what it wanted, and nobody uses a
word for it — the nearest anybody gets is *the one*, in #4, from a woman talking about damage
