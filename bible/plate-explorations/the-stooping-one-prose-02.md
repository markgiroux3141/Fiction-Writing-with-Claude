# The Stooping One — Prose 02: what the additions were for

Mark, 2026-09-13: *perhaps this "stooping one" was something the father was haunted by,
and his strange additions to the house were meant to prevent it from finding him.*

This is item 1 on the ban list at the head of `prose-01.md`, chosen. The ban list did its
job — it named the modal answer so it could be looked at instead of arrived at — and the
selection is Mark's (L-013). What follows is the version I think survives, and the two
things that would kill it are named at the end rather than hoped away.

**The move that makes it work: the defences are not a system, they are a man's behaviour,
and what she undid was not a ward — it was his arrangements.** Nothing here is warded.
There is no salt, no iron, no verse over a door, nothing that obeys a rule. What Emile Ivey
spent his last ten years doing is making himself **hard to find**, in two registers: he
built the upstairs of his house so that nothing could stand up in it, and he took his name
out of every book in the parish. Both halves are period-real and neither is supernatural.

And Verna, four years a competent and solvent householder, put it all back. Not from
carelessness — from doing everything correctly. The succession. The assessment roll. The
house number when rural delivery came out. The headers taken up for light and air. The
census, answered truthfully. The stone, cut deep so it would last.

**She did every single one of them right, and they are all one-way doors.**

> **Rule (off-page), and it must never reach the page (L-008):** It is looking for Emile
> Ivey. Nobody told it he died and it is not clear it would follow that. Everything he did
> for ten years was to be hard to find, and everything she has done since has been
> correct.

**Burned before generating — the reader's guesses at *this* premise, banned:** she finds
his journal, plans or a letter and they explain the system · the defences hold until she
breaks one and then it arrives, on the page, as the payoff · salt, iron, mirrors,
horseshoes, a Bible open at a page · a priest, a traiteur or a wise woman who knows what it
is · she rebuilds the defences and it is too late · it counts, or cannot cross water, or
cannot turn a corner · her father appears to warn her · it came with the land · **it was
after him for something he did** — a debt, a killing, a bargain.

Verna Ivey, forty-one in 1903. Emile Ivey, born March 1838, found under the house in
January 1900. Doucet the carpenter, Guidry the stonecutter, Mr. Latour the lawyer, Alcide
Landry on the mail, Mrs. Hebert over the fence. The piney woods above the lake.

## 1 — The spelling

Guidry's yard is behind the depot and he does the lettering himself, and he asked her how
she wanted it, and she said the name and the two years and nothing else, because there was
no money for a verse.

He chalked it on a slate and turned the slate round to her.

Then he read it out, the way they do, so that there can be no argument afterwards. "Emile
Ivey. Eighteen thirty-eight to nineteen hundred. That right?"

She said it was right.

He read it again, because the second reading is the one that counts and he has been sued
once over an initial. "Emile. Ivey."

She got out of the yard and most of the way to the depot before she stopped, and she stood
in the middle of a public road in a town where everybody knows her, doing nothing at all,
long enough that a man with a dray went round her.

She could not have said what was wrong with her. She got home and got the fire in and had
the kettle on the hook before it came, and what came was not a memory. It was
arithmetic.

Twice, that afternoon, in a shed behind the depot, out loud.

That made four times in her life.

## 2 — The book

The succession cost eleven dollars and Mr. Latour said it was money well laid out, because
the roll still had the place down to an estate settled in 1871, and a thing like that will
cost you the property one day when somebody's grandson comes out of Texas with a paper.

At the courthouse the clerk did the writing and she did the waiting, on a bench, for most
of a morning.

Then he turned the book round on the counter so that it faced her, and gave her the pen.

She signed underneath what he had written. *Ivey, Emile, dec'd, owner of record.* Then the
parcel and the arpents and the assessed value, and her own name below his as the heir, in
her hand, which is a better hand than his ever was.

When she had done it she put her palm flat on the page.

She had to take it off again so that he could turn the book back, and she asked him,
holding the pen still, whether she could have it a minute longer.

He said she could look at it any day she liked, it is a public book, that is the whole
point of one — and he put it up on the shelf behind him with the others, spine out, the
year stamped on it in gold, where it will be in a hundred years, and asked whether there
was anything else.

## 3 — The stick over the little door

Doucet took the headers up in the four upstairs rooms in the May for fourteen dollars, and
the timber that came out of the first one was a twelve-by-twelve of heart pine over a
six-foot-six opening, which is four times what the load wants.

He stood on the ladder with it across his shoulder and said, with no particular meaning in
it, that her daddy had put a mighty stick over a little door.

She said he had had a lot of lumber and not much sense at the end.

They got it down the stairs between them and it took both of them.

That evening she was in the hall and there was light on the top of the staircase.

It comes off the west window in the upper passage, through a doorway that is now five
inches taller than it was, and it lies across the top three treads from about six o'clock
until dark. She had never seen the top of her own stairs. She had known they were up there
the way you know a well is out there.

She watched it until the light went.

She has watched it every clear evening since, from the hall, sitting down, and she cannot
say what she is watching for.

The chair used to face the fire.

## 4 — Under the house

Doucet wanted her under there to see the sills for herself before she agreed to the money,
because a man who does not show you the rot gets accused of inventing it, and so in October
she went under her own house on her elbows with a lamp pushed along ahead of her.

The sills are out at the edge where the light is. He went along the south side pointing
with a screwdriver, and she came after him, and it is a bit over two feet under the joists
at the piers and less than that as you go in. The ground is dry and grey and smells of
nothing whatever.

She saw the rot. She said so. Then she asked him which part was the hall, and he put the
screwdriver up and knocked twice on the boards, and she went in toward the knocking, and he
did not come.

She could not get her elbows under her by the time she stopped.

The ground had been taken out under there. Not dug — scraped, and gone hard again since, a
long shallow dish of it running the way the hall runs and about the width of a man, deep
enough at the middle that she understood she was lying in it.

His lamp was standing at the far end.

Not fallen. Not on its side, not broken, not rolled against a pier. Set down on its base on
flat ground with the chimney still on it, four years, and a little soot up the inside of
the glass where it had burned itself out.

She brought it out with her. She has it. She does not know what a person is supposed to do
with a thing like that.

## 5 — E. Ivey

Rural delivery came out the Franklinton road in the spring of 1902 and the office wanted a
number on the post, so she had one painted, and the first thing that ever came in the box
was a seed catalogue out of Memphis. Forty pages. Free for the asking and sent to people
who never asked.

It was addressed to E. Ivey.

She knew at once how it had happened and she was right about all of it. The houses on that
road were listed off the assessment roll; the roll says what she signed it to say, which is
that the place is the property of Emile Ivey, deceased; and a clerk in Memphis who buys a
list of landowners off a clerk in Amite is not told which of them are dead.

That is the whole of it. There is nothing else in it. She worked it out standing at the
gate in the sun with the lid of the box still up, and it took her fifteen seconds, and
every part of it is correct.

She carried the catalogue up to the house held out at the length of her arm, the way you
carry something dripping, and she did not know she was doing it until she reached the steps
and had to change hands to work the latch.

They come every month now. Some of them come to her.

His go in the drawer of the hall table, and there are enough in there now that the drawer
does not shut on the first push.

## 6 — Verna

He taught her her name at the kitchen table when she was five, and he taught her *Verna*,
and he did not teach her the rest of it.

She took it for years that this was because the rest of it is harder, and it is harder;
there is nothing in *Verna* that gives a child any trouble. At the school Miss Dupre gave
her a mark for it in front of the room and sent a note home about it, and her mother
laughed at the note and said he had never had a scrap of patience.

He read the note that evening and said he would see to it. That was the end of it. The mark
stood.

She has signed the whole of it since she was nine and it is a good hand. It is on the
succession and on the assessment and on the receipt for the stone and on the order for the
lumber and on a note at the bank for a hundred and forty dollars with three years still to
run, and every one of those is in a book somewhere in this parish with the year on the
spine.

She sat down last week to work out how many times.

She got to about sixty and stopped, because she had begun the list on a piece of paper.

## 7 — Papa, your back

He came across the kitchen at dinner-time in the last summer with his head down and his
shoulders up round his ears, and she said, Papa, your back, and he straightened up.

He did it at once and he did it easily. There was nothing in it at all. Sixty-one years
old, and he came up out of it like a man of thirty and stood there square under a nine-foot
ceiling with the plate in his hand and asked what there was to eat.

Then he sat down and ate his dinner and got up and went out, and she does not know how he
went out, because she had turned to the stove.

She has had four years on what she saw and she has got it down to this. A man whose back is
bad cannot straighten up. A man whose back is sound has no call to be going about his own
house like that. Her father did both of those things inside of one second, in front of her,
because she asked him to.

He never did it again where she could see him.

That is the part she would have back, if she could have any of it back.

## 8 — The gatepost

She painted out the number in the March, one coat of white lead over the four figures, and
it took ten minutes and she was the better for it for about a day and a half.

Then Alcide Landry came up the lane on his second morning of not being able to find her
gate — he found it; he has been up that lane two hundred times; but the rule is the rule
and the rule is that the box has a number on it — and stood at the bottom of her steps with
his hat off and asked her, very politely, whether she had been painting.

She said the post had got shabby.

He said it had, and that he would want the number back on it, and that he was sorry, and
that it was the office and not him.

She said she would see to it.

Then he said — and she could tell he had settled on saying it somewhere on the way up the
lane — that if she would rather have her letters left at the store she was entitled to
that, and nobody would think a thing of it, and a good many widows did.

She is not a widow. She thanked him.

She had the number back on the post inside the week, in black, and bigger than it had been,
because while he was standing there he had remarked that the old one was small for the
road.

## 9 — There is no column for it

The enumerator came on a Thursday in the June of 1900 with a case and a hard hat and sat on
her steps, because she had not asked him in.

He asked who was the head of the household.

She said her father.

It was the true answer in June of 1900. The succession did not go through until the August,
and until it did, the place was his and the stock was his and the timber was his and she was
living in her father's house, which is what she had done her entire life. She had said that
same sentence to the assessor and to the man at the bank, and Mr. Latour had told her to go
on saying it until he told her to stop.

The enumerator wrote it. Name first, and then the relation to the head, which for her was
*daughter*.

Then he asked for the month and year of birth of the head of the household, and she had to
think, and the thinking took long enough that he looked up, and she heard herself say
March, eighteen thirty-eight.

He wrote that too, and went on down his columns — race, sex, age at last birthday, single
married or widowed, years married, occupation — and she answered every one of them, and
every one of her answers was correct, and the case went up to Washington in the August on
the same train as everything else in the parish.

He never asked whether the head of the household was alive.

There is no column for it.

## 10 — Eleven

Granite takes about forty blows to the inch from a man who knows the work, and Guidry cut
those letters two inches deep so that they would still be legible when everybody who could
read them was dead. He charged her extra for the depth and told her it was the last money
she would ever spend on her father.

She went out there on a Sunday night in the July with a lamp and a two-pound hammer and a
cold chisel, and set the lamp on the coping of the next tomb over, and knelt down in the
grass, and put the chisel into the top arm of the E.

She hit it eleven times.

What comes off granite is dust and a chip the size of a grain of rice. After eleven she had
taken the corner off one serif and put a white star in the polish about an inch across, and
her hands were finished, and at that rate it was going to take her until Michaelmas, and
somebody would come.

So she stopped. She sat back on her heels in a burying ground at eleven at night with a
hammer in her hand.

Then she picked up the lamp and held it up and looked at what she had done.

The name is perfect. Every letter of it is perfect. What is not perfect is the white star,
which takes the light from the road, and which is the first thing on that stone that
anybody's eye goes to, and it is an inch from his name, and it makes people stop.

Mrs. Hebert asked her about it in the September.

She said it was boys.

### Why this does not explain the haunting, which is the whole question

D-004's test is whether a reader can finish the story and answer *what was it* in a line.
Under this premise they can answer *what was he doing*, which is a different question with
a different object, and the distinction is already doctrine: **which end of the rhyme is
the thing?** (D-004, amended 2026-09-09.) Attach it to a person and it prices the haunting;
attach it to the haunting and it explains it.

Every precaution in this file attaches to **Emile**, and every undoing attaches to
**Verna**. Nothing attaches to the thing. The reader finishes knowing that a man spent ten
years being hard to find and that his daughter, doing everything a good daughter does, made
him findable — and knowing nothing whatever about what he was hiding from, what it wants,
what it can do, or whether it exists.

**And the town's reading is always available and is never refuted.** He went soft at the
end. Every single piece of evidence in this file is also evidence of that: a mighty stick
over a little door is a man who had lumber and no sense; a scraped hollow under the hall is
where a man went to look at his sills; a child taught half her name had a father with no
patience. The story must not at any point come down on one side.

**The mundane explanation is given in full, is correct, and does not help.** #5 hands the
reader the entire chain — roll to Amite to Memphis — and it is airtight, and it makes the
passage worse rather than better. That is L-018 inverted on purpose: that rule says kill
the mundane explanation because a fact needs no lawyer, and here the lawyer wins the case
and the horror is untouched by the verdict. Logged as a deliberate break rather than
smuggled; if Mark likes it, L-018 gains a boundary clause.

### The two tripwires that would kill it

1. **Nothing may ever be confirmed to have worked.** One precaution that visibly works is
   an observation. Two that agree are a system, and a system is L-016 basis 4 — *a
   consistency demonstrated at scale is a mechanism*. This premise runs straight at that
   rule and the only defence is that the story never scores itself. No passage may show a
   defence holding, and no character may notice a pattern out loud.
2. **It must never arrive as the consequence.** The obvious last movement — she took the
   last one down, and it came — converts the entire story into a demonstration and hands
   the reader the one-line answer at the door. The endings that are available are the ones
   here: #10 (she made it worse and lied about it), #5 (the drawer), #3 (the chair). All
   three cost her something and none is a payoff.

**And the thing to watch, which §4 names by its own word.** `craft-principles.md` §4 says a
thing that is the shape of grief is an explanation, and the D-004 clause generalises it —
*substitute devotion, **inheritance**, debt.* Inheritance is precisely the shape this
premise is standing next to. The guard is that **nothing is inherited**: the thing was never
his property and is not hers, she is owed nothing and owes nothing, and what she actually
came into was a set of household habits she did not know were habits. That is a cost. The
moment it reads as *she inherited his enemy*, it is a lineage story and it is over.
