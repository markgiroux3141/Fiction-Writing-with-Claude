# The Stooping One — Prose 04: at second hand

Mark, 2026-09-15: *"a scene where a person is describing seeing the entity or someone is seeing
someone else reacting to seeing it… Try to find inventive ways to do it that are new, not
cliche, and are clever."*

Twenty, and the brief is the whole constraint: **nobody in this file is frightened by the thing
directly.** Either somebody is giving an account of it, or somebody is watching a person who is
looking at it. Twelve of the first kind, eight of the second.

**Where the risk actually is, named up front.** Twelve of these are somebody talking, which is
the least visual mode anything in this repo has used, and D-006 says a passage that does not
frighten is not a candidate. Testimony is exactly what went inert in
`the-scratched-face-prose-01.md` — five passages of people handling an anomaly, nobody afraid in
any of them. So the failure mode here is not cliché, it is **flatness that reads as competent.**
I have tried to put the involuntary event in the *teller's body* or in the *listener*, and where
I could not find one, the passage should be called dead and I would rather you did that than not.

**The trap specific to this brief, and it is a prohibition not a preference.** An account drifts
toward explanation on its own — that is the knowledgeable local (`ai-tells.md` §C) and it is what
D-004 forbids. **Nobody in this file knows anything.** No witness has lore, history, advice, a
theory, or a second occasion. Each of them saw a thing once, has no idea what it was, and is not
told.

**And the second one, which is subtler.** Where a witness will not describe it, **the refusal
belongs to the character and carries a plain reason** — she was raised not to say, he could not
find the words, it was not her house. A refusal that belongs to the *prose* is L-003 obliquity
wearing restraint's clothes, and the passages here that say something about the thing say it in
four flat words: a man, bent down, in the door.

> **Rule (off-page), provisional and mine (L-008):** It comes down to the height of whatever face
> is in the room, and it waits to be looked at. NEVER STATED. No witness below is correct about
> anything.

**Burned before generating (invention-protocol rule 5) — the modal ways to stage a second-hand
sighting, banned:** the deathbed confession, *I never told anybody this* · the old-timer at the
store telling a newcomer the town's history · the madman raving while a doctor takes notes · a
found journal or letter read aloud · a witness struck mute who can only draw it · the drunk in a
bar nobody believes · the inquest with a sceptical coroner · the innocent child describing it to
a parent who goes cold · an animal that will not enter the room · hair gone white overnight ·
dead of fright with the face fixed · two accounts that match exactly · the listener who realises
they have seen it too · a photograph or sketch produced in evidence · she screams and points and
everyone turns and it is gone · the priest in the confessional · *it had no face* as the
punchline · the séance, the medium, the channelled voice · speaking in tongues · *you'd never
believe me* followed by a paragraph of purple.

## 1 — In the first person

Sidonie had done her uncle's English for him since she was eleven — the land office, the bank,
and now the doctor down from Baton Rouge with his notebook open on his knee.

Her uncle said it in French and she put it into English, and because that is how it is done she
put it as *I*.

*I see it in the door of the room. I am not able to get up. It comes down until it is in front of
my face and then it waits for me to look at it.*

The doctor wrote. He asked how long. Her uncle answered, and she heard her own voice say *until
morning*.

She asked to be let out. The doctor said they were nearly through, so she stayed, and said the
rest of it, all of it, in the first person.

## 2 — The fence line

Mr. Lunsford had come out about the fence line and nothing else, and he had the plat on the table
and a pencil he kept sharp.

Comeaux answered everything he was asked — the corner post, the ditch, the year the survey was
run. Then he said again that there had been a man bent down in his hall door and that he had
waited for it to move, which had nothing to do with any fence, and which he had now said four
times.

Lunsford said he understood, and asked about the ditch.

Comeaux said it a fifth time, the same words in the same order, and said it looking at the plat
instead of at Lunsford, with his finger resting on a part of the map that was not his land.

Lunsford looked at where the finger was. Then he moved the plat.

## 3 — Two cents

Azélie wrote letters for four or five men on that road who could not, at two cents, and she had
written Valsin's to his brother in Texas every month for six years.

He gave her the crop first. Then the price of the mule, then his knee.

Then he gave her, in the same voice he had given her the mule, that there was something come into
the house that got down on its heels to put its face in front of his, and that he had not been
able to look at it, and that he would be obliged if his brother would come.

Then he gave her the weather.

She wrote all of it. She read it back to him, which is the part she does for nothing, and he said
that was right, and she took his two cents.

## 4 — Mark the day

Pike had eleven men on the ditch and he wanted to know why the north end had not been cut.

Onézime told him. There had been somebody in the shed door, bent down, and he had waited on it,
and it had not moved, so he had come away and done the south end instead. He said it the way a
man says he had to wait on a train.

Pike asked did he want the day marked.

Onézime said no, he had the south end, mark the day.

Pike marked the day. Then he sat on the barrow with the book open on his knee and did not write
anything else in it for some while, and the men went past him going in.

## 5 — Company manners

Clotilde had company manners on her all her life and she did not put them down for this.

She told her daughter that a gentleman had come as far as the hall door and had stooped down to
her, being tall, and had stopped there; that she had not known what to offer him; that he had not
stated his business; and that she had sat and waited, which was all a person could do.

Her daughter asked her what he looked like.

She said it would not be kind to say.

## 6 — Forty seconds

Eraste had to say it to the priest at four, and at two he was in the barn saying it to the mule.

He got it wrong twice. The first time he put the door before the lamp. The second time he left
out that he had been standing up, which mattered to him, and he started again from the beginning
both times.

The third time it came out clean and took about forty seconds.

He said it once more to be sure, and then a fourth time, and by the fourth he had found a place
in it where he stopped and let it be quiet, because it was better there. He heard himself do
that. He put his forehead against the mule and stayed there until it was nearly four.

## 7 — Agreeing

They had Melançon in the front room with a glass he was not drinking and three of them
listening, and he told it straight through.

When he was done his brother-in-law said he had been working in the sun, and asked how much
water he had taken that day. Melançon said not much.

His sister said there had been a man on that road before, a peddler or some such, and that it
would have been him. Melançon said that was likely.

He agreed with everything they put to him for the better part of an hour, and thanked them for
it, and when he got out onto the gallery he took hold of the rail with both hands and stood in
the dark agreeing with it some more.

## 8 — He stood up

They were at the table and Octave stood up.

He was three quarters through his dinner and he came up out of his chair the way a man does when
a woman comes into a room, and got his napkin off his knee and into his hand, and faced the
doorway to the front room, and waited there.

Nobody at that table turned around. His wife looked at his face and his son looked at his face
and the hired man looked at his plate.

After a while Octave sat down and ate the rest of his dinner.

His wife has never once asked him. She is also the one who cleared the press and the churn out
of that doorway the following week, so that it is clear all the way through now.

## 9 — Yes sir

Dorélia slept on her back with her hands outside the sheet, and her husband knew every noise she
made.

She did not make one. Her head turned on the bolster — slow, all the way over to the edge of the
bed, and stopped, with her eyes shut the whole time, and stayed.

He said her name.

She said *yes sir*, plainly, out of a dead sleep, and did not move, and after a while her head
came back around by itself.

In the morning she had nothing to tell him. She was hungry and she ate.

He sleeps between her and the door now, which is not the side he can see her face from.

## 10 — The flour scale

There were five of them in Segura's store waiting on the mail to be sorted, and Mrs. Ware was
telling about her sister.

Aucoin stopped listening. He was looking over Mrs. Ware's shoulder at the door to the back, and
one of his knees went, and he put his hand out sideways and took hold of the flour scale, which
is bolted down.

Mrs. Ware went on about her sister. Segura went on sorting. Nobody in that store but Aucoin was
looking anywhere other than where they had been looking.

Aucoin let go of the scale and said, to nobody, that he would come back for his sack.

He left it on the counter. It is behind the counter yet and Segura has not sent it out.

## 11 — The rest of the sentence

Anatole was telling his wife what the man in town had offered for the heifer when he stopped in
the middle of it.

He stopped on a word. Not at the end of a piece of it — on *and* — and he stood in his kitchen
with the heifer money unsaid and looked past her at the hall.

She did not turn around. She has told her daughter since that she did not turn around because
her hands were in the dough, which is true.

It went on longer than a person can hold still for.

Then he said the rest of the sentence. He said it from where the *and* had been, in the same
voice, and finished about the heifer, and they had their dinner, and he has not gone into that
hall first since. He sends the boy.

## 12 — Four words

Pitre had seen it and was willing to say so and could not get past the front of it.

He said there had been a man. The deputy wrote *man*. He said he was bent over. The deputy wrote
that too. Then Pitre sat with his hat in his hands working at it, and what he came up with was
that the man had been bent over, and then that he had been bent down, which the deputy did not
write because he already had it.

They were at that twenty minutes. At the end of twenty minutes the deputy had four words and
Pitre had the brim come apart in his hands where he had been working at it, and he said he was
sorry.

## 13 — In writing

Mrs. Renfro came into the office about the house on Orange Street. She came in the way a person
comes in about a roof.

She had paid to April. She wanted to know what Tolliver intended to do about the thing in the
hall that got down on its heels in front of people, because her mother-in-law would not come any
more and that was a real inconvenience to her.

Tolliver said he would look into it.

She said she would appreciate that and she would like it in writing, and she stood at the counter
until he had written something, and read it, and folded it into her glove, and thanked him.

She did not ask him what it was. She has never asked anybody what it was.

## 14 — Through the glass

The kitchen window at that house looks down the yard at the barn, and Euphémie was at it doing
the pans.

Her husband was in the barn door talking. She could see his mouth going and his hands doing the
thing they do when he is being agreeable, and she could not hear one word of it through the
glass, and there was nobody in the barn door with him.

She watched him be agreeable for a minute and a half.

Then he came up the yard and she got the window open and asked him who that was, and he said
what, and she asked him again — and he stood down there in the grass looking up at her in the
window, and she watched him decide not to have heard her.

## 15 — Half up

Adélaïde was having her hair done by her sister-in-law, who had the comb in it and forty pins in
her mouth.

The comb stopped.

You cannot turn your head when a woman has your hair in her hand, and she had been raised not
to, so she sat and looked at the glass in front of her, which showed her her own face and the
window and nothing else, and waited for the comb to start again.

Her sister-in-law took the pins out of her mouth one at a time and put them in the dish, and
said she would finish it tomorrow.

Adélaïde went home with her hair half up, past two people she knew, and did not ask, because it
was not her house.

## 16 — Who went down

Two of the Castille brothers had been in that house together and the younger one told it at the
table.

He told it well. He had it in order. When he came to the part about it coming down, he put his
hand out flat and lowered it to show them, about to the height of a man's face sitting in a
chair.

His brother said that was not right.

Everybody waited. The older one had not said one word until then and did not appear to want to
say this. He said it had not come down to them. He said they had gone down to it — that the two
of them had been on their knees on the floor of that hall the whole time, and that the younger
one had got down first.

## 17 — Imagine that

Leufroy came in off the road and told it to his granddaughter, who is five, as a funny thing that
had happened to him: a tall fellow bent down in the door, and Grandpa said good evening to him,
and he never said a word back. Imagine that.

He did the voice. He did the bending.

The child laughed and asked him to do it again, and he did it again.

Her mother was at the stove through all of it with her back to the room. She did not turn around
and she did not say anything, and when he did it the third time she put the lid on the pot and
went out the back and stood in the yard where the child could not see her.

## 18 — The man

They got Sosthène back to the house and put him in the chair, and he would not say *I*.

He said a man had been in the hall. He said the man had stood there and had not been able to
move, and that when the thing came down in front of the man's face the man had shut his eyes,
which was the only thing left for him to do.

His wife said his name. He said the man had kept them shut a good while.

She said it again.

He said that the man was all right now, and asked her, politely, the way you ask after somebody
else's business, whether the man had been missed at his work.

## 19 — Twice

From that gallery you can see the whole of the near field, and Marcelite was watching her
father-in-law set tomato plants down the second row with the dibble and the bucket, going at the
rate he goes.

He stopped. He came up out of the row with the dibble in his hand and faced the shed, and was
that way a while, and then bent back down and went on.

He went on from the wrong place. He started again two rows over, in the row he had already done,
and set plants on top of the ones that were in it, all the way down, and then did the third row
the same way.

She watched him plant that afternoon twice. When he came up at dark she gave him his supper and
said the plants had gone in nice.

## 20 — About the door

Mabry came in from the smokehouse and his mother was standing in the middle of the kitchen with
the hall door open behind her and her arms down at her sides.

He asked her what was the matter.

She said the hall door had been open.

He asked her again. She said she had shut that door at dinner and it had been open, and she said
it twice more, and he could not get her off it — and the whole time she was answering him about
the door she was not looking at him, but at the place behind his shoulder where he had just come
in from.

He turned around. There was the smokehouse and the yard and the light.

His mother said the door had been shut at dinner, and would he see to it.

Verbal + motor-in-the-teller #3, #12. Verbal/social with the fear displaced to the listener #4,

Visual-at-distance #19. Verbal/deflection #20. **Nothing tactile anywhere in the file** — no

**Flags, honestly.**

- **Twelve of twenty are somebody talking, and that is the file's structural weakness, not a
  feature.** No story can be built out of this mode alone — it would be an oral history. What the
  file is for is finding the two or three mediated beats that a story built from `prose-03` could
  use once each, probably early, to put a sighting in the book without the reader ever being in
  the room for one.
- **#4, #5, #17 put the fear in the listener and leave the witness perfectly calm.** I think this
  is legal under L-026 and worth your ruling, because it is the most useful thing in the file if
  it is: it means a passage can contain a completely unfrightened account of the thing and still
  frighten. The rule says somebody has to be frightened *on the page*; it does not say it has to
  be the person who saw it. If you disagree, those three go and so does most of the value here.
- **#8 and #11 end on accommodation** (she clears the doorway; he sends the boy) — L-006, basis 1,
  fired twelve times. Two of twenty, against three of twenty last file. Both are also complicity
  (L-021), which is why I kept them, but they are the two I trust least.
- **#16 is not corroboration and I want to be explicit about why.** Two witnesses to one event
  whose accounts *agree* is L-016 basis 4 — a consistency demonstrated at scale. Here they
  disagree, and the disagreement is about what the two men's own bodies did, not about the thing.
  Nothing is established about it except that one brother's account of himself was wrong.
- **#2 and #13 are both bureaucratic refusal and both near neighbours of `prose-02` #9** (*there
  is no column for it*). Same instrument three times now across the explorations. One of the
  three, at most.
- **#5, #9, #13 and #18 all let a person call it *he* or *sir* or *a gentleman***, and that is a
  pattern across the file rather than inside any one passage. In a single story it is how people
  talk. Used twice in the same story it becomes a shared convention, and a shared convention about
  what the thing is *is* a mechanism. **One story, one witness, one pronoun.**
- **#11 and #17 have children in them** and neither is a protagonist — the boy sent into the hall
  first, the granddaughter being performed to. The plate inventory's children row is a warning and
  this is as near it as I want to go.
- **#10 is the licensed inverse of L-016 basis 4**, Mark's own fix: five people in a store, four
  of them unaffected and going on with what they were doing, one knee giving out. The rule gets
  established by the exception rather than by anybody being seen to obey.
- **Length.** 95–150 words, tighter than `prose-03`'s 105–165 and inside hailing distance of
  L-023. #2, #7 and #12 are the three that run long.

**What I need from you.** Which of the twenty did something to you and which were inert — and in
this file I expect a higher dead rate than the last one, because testimony is where prose has
gone flat on us before and I cannot tell from in here which of these are scenes and which are
transcripts.

And the ruling that decides whether this mode is usable at all: **is a calm witness with a
frightened listener a scare, or is it a scene about somebody hearing news?**
