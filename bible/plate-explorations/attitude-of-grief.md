## 1. First: what has already been written on this plate

**This plate has been written.** `craft/vignettes/batch-02.md` #4, *The angel's feet*,
built on bank entry **F2**, with a photographer named Boudreaux, a Villere family
commission, morning light, and the leaves at the foot of the plinth pressed flat in two
ovals — pressed a foot to the left in the second exposure. It is **unscored**: one of the
sixteen waiting on *did anything land physically / which sentence did the work / which
sentence is a lie.*

Two consequences, and they are the most useful things in this file.

**(a) The event has been used once, and I over-read that as a ban.**
`invention-protocol.md` Part 3 logs *a monument that shifts between two exposures*, and
vignette **F1 (the worn chair)** ran the same lever and was scored. My first pass declared
the whole family dead - pressed leaves, worn plinth, flattened grass, two-plate comparison,
the ground reporting what the figure will not. **Restored (L-022).** One 150-word vignette,
*never scored*, was doing the killing; the evidence for that ban was zero, and a spent event
is not a spent register. What survives is the honest version: the shifting-leaves *event* has
been written once, so re-using it verbatim would be repetition, and for anything nearby the
question is whether the story it grows into is a different story. **H31-H36 below are that
family, back on the list.**

**(b) The vignette's off-page rule is legible, which is not the same as fatal.** It reads:
*"She stood for the sculptor and never came out of the pose. The stone was taken from her;
she was not made from it."* My first pass ruled that out on D-004, because a reader could
finish it and say *she is the model who never left*. **That was a category error, and it is
the clearest instance behind L-022.** D-004 asks whether a reader can answer *what was it*
having finished **the story**. A premise is a one-line answer by construction - every idea in
this book is - so running the test at invention forbids all of them. Whether the prose lets a
reader assemble it is a fact about a draft.

What is left is a real but ordinary risk: this premise sits *closer to assemblable* than
most, because the figure is a grief monument and the woman is grieving, so the principle
that *a thing that is the shape of grief is an explanation* has less distance to travel.
That is a note to carry into drafting - keep the sculptor off the page, let no character
raise the model - and not a reason to drop it. **R9 below is restored to the off-page list
on those terms.**

**And the cheap thing that is owed first.** L-015 says stories grow from the vignettes that
generate *appetite*, not from the ones that are best written. There is already a paragraph
on this plate in front of you. Whether it made you want to know what happened on either
side of it is a two-line answer and it would tell us more than anything in this file.

## 2. What the photograph fixes, and what it leaves open

**Fixed by the picture.** A winged figure standing full-height among overgrown graves.
Wings fully spread — not folded, not drooping. A **Celtic cross** to the left and a
flower-carved headstone to the right, both weathered. Ivy over everything. The face is
**not carved**: slack, tired, human, hair hanging in damp ropes over the eyes. **Bare feet
on the leaf litter.** The stone drapery has the give of wet cloth. And the object itself is
a **paper print, folded in quarters**, water-stained, with the creases running through the
picture — this was carried, or kept in a Bible, not filed.

**Not fixed, and deliberately open.** Who commissioned it; whether it is a monument at all;
whose plot it stands on; the year; whether anybody in the story ever looks at it directly;
and **why this photograph exists in the world of the story**, which is the single most
productive open question in the set, because a folded paper print of a grave has an obvious
in-world purpose and it is not decoration. See theme 12.

## 4. Step 0b — the reader's guesses, listed

Named so they stop being the default answer - **not banned** (L-022). All three are
recoverable by execution; they simply start with a reader who has already met them.

1. **The weeping angel comes alive, or moves when unobserved.** Including the soft versions: it has turned, it is facing the other way, it is nearer.
2. **It is a statue of the dead person, and the dead person is in it.** The reveal.
3. **A grieving mother keeps vigil at her child's grave.** The most-written version of this picture, and the one a reader will assume from the title.

Also modal, also available: the cemetery at night; a rubbing or an inscription that yields a name; a date on the stone that has not happened yet (`ai-tells.md` §F's clock, relocated); the statue that weeps actual water.

## 5. Themes

Not events. What a story built on this plate could be **about**, in the §4 sense — an effect
and a cost, never a meaning. Twenty-two, unranked.

- **T1. Grief as a posture you can be held in.** The plate's own note. The horror is duration, not incident: a body kept in an attitude past the point where the attitude means anything.
- **T2. A memorial is a likeness, and a likeness is a portrait.** Somebody sat, or a photograph was sent, or a description was given to a carver who never met the dead. This book already treats portraits of the dead as dangerous (`the-sitter`, `the-wrapped-child`).
- **T3. Maintenance as love, and the failure of maintenance as a public statement.** Whitewashing, liming, weeding, flowers renewed. Who stops has said something to the whole parish, and everybody can read it at a glance.
- **T4. The obligation that outlives the obligated.** A promise in writing to tend a grave *forever*, sold once, for a lump sum, by an institution that cannot outlive the promise.
- **T5. Being counted as part of the furniture.** Not mistaken for a statue in a moment of fright — *filed* as one. Mown around. Left off the list of people present.
- **T6. Grief sold by catalogue.** Stock designs, in listed attitudes, at listed prices, chosen from a printed page by people who have never seen the thing they are buying.
- **T7. Hollowness.** Some period monuments are hollow, panelled, and openable. Something has been put inside one, or taken out.
- **T8. The wings are a consolation nobody's doctrine warrants.** Nothing makes the Catholic dead into angels. A winged figure over a grave is a folk comfort with no theological standing, and a devout household contains at least one person who knows that and has been overruled.
- **T9. The dead with nobody to come.** Labour dead, epidemic dead, storm dead, company dead — buried by a parish or an employer, in ground their people cannot find or cannot reach.
- **T10. Substitution.** A body that was never recovered and a figure bought so there is somewhere to go. The question the story never answers is whether the substitution was accepted, and by what.
- **T11. Exhaustion as the true face of mourning.** The face in this photograph is *tired*, not sorrowful. That is a register the book has not used and it is fresher than grief: a woman too worn out to feel the thing she is performing.
- **T12. The photograph as a substitute for the visit.** This print is folded in four because somebody carried it. Cemetery views were made to be *sent* — to relatives who could not travel, could not afford to, or had gone north. The whole story can run through the post.
- **T13. Bare feet.** Statuary is draped or shod. Bare feet on leaf litter is either bad workmanship or not workmanship, and a family that paid for the figure would have opinions about which.
- **T14. Grief is expensive repeatedly.** Crape is ruined by damp; marble sugars and loses a face in thirty years in this climate; ivy has to be cut back or it takes the inscription. Mourning in the Gulf South is a subscription, not a purchase.
- **T15. Class, read at a glance.** Above ground or under it; coping or wooden marker; figure or no figure. Nobody in the story will explain this and everybody in it can read it.
- **T16. The sexton.** A man paid to know where everybody is. His ledger is the only record, he is not a gentleman, and he is the one person who cannot pretend not to have noticed.
- **T17. Tending the wrong grave.** Care correctly given to the incorrect object, by somebody who cannot now stop without admitting the error.
- **T18. What is owed the dead against what is owed the living, when there is one payment available.** The figure or the doctor. The coping or the seed.
- **T19. The vegetal claim.** The book's own register, pointed at a monument instead of a body: ivy planted deliberately on graves in this period, then not cut.
- **T20. Being the one who grieves wrongly.** Too long, too little, too cheaply, or in the wrong clothes — and a district's judgment arriving as kindness.
- **T21. The figure that arrives.** Freight, a dray, a bill of lading, a thing set up by men who leave. Monuments are *delivered*, and delivery is a period-real event with paperwork and witnesses.
- **T22. The last one who knows.** An old woman is the only person left who can say who is in which plot, and the parish's map disagrees with her, and she is right.

## 6. Period seams

The strongest decorrelator available is real material taken without regard to whether it is
spooky (protocol Part 2, rule 4). **None of this is in `reference/period/` yet** — that
folder has death, mourning, medicine, weather, material culture and belief, but **no
monument trade and no cemetery administration.** Confidence is marked the way
`timeline-1850-1920.md` marks it, and per D-005 period is a register, not a research
standard: specify to serve the story, and verify anything load-bearing before it reaches a
draft.

- **S1. White bronze.** Hollow cast-zinc monuments sold by mail order and by local agents, roughly the 1870s to the 1910s, from a Connecticut foundry. They are **hollow**, assembled from panels, and a panel can be unfastened. They ring when struck. They also do not weather like marble, so the newest-looking stone in an old cemetery is often the cheapest one bought. **[check — confident on the trade, verify the dates and the maker before a draft]**
- **S2. Perpetual care.** Cemetery associations selling maintenance in perpetuity for a lump sum, and setting up funds to pay for it — a late-nineteenth-century development. A contract with a term of *forever*, sold by an entity with a balance sheet. **[check]**
- **S3. The monument agent and the catalogue.** Stock designs in numbered *attitudes*, ordered off a printed page, cut in a yard hundreds of miles away, freighted in and set by men who leave the same day. Italian-trained carvers working in New Orleans. **[check]**
- **S4. The *Angel of Grief*.** A famous 1894 memorial sculpture, copied and adapted across America for decades — which is where the phrase *attitude of grief* sits, and it is a period register: an *attitude* is a pose you can buy. **[solid on the sculpture; the plate's title is ours]**
- **S5. The Celtic cross in this photograph.** It reads Irish Catholic, and Irish New Orleans is real and specific: a large immigrant labouring population from the 1830s onward, canal and levee work with heavy mortality, its own parish churches. Death tolls for the canal digging are widely repeated and badly sourced — **do not put a number in the prose. [check]**
- **S6. All Saints', 1 November.** Already in `folklore-and-belief.md` and still the best single fact in the folder: the whole parish out at the tombs in the sun, cleaning and dressing them, so anything wrong is wrong in public. **[in reference]**
- **S7. Copings, the caveau, and reuse.** A tomb has room for a set number and remains are moved back to make space. **[in reference]**
- **S8. Cemetery views as a photographic product.** Grave photographs made on commission and sent by post, and after 1896 rural free delivery brings the mail to the farm. A folded print is a *carried* print. **[check]**
- **S9. Ivy planted on purpose.** Deliberate grave planting in the period, then twenty years of nobody cutting it. **[check]**
- **S10. Marble in this climate.** Sugars, greys, and loses fine detail in decades; a finished face is expensive and rare, which the existing vignette already knows. **[solid enough]**
- **S11. The sexton's role.** Digs, fills, keeps the ground, keeps the book, is paid by the parish or the association, and is nobody's social equal. **[in reference by implication; not written down]**
- **S12. Yellow fever's mass burials**, 1853 and 1878, and the unidentified. **[in reference]**

## 7. Protagonist candidates

Who tells it. The POV decides which theme is even reachable.

- **P1. The sexton.** Paid to know where everybody is, and the only person who cannot claim not to have noticed.
- **P2. The monument agent.** A salesman with a catalogue, working the parishes, taking deposits from people who cannot afford the balance. Complicit by trade, which is L-021's direction.
- **P3. The photographer on commission.** The existing vignette's man. Available, but he has been used once already on this plate.
- **P4. The woman who keeps the plot.** The obvious one; the risk is that she is the grief and the story becomes a thesis.
- **P5. The woman who has stopped keeping the plot**, and has to go back on All Saints' in front of the parish.
- **P6. The girl who was made to model** for the figure — from her side, forty years later, in her own voice. (Crosses hard with `the-winged-child.png`: same wound, one story.)
- **P7. The treasurer of a burial society or cemetery association**, holding a perpetual-care fund that has been spent.
- **P8. The carver**, or a hand in the yard, who cut a face from a description given by somebody who never saw the dead.
- **P9. The priest**, on the ordinary round, working off a list.
- **P10. A nun keeping an institution's plot** — the children's ground, unmarked and paid for by nobody. (Pairs with `the-house-with-everyone-in-it.png`.)
- **P11. The freight agent or drayman** who delivers a monument nobody in the parish ordered and needs a signature for it.
- **P12. A modern descendant** (D-002) on a cemetery-restoration crew in a parish whose families all went north.
- **P13. The person mistaken for the statue** — and the story is not the fright, it is the two years afterward.
- **P14. A stranger in mourning** who has been tending a plot in the wrong parish and is told so, kindly, by somebody who has watched her do it for a year.

## 8. What the impossible condition might be

**H —** Thirty-six, unranked, and **none scored against the four tests** - which per L-022
is correct at this stage, because the four tests are a judgment about a chosen story.
H1-H30 were written under my own pre-screen, which is why they all avoid wear, pressure,
plate anomaly, origin and schedule. **H31-H36 are the wear-and-pressure family restored**,
and H33 and H35 are the two the pre-screen would have refused outright.

- **H1.** The figure is on the plot and nobody ordered it. It is on the bill from the yard, itemised — cut, freighted, set — priced fairly, and the family pays because the argument would be held in public.
- **H2.** The family *did* order it, off a catalogue, and the face that arrives is a likeness of somebody living in the household.
- **H3.** A hollow white-bronze figure has been opened. The panel is refastened with the wrong screws, and the man who set it is dead, and the yard's fitters use one kind of screw.
- **H4.** The perpetual-care plot is being kept — limed, swept, flowers renewed, better than she keeps her own — and the association that sold the contract was dissolved nine years ago and its fund is gone.
- **H5.** She goes on All Saints' and the plot has been done before she gets there. It is a kindness. She cannot bring herself to ask the sexton, and she never does, for the rest of her life.
- **H6.** Two families produce paperwork for the same figure. Both sets are in order and both are correct.
- **H7.** The agent takes the order and the deposit and is never seen again. The figure arrives anyway, on the dray, with no invoice and nobody to pay.
- **H8.** The figure was cut for another parish, misdelivered, and kept because it was beautiful and free. Somebody comes looking for it, politely, with a receipt.
- **H9.** The ivy takes everything in that quarter of the cemetery and will not touch the figure. The sexton has stopped cutting round it because there is nothing to cut.
- **H10.** A body was never recovered — water, storm, or the mill — and the figure was bought so the family had somewhere to go. The story is what accepts the substitution, and nothing in it says what.
- **H11.** A photograph of the grave is commissioned to send to relatives who cannot travel. The print comes back folded in four with a request for a different angle, and the request describes something that is not in the frame.
- **H12.** The relatives write to thank them for the visit. They describe the visit. Nobody went.
- **H13.** A devout woman objects to the wings on doctrinal grounds and is overruled by her husband's family. The wings are what the parish then honours: flowers laid at the feet, not at the stone.
- **H14.** The figure stands on the adjoining plot, not the family's. The adjoining plot is unowned, the parish map says otherwise, and the map is signed.
- **H15.** The face was cut from a description, by a man who never met the dead, and it is right. Everyone who knew her says so, immediately and separately.
- **H16.** She kneels to weed and cannot get up. It lasts four hours. Nothing else happens, nobody comes, and it happens once.
- **H17.** A stranger takes her for the statue and she corrects him. After that the parish begins doing it too — greeting her a half-second late, then not at all.
- **H18.** A stranger takes her for the statue and she does not correct him.
- **H19.** Somebody has been putting shoes on the bare feet. The shoes wear out at the toe, the way shoes do.
- **H20.** The drapery is wet in a dry week, and she is the one who has to notice, and there is no water in that quarter.
- **H21.** The sexton's book has a plot he dug and filled, with a figure on it he did not set, and no entry for the setting, and he keeps that book properly.
- **H22.** The plot is bought and paid for and the parish will not permit the burial. No reason is given. The priest is apologetic and will not be drawn.
- **H23.** The perpetual-care payment is refunded in full, by cheque, drawn on a bank in another state, with no letter. The plot goes on being kept.
- **H24.** The catalogue's new edition has withdrawn the attitude they bought, and the agent will not say why, and he is embarrassed rather than evasive.
- **H25.** A monument is set on a grave that has not been dug, on the correct plot, in the correct name, and the family has nobody ill.
- **H26.** The institution's children's ground has no markers and never had. One figure now stands in it, and it is good work, and the diocese has no record of paying for it.
- **H27.** She has the figure moved to the head of the plot — a job for three men and a windlass — and it is moved, and it is where it was, and the men were paid and remember doing the work.
- **H28.** The freight bill is dated for a delivery three weeks off, and the figure is already standing, and the drayman has not been to the parish yet.
- **H29.** Everybody in the parish believes the figure is a likeness of somebody, and no two of them name the same person, and each is certain.
- **H30.** She stops going. Nothing happens. The plot is kept anyway, and it is kept exactly as well as she used to keep it, including the two things she did wrong.

- **H31.** The leaves at the foot of the plinth are pressed flat in two ovals, and the sexton rakes that quarter, and he has stopped mentioning it to anybody.
- **H32.** The ivy has been cut back off the figure by somebody, badly, with a knife instead of shears, and the cuts are at the height a person would reach from kneeling.
- **H33.** The grass between the gate and the plot is worn to bare dirt in a line, and that plot is the only one in the quarter nobody visits.
- **H34.** The plinth is scored round its base, the way a barrel is scored by being turned, and the marks are older than the figure standing on it.
- **H35.** The ivy comes away from the drapery in a sheet when she pulls it, and the marble underneath is new-cut - sharp, unsugared, unweathered - in a climate that takes a face off a stone in thirty years.
- **H36.** The figure was photographed once before, years earlier, by somebody else. The family owns both prints, has never put them side by side, and the story is that nobody ever does.

## 10. Rule (off-page) candidates

One sentence, chosen before drafting, **never written down** (L-008). Each must fail the
one-line reader test: if a reader could finish the story and state it, it is an explanation
(D-004). Marked accordingly.

- **R1.** It wants to be kept, and being kept is the whole of what it wants. *(Safe: unstatable as a fact, visible only as maintenance.)*
- **R2.** It wants to stand in for somebody, and it is not particular about who. *(Safe.)*
- **R3.** It wants somewhere to be, and a plot is somewhere. *(Safe, and the flattest.)*
- **R4.** It is holding an attitude and cannot put it down. *(Borderline — close to T1, and a reader may complete it as "it's grief".)*
- **R5.** It accepts attention from anybody and does not distinguish between the giver and the owed. *(Safe. Reuses a line from `stories/02-georgie/event-options.md` — one of the two must drop it.)*
- **R6.** It wants the likeness to be correct, and it will correct it. *(Safe, and the most physical.)*
- **R7.** It wants to be paid for. *(Safe, and the most Louisianan — the whole economy of this book is debt.)*
- **R8.** It wants to be delivered, and it has begun doing the paperwork. *(Safe.)*
- **R9.** She stood for the sculptor and never came out of the pose. *(Restored. The existing vignette's rule. Legible as a premise, which is true of all ten of these - the working note is that the distance from grief-monument to grieving-woman is short, so the sculptor stays off the page entirely and no character ever raises the idea.)*
- **R10.** It is what the ground does to anybody who stands still in it long enough. *(**Fails** — that is a mechanism, and L-016 bans workings.)*

## 11. What choosing this plate would spend

Recorded so the choice is made with the bill visible, not as a recommendation.

**Period.** The monument trade, the catalogue, perpetual care and the *Angel of Grief*
between them put this **c. 1895–1912** — a different world from story 01's 1878, which is
what the ledger wants. It also puts it after 1900, so `medicine-and-disease.md`'s epistemic
split is on the far side and the characters know what the mosquitoes do.

**Ending.** Recontextualization is spent hard on story 01 and accommodation is over-fired
(L-006, basis 1, fired 12) — so **the door left open** is the available shape, and several
of the H-list already lean that way.

**Physical.** The vegetal register is available and cheap here (ivy, the drapery, marble
sugaring), which means it is *not* the explicit third the book owes. If this story is meant
to pay D-003, it has to go further than plants.

**POV.** First person is owed and P1, P2, P6, P8 and P13 would all carry it. Dialogue at
length is owed and the cemetery on All Saints' is a crowd scene by construction.

**Other plates.** It probably retires `the-seated-angel.png` and `the-winged-child.png` as
story plates — three winged figures across the book builds the mythology the bible bans.
And it puts pressure on `the-house-with-everyone-in-it.png`, which shares the institutional
register with P10.

**What to watch once there is a draft.** Story 01's arms sitting in the same sentence as the
wings; the pressed leaves re-used verbatim rather than grown into something else; and a draft
that lets a reader assemble *she was the model* - which is a fact about the prose and is
settled in revision, not here.
