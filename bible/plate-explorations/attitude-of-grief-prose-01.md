## Three things his misses taught, and what I did with them

- **Deference is inert. The behaviour has to be readable as fear.** He: *"the respect of a hat
  tip isn't as powerful as not laying tools on it because the latter could imply fear where
  the former, not really."* Courtesy has an innocent reading available; **not touching** does
  not. Every behaviour below is an avoidance or an omission, never a gesture of respect.
- **The avoidance needs a denial attached.** He: *"someone avoiding walking past the figure
  due to fear, and trying to convince themselves that that isn't what they are doing."* That
  is why this is **first person** — the self-deception can then happen in the sentences rather
  than be reported about somebody. It also pays the book's first-person debt, which is the
  only piece of bookkeeping in this file.
- **A physics anomaly that argues its case goes flat.** *Stone does not hang* states and
  stops; the ivy and the drying built a case, and he felt the difference without being able to
  place it. Nothing here explains an anomaly. The two that appear are one sentence each.

Names are placeholders. Nothing is settled.

## Four Times a Year

I keep the plot four times a year, which is more than some and less than my mother did.
February for the winter rot, before Easter, the end of August when the ivy has had the
summer, and the day before All Saints' so that on the day itself there is nothing to do but
stand about and be seen to have done it.

It is not a hard job. The coping wants the runners pulled off it. The shell wants raking
back into the path where the rain has taken it into the grass. There is a lamb on the far
corner for my mother's first two, who did not live, and the lamb wants a brush and a bucket
because the marble has gone the colour of a bad tooth. Four graves and a lamb and a bench I
have never sat on. An hour, an hour and a half if the ivy has got its mind made up.

I go in at the lower gate. Two people in my life have said something to me about that, and
both times I told them what I will put down here, which is that the upper path is broken
shell all the way from the road and my knees have not been right since Clovis was born. That
is true. I am putting it down because it is true and because I would not want it thought
that there was another reason.

The lower gate brings you up the north row with the wall on your left, and you come to our
plot from behind, and you do your work with your back to the middle of the ground. That is
simply how the row runs.

The figure stands on the plot next to ours.

It is not our figure. We have never claimed it and we have never been sent a bill for it,
and my father, who would have argued with a priest about a nickel, never once raised it with
anybody. The plot it stands on has no name on it and never has had. I have heard three
different families said to own that ground and I have never heard one of them say so
themselves.

I will describe it the way I would describe it to somebody who had not seen it, which is to
say badly. It is a woman with wings, in stone, standing on a low plinth about a foot high, so
that her face is a little above mine. Her hair comes forward. The drapery falls very well —
even the Guidrys, who have the tall stone and think a good deal of it, will tell you the
drapery is good work. The feet are bare and the wings are out.

That is as much as I have. I have been going out there four times a year since I was
nineteen. I could not tell you the shape of the mouth.

---

On All Saints' the ground is full from seven in the morning. Everybody is in there whitewashing
and sweeping and setting out, and the children run in the rows, and there is coffee, and men
who have not spoken since a fence went in wrong in 1889 say good morning to each other because
the day requires it. By ten o'clock every monument in that cemetery is being used. Rakes
against the crosses. A coat over the arm of the Terrebonne stone. Somebody's bucket on the
Guidry coping and somebody's dinner on the plinth beside it, and the Landry boys sitting on
their grandfather with their legs hanging.

I have never seen anybody put anything against the figure.

Not a rake, not a hat, not a coat, not a bucket. In thirty-one years. There is no rule about
it. Nobody has ever said anything to me on the subject and I have never said anything to
anybody, and I would not know how to bring it up, because to bring it up I would have to say
what I thought was happening, and I do not think anything is happening.

Last year I took my rake over and stood it against the plinth to see whether I could. I could.
I had it back in my hand before I had finished thinking about it, and I went on down the row
and did the lamb.

---

Sound carries in that ground. You can be at the lower gate and hear a family at the top of
the shell path perfectly well, which is why nobody says anything in there they would not say
in a store.

There is a stretch of about twenty yards, from the Terrebonne coping to the second cross,
where people stop talking. They pick it up again on the other side. I have listened to my own
family do it, going up in a line, my brother in front telling something about a mule, and the
mule stops and starts again at the cross, and my brother has never once noticed that he does
it. Father Ancelet does it. I do it.

---

My sister's girl Marguerite came to us in the spring, which is a long story and not this one.
She is nine and she is not afraid of anything in that cemetery, which I put down to her being
from town.

She was walking the rows spelling the names out, because that is what you do with a child, and
she stopped in front of the figure and waited for me to start her off. I told her there was
nothing to read. There is no plate on it.

She thought about that a while. She said everybody out here has got their name put on them so
you know which one they are. I said that was right. She said so how does anybody know.

At Easter I told her the angel was watching over her grandfather, because that is what you say
to a child in a cemetery, and she said it wasn't watching over him, it was watching *at* him.
I let it go, the way you let a child's grammar go. She was quiet all down the shell path and
at the gate she wanted to know whether he could see it back.

---

We had three days of rain at the end of March and I went out on the Thursday because the shell
would have moved. The whole ground was drying. Everything was drying.

The grass at the foot of the next plot was dry in the shape of the wings.

I want to say what a figure like that is for, because I have thought about it since. You set it
facing the path, so that it can be seen from the gate, and you have the wings out flat, for the
look of the thing, because a person who is paying that kind of money is paying to be looked at
in his grief.

These come forward.

I have not taken Marguerite out there since. I have told my sister it is because of her chest
and the damp in that low corner, and my sister has agreed with me about the damp, and there is
damp in that corner and always has been.

## Two that could attach

**The sexton.** He has worked that ground thirty years and he could tell you which family is
in it from the shed, by listening. He has never been asked to keep the plot the figure stands
on and he has never sent anybody a bill for it, and it is kept. He does not know who does it.
He has thought about coming out early on the day before All Saints' to find out, and he has
thought about it for nine years.

**The child, later.** Marguerite is grown and does not remember most of it, and remembers this:
that her aunt used to hold the shears in her hand the whole time she was in that cemetery,
walking, standing, talking, from the gate to the gate, and put them in the basket only when
she was through the gate and out on the road. She never once saw her aunt cut anything with
them.

---

**What I need back.** Whether the voice — a woman explaining her own behaviour to nobody, and
putting it down *because it is true* — does the work your note asked for. And whether the plot
has begun to sublimate: the unnamed plot next to hers, the thing that is kept by nobody, the
child who is not afraid, and the aunt who stopped bringing her. If any of those four is the
one that wants to grow, that is the story, and the rest is scaffolding.
