# The Stooping One — Prose 01

Mark's premise, 2026-09-12: a woman lives in the house her father built; near the end he
lost his mind and built nonsense into the upstairs — stairs that go nowhere, doors that

Verna Ivey, forty-one. Her father Emile, dead four years. A town in the piney woods above
the lake, 1903. She has the house and eleven acres and the taxes are on all of it.

---

## 1 — Four inches

Verna carried the pan of scraps out to the hens at dusk and ducked going through her own
kitchen door.

Not much. An inch, perhaps. The chin came down and the shoulders came after it, the way a
tall man does it going into a shed, and she was out on the step and down into the yard
before any part of her caught up with what her neck had done.

She stood in the yard holding the pan. The hens came round her feet.

Then she went back and stood square in the doorway and put her hand flat on the lintel over
her head, and there was the width of her hand and some left over. There had always been the
width of her hand and some left over. She was five foot one. Her father hung that door the
year she was six.

She ducked again going back in. She was watching for it, and she did it anyway.

## 2 — The headers

Doucet came about the sills, went under the house on his elbows with a lamp, and gave her a
figure on the back of a seed-house bill that she could very nearly meet.

At the bottom, under the piers and the two days' labour, he had written: *take up hdrs, 4
rms abv — $14.*

She asked him what a header was. He told her: the timber over a door, that carries the
weight across the opening. She said she had not asked for anything doing to the doors up
there, and he said no, she had not.

"Take them up how far?"

He looked for a while at the bill in her hand.

"Far as they'll go," he said. "I ain't charging you for it. You'll do what you want."

He put his hat on. At the front door she reached past him for the latch and he stepped
aside and waited, with his hat already on, until she had gone out onto her own porch ahead
of him.

## 3 — Mr. Rills

The man from Amite went up at four to see the rooms and came down at twenty past, and he
came down bent.

Not hurt. Not hurrying. He came down the way a tall man comes out of a loft, head carried
low and pushed forward and turned a little to one side, and he crossed the hall like that,
and he stood in her front room like that, under a ten-foot ceiling, and talked about what
the lot was worth without the timber on it.

She let him go on for four or five minutes.

"Mr. Rills," she said.

He straightened up. It took him about a second and a half and it went through him in two
parts, the back first and then the neck, and his eyes stayed on her the whole time and
nothing whatever changed in his face, because he did not know he had done either thing.

He was a head shorter than the door he had come through.

## 4 — The finished face

The door at the head of the back stair opens on brick, and the brick is rough.

Mortar squeezed out of the beds and left hanging. Brick ends turned outward where he had
broken them to make his closures. Nothing struck, nothing raked, nothing wiped — a wall
built by a man standing behind it and working away from himself.

She got up on a chair once with the lamp and put it into the top corner, where the
brickwork meets the old casing and there is a finger's gap, and the light went past about
an inch. On the other side the joints were struck and weathered and every course was dead
level, and somebody had run a jointer down them, which is twenty minutes' work with a tool
you buy.

She found the lamp afterwards on the floor of the landing behind her, set down straight, in
the middle of the boards, well away from the wall. She had no memory of turning round to do
it.

A man finishes the face toward the room he means people to stand in.

## 5 — Under the stairs

The crock of lard lives in the press under the stairs and she went in after it on her knees
at about nine in the morning with the door hooked open for the light.

There was fibre hanging out of the joint between two treads over her head. Root-hair, dry,
the colour of tobacco, eight or nine inches of it, in a place where a broom has never gone
because nobody sweeps the underneath of a staircase.

She took hold of it and pulled, because that is what a hand does.

It did not come. She braced her other hand on the stringer and pulled properly and it gave,
about a foot and a half of it, easy and steady, the way a root comes out of wet ground when
it means to come — and then it stopped, and it was still going up into the joint, and it
had not broken.

She let go of it. It stayed where it was in the air. She got herself backwards out of the
press and sat on the hall floor in front of the open door, and the lard was still in there,
and it was still in there at dinner, and she used drippings.

## 6 — Both hands

She came in from the yard with the washbasin full in both arms and got halfway along the
hall before she worked out what was wrong, which was that her right hand was occupied.

She had been putting that hand on the wall at the foot of the stairs for — she did not
know. Years. Every time. Flat on the plaster at about hip height, three or four paces of
contact, off again at the parlour door, and not once in her life had she decided to do it.

She stood there with the basin and worked out what the hand was for.

It was for if she had to turn round fast. The whole arrangement had been built so that
whatever happened she would already have hold of something solid, and the part of her that
built it had never put the question to her, and had been running it every day for years,
the way it runs her breathing.

She set the basin down on the hall boards. Most of the water went. She put her hand on the
wall and went the rest of the way, and she came back for the basin in the morning.

## 7 — Nineteen minutes

A wind took slates off the north side on the twelfth of August and the rain came into the
upstairs passage, so she went up in the middle of the day with a bucket.

There was nothing up there. Six rooms, four of them her father's and wrong, heat like the
inside of a stove, dust standing in the light, a wasp working at a window that does not
open, and at the end of the passage the brick, and nobody. She put the bucket under the
drip. She was up there nineteen minutes and she had the whole thing straight in her head
coming down — that there is nothing up there, that there has never been anything up there,
that she is a woman of forty-one who does not use half her own house.

She was on the bottom step when she understood how she had come down it.

Backwards. One hand behind her on the rail and the other behind her flat on the wall,
fourteen steps, the way you come down off a ladder, facing up into the passage the entire
way. She had not decided to do it. She could not account for anything at all between the
bucket and the hall.

It has rained twice since. She has not been up for the bucket.

## 8 — The Landry boy

The Landry boy got up the stairs while she was at the line and she heard him on the boards
over her head and came in through the kitchen with wet hands, and by then he was down
again, sitting on the third step with his heels against the riser, entirely ordinary.

He was not frightened. That is the thing she has kept.

She asked him what he had been at up there and he said nothing much, and she asked him
whether he had gone all the way along, and he said yes, and he asked her when dinner was.

He has been up four times since. His mother stopped Verna outside the post office to say it
was good of her to let him have the run of the place, a boy with no yard to speak of, and
Verna said it was nothing.

There is a sentence that would stop it. She has had it ready for a month. It is the sort of
sentence that is said once about a woman in a town this size and then said about her for
the rest of her life, and she is forty-one, and she has eleven acres she cannot work and no
one to leave them to, and the standing she has is the whole of what she has.

## 9 — The lath

The kitchen window sash has been out of true since before her father died and she props it
with a length of lath that lives behind the safe, and she has been propping it with that
same piece of lath for eleven years.

She remembers where it came from. He carried it up and down the stairs for the better part
of an afternoon that last winter, stood it against things, looked up the length of it, took
it away and stood it somewhere else. She was thirty then and she watched him from the hall
and thought: he is measuring the ceilings. That was what she told her aunt he was doing.

He was not looking at the top of the lath. There is a pencil mark on it, gone brown, about
a foot down from the end, and she has had her thumb over that mark eleven years' worth of
mornings.

She stood the lath up in the kitchen door to see where the mark came.

Then she put it back behind the safe and propped the window with a flat-iron, and she has
propped it with the flat-iron ever since, and the lath is still back there, because she is
not going to be a woman who burns a piece of wood.

## 10 — Nine feet of beaded board

Doucet built it in a day and a half for nine dollars: a wall across the foot of the stairs,
beaded board, skirting round the bottom, two coats and the second one rubbed down. A neat
job. He said it would want a door in it one day, for getting at the roof, and she said it
would.

The hall is dark now and it is a foolish shape and it is the best money she has ever spent.

Mrs. Hebert put her head over the fence a fortnight after and said she had heard Verna had
had the stairs closed in, and Verna said she had, and Mrs. Hebert said, kindly and without
any weight on it at all, *your daddy done that.*

She agreed with her. She went in and stood in her own hall and looked at nine feet of new
board where the stairs had been.

She has not put her hand on it. Not once, going by. There is nothing there to put a hand on
for, and she has been up and down that hall four hundred times since he painted it, and her
right hand has stayed where it is.
