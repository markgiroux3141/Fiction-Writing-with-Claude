# The Scratched Face — Prose 02: Mark's Premise, Applied

Commissioned by Mark 2026-09-14, after the L-031 correction: *"bascially we just want another
prose file where my premise is applied to see if it works."*

The premise, as he gave it on 2026-09-11 and as it should have been rendered then: **a person
who was disturbed by the face and wanted it gone, who took something sharp and had it off in
five seconds.** One repair only, which is the single thing the craft folder is actually entitled
to object to (L-016, D-004, L-031's corollary): **the defacement does not work.** Nobody in
these knows that it will not. Nobody finds out why.

`prose-01.md` is not superseded. It is the record of the slow reading, and passages 1, 2 and 4
there are this same premise with the person taken out of it.

Three passages. They contradict each other. The act is on the page in all three and it is fast
in all three, and in none of them is the cost money.

## 1 — Five seconds

> **Rule (off-page):** It wants to be looked at, and it keeps whoever looks, and it has no
> particular need of a face to do it with.

Alcide Dell wrote the titles on the backs of his prints after supper, in pencil, because his
hand was steadier by lamplight than it had been all day.

He had pulled eleven off that plate since Tuesday. *Mermaid, taken at the jetty works, 1878.*
He had held the tent flap for the woman himself. He had counted for her, eight seconds and then
eight more, while she kept her arms up over her head and said what she thought of the rock
through her teeth, and at noon he had eaten with the gang and she had eaten beside him and been
dull and civil and from Algiers. Her name was in his book with two dollars against it.

None of that was any use to him at this table.

The print lay square to the lamp and he was looking at the place above her chin where the light
had come in off the water, and at some point he lost the order of his own hands. Not the
whereabouts. He knew where they were. He could not get hand and pencil and paper to arrive in
the proper sequence, and he sat working at it the way a man works at a word he has mislaid, and
it would not come.

The clock in the front room struck the half. He could not have said which half.

Then he was up. The chair was over behind him, he was at the press with the drawer out, and the
drawer had a rule in it and a box of pins and two awls, and his hand came back with an awl.

It took no time at all. Five strokes, six, back and forth with the whole arm behind the wrist —
paper does not resist — and the emulsion came up in a grey dust and got on his cuff, and the
third stroke ran off her face into her hair and the fourth went out across the sky at the top of
the frame and he did not correct it, and there was a noise and the noise was nothing much, and
he had finished before the chair stopped rolling.

And it worked.

He would want that written down. It worked. He laughed twice, out loud, in his own kitchen, over
a ruined dollar print, with the awl still in his fist; he would have paid the dollar again to do
it; he had not had an evening that good since his brother's wedding.

He was still standing there some while after.

He had not sat down. He had not put the awl down. He was looking at the print. What he was
looking at was the middle of the scratching, where it went deepest and the paper came through
white, and the lamp had gone right down while he did it.

He could not find the part of the evening where he turned back to the table. It was behind him
somewhere and there was no way to it from where he stood.

There was no face in it. That is the whole of what he could have sworn to: that there was
nothing there to look at, and that he was looking.

He folded it in four and put it in the press.

He got it out twice more that winter, in daylight, at the shop, with the door open and men in
the street — once in February and once at Easter — and both times it was a ruined print of a
woman in a costume and he could hold it as long as he liked. That is the part he could not get
past. Not the evening. That the thing kept its own hours, and he had no way to put a question to
it.

He worked another thirty-one years. He photographed brides and dead children and the
second-place crew of the regatta, and he was good, and he was cheap, and there was one pose he
would not take. Chin up, head back, the eyes off the top of the frame. The young ones asked for
it, because that was the attitude that year. He would come out from under the cloth and move
their heads down with two fingers under the jaw, very gently, the way you turn a horse, and tell
them the light was wrong.

The light was not wrong. He lost two weddings a year saying it.

## 2 — The one who saw

> **Rule (off-page):** It shows itself to one person in a room, and it leaves that person no
> corroboration.

Adele Fontenot brought the folder out after the plates were cleared, because there were six at
the table and nothing to do until the rain let up.

She had bought it off a stall on Chartres for forty cents: thirty-one prints, no two from the
same studio, a lot out of some dead man's stock. A two-headed calf. A Zouave. The Gulf at
Biloxi with the horizon crooked. She had shown them twice before and both times they went round
the table the same way, hand to hand, right to left, and came back to her with somebody's
thumbprint on the calf.

Théodule Marsh took them in the order they reached him. He was her sister's husband, he had a
bad ear, and he sat where he always sat, at the end with his back to the window and the lamp
behind him, lifting them off the top and laying them face down on his left. He had said
something about the Zouave that made the table laugh.

He went through the mermaid the way you deal a card.

Adele had the whole of it from four feet away. He looked at it, he put it flat on the cloth, he
took his knife out and opened the blade against his thigh, and he cut the face out of the print
in about as long as it takes to strike a match — a fast scrubbing motion, wrist only, up and
back, the elbow never leaving his side. Then he shut the knife, put the print face down on his
left, and picked up the next one.

She said his name.

She heard herself say it, and she heard it come out at the right volume. Her sister went on
about the guttering. The boy at the far end reached across for the bread. Théodule turned over
the Gulf at Biloxi, held it up, and said the horizon was crooked. Six people at a table, rain on
the shutters, and the lamp behind him had been on his hands the entire time, and not one of them
looked at her and not one of them looked at him.

She did not say it again. She has been over the reason a great many times and she is not
confident of it.

The folder came round to her the way it always did, and the mermaid was in it, gouged white, the
scratching running off into the hair and up into the sky, and the paper dust was still on the
cloth where his knife had been. She put her hand flat over the mermaid and drew the folder into
her lap. She found later that she had bowed the boards of it between her hands and it never sat
flat again.

When her sister asked, in June, what had become of the mermaid, Adele said it had come like that
from the stall and she had not noticed at the time.

She was sixty-one when she died and she never once handed a photograph to anybody. She held them
out at the corner and let people put their heads over her shoulder, and her family thought it was
her hands.

## 3 — The one over the mantel

> **Rule (off-page):** It does not stay in the photograph it was found in, and it has no
> interest in being got rid of.

Sylvain Thibault took the mermaid out to the yard on a Sunday and did it standing up, with the
marlin spike he kept for the boat, and it took less time than lighting a pipe.

He was twenty-six. He had bought the thing at the works for a dollar because the other fellows
were buying them, he had looked at it maybe forty times in eleven days, and then he did not have
to any more. He went in and ate a big dinner. He remembers the dinner.

Eleven days after that it was his mother's picture of his father he could not be in a room with.

That one had hung over the mantel since 1871, black frame, gilt slip, and there was nothing the
matter with it. His father at thirty-odd, seated, one hand on a book he never read, the chin up a
little because the operator had put it up. Sylvain had grown up under it. He could not now be in
the front room with it and face away from it, and he could not be in the front room with it and
face it, and there is no third thing to do in a front room.

He took it down on the Tuesday and put it in the press in the shed with the mermaid.

His mother had the frame back up by supper. She wanted to know was he ashamed of his father. He
said no. She asked him what then, and stood there, and the whole of what he had was *I cannot be in the
room with it*, and a man of twenty-six does not say that to his mother in her own front room. He
said the damp had got behind the glass and he would see to it. She said see to it, then. It hung
there.

He saw to it in January, while she was at Mass.

Same motion, standing up, spike out of his belt, the glass off and leaning against the fender —
four strokes, five, wrist and forearm — and it went off the face into the collar and up into the
grey behind the head, and the mount was thick and took the point deeper than paper would have,
and the glass was back in before the frame had stopped swinging on its wire.

Nothing came. He stood in front of it with the spike in his hand and waited, the way you wait for
a step to hurt, and nothing came at all.

He hung it straight and went out to the boat.

She never took it down. It was up over that mantel for twenty-two years, his father with a white
blaze where his face had been, and she put a clean runner under it and kept the runner clean, and
visitors looked at it and got no encouragement and looked at something else. He sat under it at
every meal he ate in that house, because the chair facing the mantel was his chair and always had
been.

At Christmas in 1897, with the table full and his own sons at it, his mother said across the ham
that Sylvain had seen to the damp, and that he had seen to it thoroughly.

He laughed. Everybody laughed, and then they went on to something else, and he has laughed at it
every Christmas since, and it comes round every year, and it will come round again.

## What these do, and what to look at

**The act is the scare in all three, which is what `prose-01.md` did not have.** There the
defacement was a job (1), a purchase (2), or had not happened yet (3). Here somebody with
something sharp in their hand ruins a photograph in five seconds in front of the reader, meaning
it, and gets nothing durable for it.

**Each one fails differently, and none of them fails by being anticipated.**

| | Who holds the tool | What the five seconds buys | What it costs, permanently |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The man who made the plate | Everything, for about a minute | One pose he will not take, for thirty-one years, and the money that goes with it |
| 2 | Somebody else, seen by one witness | Nothing to him; he goes on to the next print | She corroborates his story for forty years, and never hands anybody a photograph |
| 3 | A man who had it work once | Nothing. He stands and waits and nothing comes | The seat facing the mantel, and the laugh, every Christmas |

**On #1, and the thing that makes it frightening rather than sad.** The defacement is not futile
because he was foolish to try. It works, completely and physically, and he would do it again —
and then he is still standing at the table looking at the place where the face was, and he has
thrown away the only means he had of telling whether it was looking back. He removed the
instrument, not the attention. Nobody says this and no character is in a position to.

**On complicity (L-021).** All three men act. None of them is taken over, nothing moves their
hands, and the reading in `prose-01-r2.md` — the knife going on working by itself — is
deliberately not reused, because it is an alibi. Dell decides. Marsh decides. Thibault decides
twice, and the second time he has had two weeks to think about it.

**Flags, honestly.**

- **#3 brushes L-016.** *It moved to another photograph* is one event, once, never repeated and
  never explained, which is why I judge it an event rather than a demonstrated rule. If you read
  it as the thing acquiring a mechanism, #3 is the one to cut — it is the passage I am least sure
  of.
- **`1878` is unverified.** The jetty works date is still not in `reference/period/`; the
  exploration flagged it and it is now in prose. D-005 register standard, but check it or lose
  the year.
- **"the attitude that year" in #1** resonates with the `the-attitude-of-grief` plate title.
  Probably an asset, possibly a collision. Your call.
- **#2 has the least body in it.** The bowed folder boards are the only involuntary event and
  they arrive late, after the room fails to react. It may still be a reported anomaly with a
  witness attached rather than a passage where somebody is frightened (L-026).

**Pre-flight item 0, answered per passage.**

1. **(a)** He goes to get a pencil and comes back having destroyed something with an awl, and
   cannot find the moment he turned. **(b)** *There was no face in it. That is the whole of what
   he could have sworn to: that there was nothing there to look at, and that he was looking.*
2. **(a)** She watches a man mutilate a photograph in lamplight in front of five people, says
   his name at the right volume, and the room does not arrive. **(b)** *and not one of them
   looked at her and not one of them looked at him.*
3. **(a)** He is put out of a room he grew up in by a picture of his own father, and cannot say
   so to the person who keeps hanging it. **(b)** *Nothing came.*
