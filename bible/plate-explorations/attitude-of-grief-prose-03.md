# The Angel — Ti-Jean

Mark's conception, 2026-09-09: the immune one is *"someone slim with a small stature, perhaps
mentally challenged with a physical deformity that is unsettling to describe, microcephaly
maybe but described in 19th century talk... a birth defect that affected his mother who died
giving birth to him. He is taken in by relatives, perhaps they are mean to him. He seems drawn
to the figure but is always with family members who instinctively lead him away from the
figure. At some point he finds his way to the figure, there is some vague and unsettling
description of death, people find him in some state or some pose that is unnatural and
extremely unsettling, and there is some extremely careful hinting at a connection between the
figure and his mother."*

## Where I have moved off it, and why

**The relatives are not mean.** They are tired, and he is a great deal of work, and the
narrator is one of them. Cruelty hands the reader a moral structure, and a moral structure is
something to solve — the family are villains, he is innocent, the story is *about* how we
treat such people. Ordinary exhausted decency is far worse, because there is nothing to
convict anybody of and the narrator cannot get out from under it (L-021).

**He is not found in the figure's posture.** Story 01 spent a body laid out with the arms
straight out to the sides, and a corpse echoing the statue answers *what was it* in one
picture. What is unnatural about how he is found is a **practical** problem, and the story
gives the reader one detail of it and withholds the rest, because the people who saw it will
not describe it and never have.

**The deformity is rendered by consequence, not described.** No clinical account and no
inventory. Hats, barbers, where people put their eyes. It is more unsettling and it does not
turn him into an exhibit.

**The mother-link is in his mouth only.** He says a thing that makes sense only if he believes
it; he is a man nobody credits; and he has a habit that undercuts it. The reader gets the
chill and can never close the question, and the story never once takes a position. If it still
reads as an answer, the two passages carrying it (5 and 9) lift out cleanly and nothing else
changes.

## 1. His hats

Jean-Baptiste Guidry, that everybody called Ti-Jean, and he was nineteen when this happened
and had been called Ti-Jean since he was carried.

Nothing in the store ever fit him, so his aunt made his hats. She took the band out of a
child's hat and cut it and worked it round again, and it took her an evening, and she did four
or five of them over the years and never made anything of it. His shoes were a child's shoes.
He wore a man's coat because a coat does not have to fit.

I will say this once and then leave it alone. People talked to his hands. You would see it in
the store — a man would say good morning to Ti-Jean, perfectly friendly, and he would say it
to Ti-Jean's hands, and Ti-Jean would answer, and the man would go on looking at his hands
until it was over. I did it. I did it for nineteen years and I never once decided to.

## 2. His mother

Léonide was my cousin and she was twenty-two and it was a long birth and a bad one, and the
doctor came out from town on the second morning and there was nothing for him to do that had
not been done. She lived four days after. There is a version of this in that family where the
child did it to her, and it has never been said out loud in my hearing, and every single
person in that house has heard it.

He went to his aunt because his aunt had the room. That is the whole of the reason. There is
no cruelty in it anywhere and I have gone looking.

## 3. Leading him away

He liked the cemetery, and he was taken there like anybody, on All Saints' and for burials
and when somebody was going out to do a plot and could take him.

He went for her every time. Not fast, and not in a way you would call anything — he would be
with you, and then he would be four steps off and going, in the middle of the path, the way a
child goes toward a dog.

And whoever had him would put a hand on his shoulder and turn him. That is all it was. A hand
on the shoulder and *come on now, Ti-Jean, come see*, and he came, because he was easy and he
always came. I have seen his aunt do it. I have seen Ovide do it and Father Ancelet do it and
I have seen his aunt's girl do it, who was eleven.

I have done it more times than I could count. It was nothing. You do it to a child near a
horse.

## 4. What he wanted

I want to be accurate. He was not frightened of her and he was not excited about her. He
wanted to go and stand where she was, the way you would want to go and stand where somebody
you knew was standing, and when he was turned he did not fuss and did not look back, and ten
minutes later he would go again.

Nineteen years of that, four or five times a year, and I do not believe he was ever once let
get there.

## 5. What he called her

He called her *maman*.

I want that put down carefully because it will be taken up wrong. He called a good many people
maman. He called Father Ancelet's housekeeper maman for eleven years and she was seventy and
she liked it. He called my mother maman. He called the mare maman, and my brother thought that
was the funniest thing he ever heard and used to get him to do it in front of people.

So it is nothing. It is a word he had and he put it on things he was fond of and there is no
more to it than that, and I would say so to anybody, and I have.

## 6. The day

It was the Saturday before All Saints', which is the day the whole parish is out there getting
the plots ready, and it is the busiest day in that ground all year, and there were forty
people in it.

That is how, and I have thought about it for a long while and it does not get any better. Any
other day there would have been one person with him and that person would have had a hand on
his shoulder. That day there were forty, and every one of them had a bucket and a brush and
their own dead to see to, and every one of them saw him go by and thought he was with
somebody.

He was with somebody all morning. He was with everybody all morning.

## 7. How he was found

Ovide found him and Théo Landry was with him, and neither of those men has ever said what they
saw, not to me and not to anybody, and both of them were asked at the time by people with more
right to ask than I had.

Ovide told me one thing, in the yard, that evening, because I would not stop at him until he
gave me something. He said they could not get his hand down.

I have never asked another question about it since. Not one, in thirty years, of either of
them.

## 8. His hat was on the plinth

That is the part I have.

He always put his hat on the plinth. I had never seen him do it, because he was never let get
there, but the sexton had seen it — he had seen it twice from the shed, on days when nobody
was minding, and had come out and led him off himself. Ti-Jean would set his hat down on the
plinth first, the way a man sets his hat down when he comes into a house, and then go on.

His hat was on the plinth.

So he had been in no hurry. He had gone up there and he had put his hat down, which takes a
moment and which you do not do if anything at all is the matter. That is the thing I have not
been able to put anywhere for thirty years. Not what was wrong with him afterwards. That he
had been comfortable.

## 9. The year

The figure came into that ground the year Léonide died. I know that because my mother knew it
and said it once, in the kitchen, about something else entirely, and never said it again.

It does not signify. Half the stones out there went up in the nineties and people were dying
the whole time, and you could take any two things in that parish and lay them next to each
other and get a year to match.

I have never said it to anybody. I am not saying it now. I am putting it down.

## 10. What I have to put down about myself

I turned him away from her more times than anybody except his aunt.

I have let it be understood, for thirty years, that this was for his sake — that he was a
young man with no sense of a road or a horse or a stove, and you keep hold of such a person,
and everybody agrees with that and it is true.

It is true and it is not the reason. The reason is that I could not stand to watch him get
there. I could not stand to watch him put his hand up to it, which is what he would have done,
because I had a very good idea by then of what his hand would look like against it and I did
not want that in my head.

So I kept it out of my head for nineteen years, with my hand on his shoulder, and on the
Saturday before All Saints' I was three rows off doing my own plot with my back to the middle
of the ground, and I saw him go by me up the middle of the path, and I thought he was with
somebody.

## Notes

**What this pays.** The avoidance is now proved by its exception and never asserted. The
narrator has no alibi — she supplies the reason, and then supplies the real one, and they are
both true (L-021). The physical arrives late, once, and mostly withheld, which leaves D-003's
explicit third still owed by the book rather than spent here on hearsay.

**The two removable passages** are 5 and 9. Pull them and the story is a man who was fond of
a statue and died at it; keep them and a reader has something they can neither finish nor put
down. My read is that they hold, because 5 destroys its own evidence in its second paragraph
and 9 refuses itself in its third — but this is exactly the call that wants a fresh-context
read before it wants my opinion (L-020).

**Still open, and not decided here.** Where he is buried. The family ground, the unowned plot,
or somewhere else entirely — and who decides it, and how fast the parish agrees.
