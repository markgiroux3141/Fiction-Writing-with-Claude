## What these are, and what they are not

**These are premises, not events.** A premise supplies an arena, a wound and one
impossible condition. The **event** — what actually happens on the page, scored against
the four tests — is generated *after* a premise is chosen, in the story folder, the way
`stories/02-georgie/event-options.md` was. A premise that survives selection gets its own
twenty-candidate pool. Nothing here has been scored against the four tests, because most
of it is not yet at the grain the tests measure.

**No ending shapes and no ledger rows are assigned.** L-015: stories are grown, not
outlined, and an honest TBD beats a guess that then constrains what the story could have
become. Haunt logic and period land early; ending type and physical band land late.

**No origins, no workings, no schedules.** L-016 and the beat index bar all three, and the
pull toward each is strong at premise stage — a premise is exactly where a mechanism gets
smuggled in. Where a candidate below drifts toward one it carries a bracketed flag rather
than being quietly repaired.

**Flags used.** `※` = seeded from a documented fact in `reference/period/`, per protocol
Part 2 rule 4 — a real object, custom or trade taken **without regard to whether it is
spooky**, which is the strongest decorrelator available. `[near: …]` = collides with a
spent event in invention-protocol Part 3, or with a modal event in `ai-tells.md` §F, and
is listed anyway with the collision named. `[flag: …]` = drifts toward something doctrine
bars, stated so it can be argued rather than discovered in draft.

## Two subjects that are Mark's call, not mine

**The region's actual wound.** `craft-principles.md` §4 asks what is under the ghost, and
in this setting the honest answers are slavery and its successor arrangements — tenancy,
the crop lien, convict leasing, who is buried where and whose graves the parish keeps. The
modal treatment is an avenging spirit of the wronged dead, and it fails twice over: it
explains the haunting (D-004) and it uses the history as atmosphere. It is in the burned
band below for that reason, not because the subject is off limits. The non-modal route is
the one `folklore-and-belief.md` §4 already prescribes for conjure — peripheral, social,
and in the present tense of the story: the lien, the account, the commissary, the coping
nobody limes. Several candidates below sit on it. Whether the book goes there, and how
far, is yours.

**Conjure and hoodoo.** `folklore-and-belief.md` §4's warning stands and is not restated
here. One candidate touches it (G7) and is marked ⚠.

## Step 0b — the reader's guesses, banned

Before generating: what does a reader predict from *ghost story, Louisiana, 1880s*?

1. **A plantation house, a belle, a wronged slave, and the past taking revenge.**
2. **Voodoo.** A queen, a doll, a curse bought and paid for.
3. **The swamp itself as the monster** — something in the water that eats people.

**Listed, not banned (L-022).** Naming the obvious is the whole anti-collapse mechanism;
burning it adds nothing and costs the candidates. The band below is written out in full and

## The modal band — the obvious ones, exhausted

Eighteen. This is what the distribution returns unprompted, which is the only thing wrong
with most of them. **Revised 2026-09-09 (L-022): these are available.** They start at a
disadvantage — a reader has met them — and three (X1, X2, X3) carry an argument for refusing
them that is about more than familiarity. The rest are premises that would have to be
*executed* past their own reputation, which is a real cost and not a prohibition.

Where a candidate further down is a near neighbour of one of these, it says so as a flag.

- **X1.** The antebellum plantation house, the belle in white on the gallery, the family curse working itself out across generations.
- **X2.** The avenging spirit of an enslaved person, punishing the descendants of the household. The one entry I would still argue against on grounds other than familiarity — it explains the haunting (D-004) and uses the history as atmosphere — but the argument is above, for Mark to overrule.
- **X3.** The voodoo queen, the gris-gris, the curse commissioned in the Quarter and delivered upriver.
- **X4.** The bride drowned on her wedding day, still in the dress, still in the water.
- **X5.** The séance that calls up something real, and the medium who turns out to be genuine after all.
- **X6.** The photographer whose plates come out with the dead in them. `ai-tells.md` §F's most-worn entry, and the hardest of these to make new.
- **X7.** The cursed heirloom — locket, ring, music box — that must be returned or destroyed.
- **X8.** The Confederate soldier at the crossroads, asking the way to a town that is gone.
- **X9.** The swamp witch in her cabin, who knows things and dispenses warnings.
- **X10.** The priest who loses his faith fighting it, and the exorcism that half works.
- **X11.** The alligator that took the child, and what comes back out of the water instead.
- **X12.** The Spanish moss legend — hair of a buried bride, growing ever since.

- **X14.** The fifolet told straight as legend, by name, with a lantern and a lost traveller and a moral.
- **X15.** The hanging tree, and the ground under it that will not grow.
- **X16.** The twin, the double, the man met on the road who is himself.
- **X17.** The yellow-fever ward, and the nurse who starts to see which patients are already gone.

Note that it would pay the book's first-person and dialogue debts at once, which is more than most of this list can say.

## Candidates

Fourteen axes. Protocol Part 2 rule 3 says quota by category and choose the categories by
*who is affected* rather than by mood; at premise grain the working equivalent is **which
ordinary system supplies the story its rules** — the trade, the sacrament, the season, the
paperwork. The category structure is what produces the variance, not the instruction to be
original. Every axis is a real institution of the place, and the ※ count is high on
purpose.

### B — Water, and the level it is at

- **B1.** ※ Drought, late summer, low water. A stock pond drops two feet and shows a fence line taking in ground the family does not own — posts of new cypress, nobody's work, and the line puts their house on the wrong side of it.
- **B2.** ※ High water. The family moves upstairs and lives on the second floor for eleven days with the flood in the parlour, poling in and out through a window, which is ordinary on the batture. Something else is living downstairs in the water. The water goes down before anybody has had to say so out loud.
- **B3.** ※ A borrow pit, dug for levee fill, that fills in the winter and never drains. The parish waters stock at it and washes in it. Everything taken out of it is heavier than it went in — a bucket, a shirt, a dog.
- **B4.** A man drains a wet field by hand with a coulee, a season's work and the best thing he ever did. The water leaves, and something that had been floating in it is walking on the ground he made.
- **B5.** ※ Standing versus running water, and everybody local knows which is which. A woman crosses the running water at the ford daily and has never once crossed the still water in the ditch behind her own house, and has never put to herself why she goes round. [flag: pricing the fear is the *ending* instrument (L-006, fired 12); as a premise it starts where a story should finish]
- **B6.** ※ A cistern in a drought. The water goes low, the taste changes, and the household drinks it anyway, because it is August and there is nothing else to drink. Nobody covers it. Nobody in 1890 knows to.

### C — The trades that ate the landscape

- **C1.** ※ Moss-picking: grey moss pulled down with a wire hook on a cane pole, hung on the fences for weeks to rot the outer bark off, then baled for mattress stuffing. Beaten out for the ticking, one bale gives up black fibre and hair, and the hair is somebody's, and the family finishes the mattress because it is sold.
- **C2.** ※ A pullboat crew cutting radial runs into the swamp, the cable dragging a hundred and fifty logs a day. Something comes up the cable with the logs and into the yard, and nobody stops the winch, because the crew is paid by the day and the winch is not theirs.
- **C3.** ※ A felling crew on springboards above the buttress, in water, among snakes, paid by the tree. The cut they make in one tree is closed by morning and the wedges are on the ground. Five men re-cut the same tree for three days and are paid three times for it, which is the best week they have had.
- **C4.** ※ Company town: commissary, scrip, mill housing, a doctor on the company's account. A man's account is settled in full, in coin, on a day he spent unconscious in the company hospital. [near: batch 03 #2, the dead tenant's account credited weekly]
- **C5.** ※ A dummy line laid into the cut on temporary ties, running twice a day. It stops for somebody where there is no reason to stop, and the fireman gets down and helps them up, and the crew does not discuss it, because they are paid by the run and a stop is a stop.
- **C6.** ※ Skidder work: the winch drags what it is told, and his job is to set the tongs. One morning the tongs are already set, on something, and the pull is easy.

### D — Debt, paper, and the ledger

- **D1.** ※ A crop lien — seed, cloth, a doctor's visit, all advanced against the coming crop. At settlement he owes more than he made, which is ordinary. What is not ordinary is the doctor's visit on the account: itemised down to the mileage, for a house that never sent for him.
- **D2.** ※ A tax notice arrives for forty acres the family does not own and never owned, in their name, correctly assessed, right section and township. Fighting it costs more than paying it. They pay, and it comes again, and the ground is theirs now in the only way that matters.
- **D3.** ※ A deed in the courthouse carries a second signature under the first, in the same ink. The clerk has seen it and is bored by it, and the sale is good.
- **D4.** ※ A family Bible flyleaf, births and deaths entered in one hand across forty years. A **birth** is entered that nobody can account for, with a name nobody chose, and within the month the household is using that name for the youngest child without anyone having decided to. [near: vignette C1, a ledger entry in the writer's own hand]
- **D5.** A store day-book in town lists a week of his purchases from a week he spent at his sister's, forty miles off. The goods are goods he would have bought. He pays for them.
- **D6.** ※ He inherits his father's store account, as sons do, and it is not closed by the death — it is closed by somebody paying it off, in full, in silver, and the storekeeper's only remark is that it was about time.

### E — Sickness, and what people believed instead

- **E1.** ※ 1878. Shotgun quarantine: armed men on the road for nine days, turning back everything that moves. The fever arrives in a house that nobody entered, and the district's mistake was that they were watching the road.
- **E2.** ※ Malaria, and the chills returning on a cycle the sufferer learns to predict. Everything a fevered person says is discounted by the household as a matter of course. On the fever nights there is somebody in the room helping with the basin; on the other nights the household says she managed alone, and she did.
- **E3.** ※ Nursing in shifts, weeks on end, two women and a sick child in one room. One of the shifts is being kept by neither of them, and the child is better on those nights, and neither woman will be the first to raise it.
- **E4.** ※ Calomel — mercury, dosed by the spoon by a country doctor who charges it to the lien. The level in the bottle goes down between visits, the child does not improve, and the mother has not given it.
- **E5.** ※ 1905, New Orleans: cover the cisterns, screen the windows, drain the standing water, and it works immediately, which must have been extraordinary to live through. One household does all three, correctly, and what was in the water is now in the house, and the story never joins the two facts. [flag: `medicine-and-disease.md` says spend the 1900 epistemic gap **once, quietly, never named** — using it here spends it for the whole book]
- **E6.** ※ Black vomit is the sign every adult in the parish can read. A man produces it in the middle of an ordinary week, in a district with no fever in it, and is well by Thursday. Nobody wants him in the store, and he still has to buy flour.

### F — Laying out, sitting up, and ground that will not take a grave

- **F1.** ※ A vigil: the body is never left alone, one to three days, in shifts, with coffee and talk and sometimes cards. Twice she comes in to relieve the watch and finds it not alone, and the person keeping it is somebody the family would have been glad to see, and who is not in the parish.
- **F2.** ※ Wet ground. A coffin buried in the ordinary way is lifted by the groundwater, comes up, and breaks — which is why the region builds copings. The parish reburies him higher and the second grave holds. What the family cannot get past is which way he was facing when he came up.
- **F3.** ※ All Saints', the whole parish out in the sun whitewashing and dressing the tombs. Their own tomb has been done before they arrive — limed, swept, flowers laid — which is a kindness, and stands open a hand's width, which is not. [near: `stories/02-georgie/event-options.md` C4 already uses the unclaimed-coping version of this day; one of the two has to go]
- **F4.** ※ The bell is tolled one stroke per year of the deceased's life, so the district knows who before anyone has told them. The sexton is a careful man, and he rings it correctly for somebody who has not died, and the count matches the narrator. [flag: a harbinger with an arithmetic is a rule (L-016); the count would have to be nobody's decision]
- **F5.** ※ Laying out is women's work: coins on the eyes, the jaw bound with a cloth until it sets, the body on a door across two chairs. She has done nine of them. On the tenth the jaw is already set, and set wrong, and she has to break it to close the mouth. [flag: D-003 rations the physical to late, once, and **vegetal** — this is the visceral register and would need arguing]
- **F6.** ※ The post-burial return: the household is put back in order and the family fed, which is the parish's actual custom. They come back from the grave and the sickroom has been put back the way it stood in the spring — two hours of furniture-moving that nobody did. [near: `ai-tells.md` §F, the ghost that tidies or arranges — our own batch 02 failure]

### G — The two apparatuses, and what people reach for instead of an explanation

- **G1.** ※ A novena — nine days of prayer for one named intention. It is being said in the front room, at the hours, by somebody, and every person in the household is accounted for elsewhere, and none of them will say what the intention is.
- **G2.** ※ Purgatory: the dead can be in transit, and a haunting inside that frame is a debt of prayer rather than a monster. The Masses were paid for out of the estate and the register shows them said. They were not said. The priest who did not say them is dead as well, and is owed the same thing.
- **G3.** ※ A devout Baptist household, where there is no purgatory, so no soul in transit, so no legitimate category for what is in the house. Their response is not fear but scandal: it is a disgrace, and the work of the story is keeping it off the road.
- **G4.** ※ The circuit preacher comes monthly and sleeps in whichever house will have him. Every visit he asks her to pass his regards to her father, who died in the spring. She lets it go the first time out of politeness.
- **G5.** ※ A mixed household — Catholic wife, Methodist husband, adjacent parishes, intermarried, which requires no invention at all. Two apparatuses under one roof disagree about what is in the house, the disagreement is the plot, and the story never adjudicates it.
- **G6.** ※ Holy water in the house, a blessed palm behind a picture, salt, a broom across the doorway — done briskly, in passing, by a busy woman who would deny all of it in company. She does everything correctly and it has no effect, and what breaks her is her own certainty that she must have got the order wrong.
- **G7.** ⚠ ※ A Catholic woman consults a practitioner in town, pays a fee, and gets a receipt, and the story stays entirely on the social surface of it: who recommended whom, what she will and will not admit to having done, and which of her neighbours turns out to have gone first. `folklore-and-belief.md` §4's three rules govern. Mark's call whether the book goes near it at all.

### H — Photography as a trade and as an object

- **H1.** ※ An itinerant tintype man working the parishes out of a wagon, a dollar a plate, twenty seconds an exposure. He is asked to photograph a dead child, which is ordinary work and often the only picture a family will ever have. It is the easiest sitting he has ever taken. They pay him and book him for Thursday, for the other one, and there is no other one yet. [flag: booking the next death gives it a schedule and a reach (L-016); the booking would have to be the family's own error]
- **H2.** ※ 1880s: gelatin dry plates arrive, exposures collapse, the portable darkroom becomes unnecessary, and twenty years of wet-collodion skill is worth nothing by Christmas. He cannot get a dry plate to come out at his own house, and can everywhere else, and his trade is finished anyway.
- **H3.** ※ Mumler, and the fact that everybody knows some of them cheat. A New Orleans operator who does cheat — double exposure, a stock of grieving-relative negatives, a real business with real overheads — delivers exactly what he sold, and it is not his negative. His problem is commercial: he has been paid for six and cannot repeat it.
- **H4.** ※ A photographer's day-book: date, sitter, format, fee, all in the assistant's hand. There is a sitting he does not remember, correct in every column — and the assistant remembers the man, and describes him, and describes the beard. (Plate V.)
- **H5.** ※ Postmortem work, unembarrassed and ordinary. The family commissions one and the plate is clean, and they want another, because that is not how he looked. The second is not either. They keep commissioning and the operator keeps taking the money, and the fifth one is how he looked.

### I — The household, the lamp, and the dark

- **I1.** A house up on brick piers, and the space underneath where the dogs sleep and the barrels go. A child plays under there and has a friend under there, which is nothing. What the mother cannot get past is that the child comes out clean.
- **I2.** ※ Matches fail in the damp, and in this climate they fail often, and a lamp left burning is how houses go. A woman goes through the house at night with a box that will not strike, and every room she reaches is already lit, and she puts each lamp out as she comes to it, because that is what you do.
- **I3.** ※ Cypress boards, brick piers, wallpaper that lifts at the seam in the damp. The seam is re-pasted every spring and lifts in the same place, and the wall behind it is warm. [near: `ai-tells.md` §F's cold spot, inverted; the seam is period-true and the warmth is doing modal work]
- **I4.** An old man is moved into the front room to die, and the household organises itself around him for a year, and then he gets better. Nothing about the arrangement changes: the room stays a sickroom, the shifts go on being kept, and what nobody will say is that there is a dying to be finished and it is not his.
- **I5.** ※ Water is carried in by hand and carried out by hand — a hip bath filled from the stove, a basin and ewer, a slop jar. What is carried out of the house has something in it that was never in the house.
- **I6.** ※ A house at two in the morning in 1890 is total darkness; nobody flicks anything on. He begins going through it at night without a lamp, confidently, and getting it right, and he was never able to do that in daylight.

### J — Distance, sound, and the road home

- **J1.** ※ Sound carries strangely over flat water, and a voice from a mile off can be perfectly clear, and this is verifiable. On one clear night a woman hears two men across the water decide something about her family, plainly and without malice, and she knows one of the voices, and has to go on saying good morning to him.
- **J2.** ※ A ferryman knows who crossed and when, which is why he is the obvious character; the tally-over-by-one version of him is spent. This one is asked by a passenger to hold the boat for somebody coming down the road, and holds it an hour and a half, and loses the afternoon's fares, and would do it again.
- **J3.** ※ The behaviour without the name: a light on still water that is a lantern until you go towards it. A man who followed one and got out cannot be trusted with a lantern now, and the district goes on asking him to carry one, because he is the one who knows that ground. [`folklore-and-belief.md` §6 — the phrase must never reach the prose, and the behaviour is spendable once in the book]
- **J4.** ※ Six miles is a long way at night. He walks it home and arrives, and the walk took four hours instead of two, and he can account for every one of the four.
- **J5.** ※ A pirogue sits how it sits, and a man knows his own boat. He poles home with nothing in it and it sits low the whole way, and he keeps poling, because the alternative is getting out. [near: batch 03 #3, the flat that sits lower at dusk]
- **J6.** ※ Distances are deceptive across flat ground, and everybody local knows it, and they are still wrong sometimes. A boy sent to the neighbour's comes back and says the house is further than it was, and is scolded for it, and his father walks it.

### K — After the storm

- **K1.** ※ 1893, Chênière Caminada: around two thousand dead, a whole community buried at once in ground that will not keep them, and the graves still going into the marsh. A family that came back to rebuild, whose difficulty is not grief but bookkeeping — which of the dead they buried are theirs. Nobody here is shocked by a corpse. They are shocked by a corpse behaving incorrectly.
- **K2.** ※ Bodies are recovered from trees and from other people's property. A man's yard has three in it. Two are claimed. The third is buried on his ground, and is now his, in the way ground is.
- **K3.** ※ 1856, Last Island: a resort island split in two in a night, a hotel, a dance, a steamer, at least a hundred and ninety-eight dead. A woman who survived by holding onto something for six hours, and who has to go on being asked about it politely at other people's tables for the rest of her life, and who has stopped correcting them about what it was.
- **K4.** ※ 1915: the second storm ends the rebuilt settlement for good and the place is now a name and some brick. A man keeps his store open in it, and takes money.
- **K5.** ※ The afternoon thunderstorm is a daily event in summer and it ends. One week it does not arrive at all, and the district is glad of the dry, and one field stays wet.

### L — Mourning as a material with a price

- **L1.** ※ Crape is chosen because it absorbs light and reflects nothing, and it is destroyed by rain and by damp, and this is a wet climate. Two years of first mourning for a husband, in a climate that eats the cloth, on money she does not have. The horror is economic for a long time before it is not.
- **L2.** ※ Mourning is publicly legible: a stranger's clothes tell you who died and how long ago. A woman appears in the district in half-mourning, grey and lavender, correct for a grandparent at nine months, and nobody has heard of any death, and the grandparent is alive.
- **L3.** ※ Hair jewellery — a lock of the dead woven into a brooch and worn daily, entirely ordinary. The hair in the brooch grows. She has it re-woven twice, by a professional in town, who charges her for the work and says nothing about it either time.
- **L4.** ※ Mirrors are covered at a death so the departing spirit does not see itself and be caught in the glass, and the household does it correctly and completely. At the end of mourning they uncover everything but one glass, and go on living around it, and the decision was made by one person in the house who will not discuss it. [`death-and-mourning.md` flags the mirror as its own trap — **once in the book**, and this would be it]
- **L5.** ※ The arithmetic of mourning: husband two years, parent or child one, sibling six months, cousin six weeks. A woman has been in black for eleven unbroken years by the ordinary bad luck of her relations, and the last one on the list is not dead yet. [flag: a list that predicts is a mechanism]
- **L6.** ※ A crape badge on the front door announces the death to the street. One appears on a door where nobody has died, and the household leaves it up, because taking it down is worse than the questions.

### M — Ground, boundary, and the paper that says who owns it

- **M1.** ※ A surveyor runs a line for a sale with chain and compass, and the line does not close. He runs it three times and it fails to close by the same amount each time. A section corner is a legal fact, and this one is not where the law puts it, and he has to sign something by Friday.
- **M2.** ※ After 1877 the central social fact of the setting is who owns the ground and whether the character owes on it. A man buys his place outright at last, the first in his family to own ground, and holds the deed in his hand — and something in the arrangement changed hands with it, and the story never says what.
- **M3.** ※ The batture: the ground between the levee and the river, which floods every year and which people live on anyway, and which nobody owns in a way that survives a flood. A family lives there on sufferance, and somebody comes to collect rent on it, correctly, with a receipt book.
- **M4.** A neighbour's fence has stood on the wrong side of the line for thirty years, and everybody in the parish knows it, and nobody has minded. The day it is put right is the day it starts.
- **M5.** ※ A tomb is property: bought, inherited, with room for a set number, and reused — the remains moved back into the caveau to make space for the next. There is room for one more and two old people in the household, and everyone has done that arithmetic and nobody has said it out loud.

### N — The moderns, if they earn it (D-002)

- **N1.** A woman clearing out a dead aunt's house finds the sickroom still made up — bed, basin, the chair drawn to the right side of it. The aunt died in a hospital in Baton Rouge four years ago. She puts the room back exactly as she found it before she leaves, which takes her a while, because she had photographed it.
- **N2.** An estate sale: a mourning brooch, a cabinet card with a studio name on the mount, a family Bible with the flyleaf entries, bought as one lot by somebody with no connection to any of it. What she has bought is not haunted. What she has bought is an obligation, and the story never states it.
- **N3.** ※ A cemetery-restoration volunteer resetting copings and re-liming tombs in a parish whose families went north in the Migration and never came back for All Saints'. She is doing the annual work of a whole parish by herself, and it is being received.
- **N4.** ※ A LIDAR survey of the Atchafalaya shows the pullboat runs still in the ground, a fan of channels radiating from one point, which is real and visible from the air today. Somebody's house is on the point.
- **N5.** ※ A pump operator on a drainage crew, keeping water off ground that sits below the water beside it — a shift job, forever, and she is good at it. Something comes up the pump, and her shift has four hours to run.

### O — Inversions of the collection's own habits (protocol Part 2, rule 6)

Not premises; turns. Compatible with most of the pool above.

- **O1.** Invert the concealment. Nothing is hidden and there is no discovery plot: the household absorbed it years ago, and the reader arrives after that, with nothing left to find out.
- **O2.** Invert the witness. The narrator is the person the district has decided is the problem, and is sorry for — and the district is right about her and wrong about why.
- **O3.** Invert the intrusion. The thing is not getting into the house; the house is being taken out from under the people, lawfully, on paper, and the impossible thing is on the side of the law.
- **O4.** Invert the register. Bright hot morning, full parish, noon, everybody watching, nobody prevented, nothing dark anywhere in it. (`ai-tells.md` §C — weather as mood ring, refused.)
- **O5.** Invert the appetite. A household that *wants* it, has done everything correctly, and is declined. The story is about being passed over.
- **O6.** Invert the delivery. First person, written as a document with an audience and a purpose — a deposition, a claim on a policy, a letter to a lawyer — which pays the first-person debt and the dialogue debt at once and puts a reason on the page for every omission.
