# Story 04 — Prose Samples 01: a vessel

Eight rendered moments. **Nothing is chosen** — these are candidates at the grain L-023
asks for (a rendered moment, not a plot fact), and they contradict each other about what
the trade is, what the boat is, and whether anything is wrong with her at all.

Read them for pull, not for correctness. The question L-015 wants answered is *which one
made you want to know what happened on either side of it.*

**The cargo question in `notes.md` §3.4 is untouched and still yours.** Not one of these
commits the boat to the slave trade. Two name a trade (ice; window glass and cover cargo),
six leave the cargo unstated, and none would have to change if you ruled that subject in or
out. That decision is not mine and it did not need making to write these.

## 1 — Landed weight

> **Rule (off-page):** It keeps whatever is given to it, exactly, and gives nothing back.

Ice went up the bayou in summer, and it was a trade that ran on loss. Terrebonne took eleven
tons out of the plant on Tchoupitoulas packed in cypress sawdust under a double tarpaulin,
and he expected to land seven, and Ducote at the landing paid him on what came off the boat
and not on what went into it. Everybody understood the arrangement. A third of it belonged
to the weather and the other two-thirds were the money.

Two days up. Terrebonne lost a man's hat and a length of sheet and nothing else.

The scale beam at the landing said eleven tons even. Ducote weighed it in eight draughts,
and then he sent his boy for the other beam and weighed four of them again, and then he
stood with his thumbs in his waistcoat and said he was not having it. His bill of lading said eleven and the load
said eleven, and one of those was a lie, and he did not know which, and he would not pay for
the pair of them.

So they took it back out into the middle and broke it over the side. Terrebonne worked the
pick and his brother worked the tongs, an hour and fifty minutes in the sun, until the beam
came down to seven and the trade was honest again. Ducote's boy entered it as seven tons
landed, which was true, and Terrebonne signed under the entry.

The pieces they put over floated a long way down and were still showing when the boat had
gone.

## 2 — Written aboard

> **Rule (off-page):** It does not lie, and it has no use for anyone who does.

A false manifest has to be written by a man who has not seen the hold. That is the whole
art. A man who has seen it puts down what he saw, and what he saw is the thing the paper
exists to prevent, so Roque did his work in the deckhouse from a list of prices and a book
of his own last three years' manifests, so as never to invent the same cargo twice. In four
years he had not once put his head down past the coamings. He was paid four dollars a sheet
and he was worth it.

For the run in March he put down forty barrels of lime, twelve casks, coffee in bags, and a
quantity of cordage.

They were boarded at the Head of Passes on a flat calm morning and the inspector did the
thing inspectors do, which is read the paper out loud and walk the hold and tick it. Lime.
Casks. Coffee. Cordage. Nine crates of window glass, entered in Roque's own hand, in the
place in the column where he would have entered them.

The glass had gone aboard at midnight, after the manifest, out of a dray that came down from
Rampart Street, and Roque had been asleep twenty feet above it in the deckhouse and had
never been told about it at all.

The inspector signed the sheet and cleared them and went back over the side.

Roque was the only man aboard who knew that anything had happened, and he could not tell one
of them, because to say what was wrong with the paper he would first have to say who wrote
it.

## 3 — Sweet

> **Rule (off-page):** It gives you what you need, and it is the one asking.

A lugger takes water and somebody stands at the pump, and on the *Mathilde* after the squall
in the pass that somebody was Alcide, because he was nineteen and the youngest man aboard
was the man at the pump. Bilge water is salt and it is old and it comes up the colour of
tea. He had been smelling it for two hours before he understood that he had been smelling
nothing.

He put his hand under the spout and then in his mouth, the way you check anything.

They were four miles inside the pass in a bay you could wade across at the edges, salt all
round them and salt under them, and what was coming out of her was sweet water, cold, with no
cypress in it and no iron.

Their own two casks had gone over the side in the squall along with the spare gaff, and
there was a day and a half of the run left, and the cook came aft at noon with a bucket in
each hand and asked Alcide where the good water was.

Alcide filled them.

They came in on the Thursday with the cask two-thirds down and every man aboard said it was
the best water he had had that summer, and Alcide agreed with them, and he was the one who
had drawn it.

## 4 — What they came in on

> **Rule (off-page):** It brings them in. It does not need her.

Odile was paid two dollars a run to sit up on the chenière with a smoked-glass lamp and put
it in the window that faced the water if the cutter was not in the bay. The channel comes up
crooked past the oyster reef, and a boat running in at night without lights runs on that
window or she does not run at all. It was the easiest two dollars on the coast and it was
the only part of the work that could not be done by a man who was asleep.

In October the cutter came in and anchored a half mile off the reef, plain, with her riding
light up, and stayed.

Odile sat all night in the dark with the lamp cold on the table beside her and did her job.

They came up the bayou a little after three. She heard the sweeps and then the water off the
bow, and she went out and stood on the shell and watched the *Bon Secours* come up the
crooked part of the channel at a walking pace, on the mark, past a Revenue cutter lying at
anchor with a man on deck, and nobody hailed anybody, and nothing happened at all.

In the morning Villere paid her the two dollars, and she asked him, while he was counting
it, whether they had had the light.

He said they had.

She asked him what colour it was, and he said white, the way it always is.

Her lamp is a smoked glass and it burns yellow, and she has never owned a white one, and she
did not ask him anything else. The lamp has not been in that window since, and they have
never once failed to come in.

## 5 — The mark on the piling

> **Rule (off-page):** It holds the water where it is, for as long as it likes.

They lay at the piling inside the bar with four tons in her and waited for the tide, because
loaded she drew three and a half feet and there was three over the bar, and in that pass the
water is as much wind as moon and a man waits on both. Ozeme cut a mark on the piling at the
waterline with his knife, which is what you do, so that you can see which way it is going
without standing there looking at it.

It went dark. The flood had an hour and a half to run.

At eleven Hypolite went and looked, and the water was at the mark. At two he went and
looked, and it was at the mark. The ebb came on by the watch and ran the whole of its six
hours and the water stood at the mark the entire time, and nothing else in that pass was
wrong — the grass went over, the mosquitoes came and went, a heron got up off the bar at
first light and went away down the coast.

They lightered her instead. Four tons out over the side onto planks laid in the marsh grass
above the wrack line, and her across the bar light at seven, and back on the Tuesday for the
rest of it, which cost them the Monday run and the hire of a second boat.

The mark was where Ozeme had cut it when they came back for the cargo, and the cargo was
dry.

## 6 — Payed and put back

> **Rule (off-page):** It comes in from the inside, and it is not finished.

They hauled her out on the beach at the low of the year to scrape and pay her bottom,
because at the low of the year there is no work and the water is down, and a bottom that is
not scraped is a bottom that is costing money. Worm gets into a hull from the outside as
something too small to see and eats inward, so a wormed plank shows as pinholes and a sound
plank shows as nothing, and Vasseur had been reading hulls for thirty years by putting his
eye along them in the flat light.

Her bottom was clean. No holes, no fouling worth the name, the tar sound between the seams.

He drove a pin into the third plank up from the keel to hang his line off, and it went in to
the head, the way a knife goes into a pear that has been cooked.

They cut the plank out. Two feet of it, and the inboard face came away in the shape of the
galleries, which were the thickness of a finger and lined all along like the inside of a
shell, and which went end to end of what they had cut and further both ways. The shells were
in there. Some of them were working. Vasseur turned the piece over twice in the light, and
there was no hole in the outer face of it anywhere, and no hole anywhere in her, and the sea
had never been into that wood.

He cut a new plank and set it in and payed the seams, and they had her back in the water on
the Sunday, because a boat out of the water is not earning.

## 7 — No bottom

> **Rule (off-page):** It is deeper where it is shallow, and only when asked.

Sosthene was the leadsman because his father had been, and in that bay it was the whole
safety of the boat: no lights, no chart worth carrying, four feet of water over mud and
reef, and every mark of it in his head. He hove the lead from the fore rigging and called it
back along the deck, and they went where he said.

He hove it at the reef edge, where it is a fathom and a quarter and has been all his life.

It ran off his hand and kept running. He had it two-blocks, ten fathom of line out in a bay
a man could stand up in, and the weight going down the whole time, easy, the way it goes
when there is nothing under it.

He hauled it in hand over hand and the line came up dry.

He coiled it and put it in the locker and took her the rest of the way in by the trees, which
is what a man does when his line is fouled, and he told them the line was fouled.

## 8 — The right hand

> **Rule (off-page):** It does the work whether or not you are the one doing it.

You can tell a man's trade off his hands here, and Pitre's said hauling: right palm worn
smooth and yellow across the base of the fingers, left palm a farmer's. Twenty-two years on
the main halyard and the sheet. The rope lived in the right hand, and that was simply what
the right hand was.

In June Pitre put that hand between the gunwale and a barrel and lost the use of it for the
season, so he hauled with the left, and he was slow, and they said things about it.

By August his left palm was tender and had begun to go the way a hand goes.

The right one went on wearing.

It was in a sling against his chest and it went on wearing — smooth and yellow and lower
every week across the base of the fingers, and by the end of the season there was a groove
in it a man could lay a match in, and there had not been a rope in it since June. He could
feel the hauling. Not in the arm, which was mending, and not in the sling: in the palm, in
the groove, six or eight feet of wet line going through it at the speed a halyard goes,
while he stood at the rail with his hand strapped up doing nothing.

Pitre lived another thirty years and never again let a man take his hand. He got a name in
that parish for being proud, and he let them have it, because the alternative was holding it
out to somebody and letting them find out what was in it.

## What varied, and what I refused

**Eight mechanisms, no two of them the same instrument:**

| # | The wrongness | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nothing was lost on a run that is paid for in losses | He destroys good cargo by hand to make the arithmetic honest |
| 2 | The forged paper is accurate about a thing the forger never saw | His own crime gags him |
| 3 | What comes out of the bilge is drinkable | He draws it for nine men and says nothing |
| 4 | They come in on a light she did not show | She stops showing it, and is still paid |
| 5 | The tide runs a full ebb without moving | A Monday's earnings and the hire of a second boat |
| 6 | The worm is inside wood the sea never entered | The plank goes back and the boat goes back in |
| 7 | Ten fathom in a bay you can stand up in, and a dry line | He reports a fouled line, which is true enough to say |
| 8 | The hand wears while it is in a sling | Thirty years of not shaking hands |

**Rationing L-006.** It is n=1, it has fired twelve times, and it is the most over-trusted
rule in the repo, so it is the ending of **one** of these eight (#8) and a half-turn of a
second (#4). The other six end on a commercial consequence, an ordinary procedure used for
a different problem, an image, or a man's own complicity — because *price the fear* is a
prescription, and prescriptions are available, never required.

**Three things I refused:**

1. **Nobody aboard has an opinion.** Not one of the eight has a man who has seen this
   before, knows a word for it, or tells the others what to do about it (L-016, L-008). A
   crew is the most natural mechanism-delivery system in the language, and the sea has a
   folklore sitting there ready to be quoted. Nothing here is quoted. The only speech in
   eight pieces is commercial: a merchant refusing a discrepancy, a cook asking where the
   water is, a man saying *white, the way it always is.*
2. **The boat is never the protagonist and never behaves.** She takes water, she draws three
   and a half feet, she is out of the water and not earning. Every wrongness happens to a
   person doing a job, in the terms of that job, and the vessel is only where the job is.
3. **No schedule anywhere.** The beat index bans the timetable outright, and a ship is a
   timetable — watches, bells, soundings, tides. Watch-keeping is texture in #3 and #5 and
   nothing attaches to it; #4 ends by *retiring* a ritual rather than instituting one.

**What the samples turned out to be about**, which was not planned: six of the eight are
about **credibility**. The wrongness is not dangerous — it is a thing that cannot be
reported. A merchant will not buy a load that weighs what it weighed; a leadsman cannot call
no bottom; a forger cannot complain that his forgery came true; a woman cannot ask twice.
That is the book's labour register arriving on its own, and it is also the answer to the
adventure problem in `notes.md` §3.8: nothing in these eight is in a hurry, because the
pressure is not pursuit, it is the account.
