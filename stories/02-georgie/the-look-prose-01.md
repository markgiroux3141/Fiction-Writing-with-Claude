# The Look — Twelve Renderings

Story 02. Scene supplied by Mark, 2026-09-10: *he has an image of it burned in from the act,
he has spent weeks avoiding the cellar, and this is the descent — bringing his eyes across to
it and finding out whether the thing matches what his head has been doing with it.*

Twelve versions. No plot, no mechanism, **no flags in the body**. They contradict each other
on purpose, particularly about whether he looks at all. Same placeholders: Ellis, Verna, the
cellar.

**This batch sits earlier in the story than `stairs-prose-01.md`** — before it speaks and
before it has a name. Nothing here calls it Georgie. That is a chronology decision as much as
a craft one and it is worth noticing while you read.

Notes at the end.

## 1. Which way

Six weeks of keeping the potatoes in the shed and a good third of them had gone. He turned
them with his boot and found the soft ones by the give, and set them out along the sill, and
there were nine. Nine is not a thing a man can go on paying. A cellar is the place for
potatoes and that is the whole of what a cellar is for.

What he had, when he thought about the cellar, was nothing to do with the act itself. It was
the setting down. He had come down the stairs in the dark and put the head on the boards
under the window and turned it to face the wall, and he could see his own hands doing it,
both of them, one on either side, the way you set down a full basin so as not to slop it.

Or he had turned it to face out.

He had been going back and forth on that for six weeks the way a man goes back and forth on
whether he shut a gate.

He went down at four in the afternoon with the door propped and Verna out at the line. He
came down slow and he brought his eyes across the floor from the bottom stair to the window,
the way you look for something you have dropped.

Then he went back up for the basket, and came down, and filled it, and worked in there
twenty minutes. He never did settle the question. The answer was in front of him the whole
time and he did not once look up from the floor.

## 2. The sleeve

Verna had asked about the preserves twice, and the second time she had not asked, she had
said it, and there is a difference. So on the Thursday he told her he would fetch the whole
lot up at once and be done, and she said that would be sensible and went on with what she had
in her hands, and did not look at him while she said it.

He was thirty-one years old and he had one clear picture out of that entire night and it was
his own sleeve. The cuff of it, seen from the inside, the way a man sees his own arm when he
is working. Not the field. Not the dark. Not the noise. A cuff with the button gone and a wet
place above it that had gone brown by morning, and he had burned the shirt in the yard the
first week, and the picture had not gone with it.

He went down on the Thursday.

It took about as long as it takes to set a jar in a basket. He came down the stairs and the
head was where it was, and he looked at it the way you look at a thing you have looked at
before, and then he was picking up preserves.

That was what he could not get past afterwards. He had given it six weeks and it had given
him back the length of one jar going into a basket, and he was up in his own kitchen with his
arms full before Verna had the kettle off the stove.

## 3. Better lit

He did not go down for six weeks and in that time it improved.

That is the wrong word and it is the only one there is. When he first came up out of there he
had almost nothing — the dark, his arms, and a sound he could not have made a mouth do. By
the third week he had the shape of the window in it. By the fifth he had the grain of the
boards and which way the head was set and a fold in the sacking that he was certain of, and
he had never seen one of those things, because he had gone down that night without a light.

Every week it came up a little clearer, the way a photograph does.

When he finally went down, in the afternoon, carrying a lamp he did not need, it was not the
head he had come to see.

He had come to see the fold in the sacking.

## 4. In the glass

He went down in the last week of October to put the storm sash up on the cellar window, which
he did in the last week of October every year, and which he had been telling himself since
September that he would do.

The window is at the far end at the height of the yard. To reach it you cross the floor.

He crossed it with a rule about where he was going to look and the rule held the whole way.
He got the sash up and the wedges in and his hands were steady enough for the wedges. Then he
was standing at the window with the daylight outside it and the cellar dark behind him, and
the glass had the whole room in it, and the head in the middle of the room.

In the glass it was about the size of a walnut. It was reversed, so it was on the wrong side,
and it was small, and it was going to be his picture from now on, because a man cannot unsee
a thing once he has seen it at that size.

He stood at the window a good while looking at it in the glass, because looking at it in the
glass was not looking at it.

## 5. To the inch

The frost came early and the last of the pears wanted putting down, and there is one place in
that house cold enough. So on a Tuesday in November he went.

He had been six weeks at the picture, the way a man is at a splinter. He knew where the head
sat and he knew the angle of it and he knew the near side was in shadow and the far side had
the window on it. He had been telling himself for six weeks that this was his own head doing
it to him, and that the actual thing, seen once, would put a stop to it.

He came down and it was as he had it. Not close. To the inch — the place, the angle, the
shadow on the near side, the light on the far.

He had carried it down in the dark. He had never once been in that cellar with it in
daylight.

He stood on the bottom stair and worked at that for a while. Then he put the pears where the
pears go and went up, and the picture came up with him unchanged, and he was never afterward
able to say which of the two of them had come first.

## 6. The lard

The girls had been told the stairs were rotten. That was the first week and it held, because
it is the kind of thing children take on trust and then repeat to each other with additions;
by October the youngest was telling people a man had gone through them.

Verna had been told nothing, and had stopped going down without being told, which is a
different thing and a worse one.

So Ellis did the fetching. Once a fortnight, in the middle of the afternoon, with the door
propped and the stairwell light behind him, and he had got it down to a way of doing it that
took under two minutes and never brought his eyes above the height of his own knees.

It held until the fifteenth, when he went down for the lard and the lard was not where the
lard goes.

He stood with his hand flat on the empty shelf and understood that he was going to have to
look round the room.

He gave it a while. Then he did it in one go, the way you take out a splinter, right to left
— shelf, wall, boards, window.

The lard was on the stair behind him, where he had set it down himself on the way in.

## 7. Weight

His arms remembered it and nothing else did.

He found that out with a sack of meal, of all things, in the second week — fifty pounds held
out in front of him coming up off the wagon, because the step is high — and something in his
forearms went on ahead of him and had the other weight instead, which is nothing like fifty
pounds, and which is the only weight he has ever carried that he could not afterward put a
figure to.

He had no picture at all. That is the part nobody would credit if he ever told it: he could
not have drawn one line of what was down there. He had a weight and a way of holding it, and
across six weeks the weight got easier, which he did not care for.

He went down in November to see whether looking at the head would put a picture to it and
take the weight off his arms.

It put a picture to it.

He would have kept the weight.

## 8. The hook

The hook on the cellar door had always been on the inside, because there is nothing in a
cellar that wants keeping in.

In September he moved it to the outside. He did it on a Sunday with the tools out and two
other jobs going, so that it would be one of three things and not one thing, and Verna came
out and looked at it and said nothing at the time.

She said something about it three weeks later, in the middle of something else. He had his
answer ready and had had it ready for three weeks, and it came out fast and it came out
complete, four sentences where the question had wanted one.

That is what put him down those stairs in the end. Not the pears and not the potatoes. It was
standing in his own kitchen listening to himself explain a door hook at length, and working
out that he was going to have to become a man who went down to his own cellar.

He went that night, which was a mistake, and he took the lamp, which was another.

He was down there a long time. Verna heard him come up. In the morning she asked him nothing
at all.

## 9. The quarter turn

I will say what the picture was, because everyone supposes they know and nobody does.

It was not his face. I had his face for about a day and then it went and it has never come
back, and I could pass his own brother in the road and be civil.

What I had was the moment I set it down and it settled. You put a thing on boards and it
moves — an inch, a quarter turn, whatever it wants — and then it is where it is going to be,
and you did not do that, the floor did. That quarter turn is what I had. I had it in the
daytime and I had it going to sleep and I had it in the middle of talking to men about
fencing, for six weeks, and every one of the six it went a little further round.

That is why I went down. Not to look at it. To see it stopped.

I went on a bright morning with the door open behind me and I looked at it, and I am not
going to put down what I saw, because I have never once been able to say whether it was the
thing or the picture, and after a while I gave up keeping them apart.

## 10. Somebody's dog

What he had was a dog barking a long way off.

That was the whole of it for the first fortnight. Pruitt's dog, or somebody's, going on the
way they do at nothing, off across the field, and himself standing still to let it finish. He
could not have told you what he was doing while he stood there. He knows he was doing
something, because a man does not stop in the middle of nothing.

The picture filled in from there and it filled in around the dog. By the end of the month he
had the fence line and the wet and the dark and the shape of his own hands, and through all
of it, still, a dog going on at nothing a field away.

He went down in October because the seed potatoes wanted sorting and it was that or lose
them. Two in the afternoon, door propped. He sorted them and he did not hurry and he did the
job properly.

He was three-quarters through when a dog started up somewhere out past the road.

He sat down on the floor where he was with the potatoes in his lap, and he did not look at
anything at all until it stopped.

## 11. The list

He went down on the second of November with a candle and a list, because Verna had written
him a list, and a man with a list in his hand is doing an errand.

He had prepared for the face. Six weeks of preparing for the face. What he had not prepared
for, and what a man could not, was that the cellar smelled of the cellar. Damp brick, the
potatoes, and the sour place under the shelf where something got spilled the first year and
never came out. It smelled the way it had smelled when he was twenty-four and had just dug
it, and it smelled that way with the head in it.

He worked down the list. He was reaching for the beans, which were the last thing on it, and
he took his look then, and he took it properly.

He had farmed twenty years. He had killed and hung and cut up more than he could put a number
to, and he knew what six weeks in a cold cellar does to a thing.

He put the beans in the basket. He went up and set the basket on the table and told Verna the
list was all there except the vinegar.

## 12. Fifteen minutes

Twice in October he got to the top of the stairs with the basket and came back and did
something else. The second time he cleaned the stove flue, which did not want cleaning, and
made a job of it that ran until dark.

On the ninth of November he went down.

He had the door open behind him and the afternoon coming in at it, and he came down at an
ordinary pace and did the whole of what he had come for. Six jars, the crock, the onions in
two trips. He was down there the best part of a quarter of an hour.

There is a way of using a room that keeps one part of it out. Everybody has done it — you can
sit a whole evening in a parlour and never once take in the corner with the sick man's chair
in it. Ellis did that for fifteen minutes in a cellar twelve feet across, with the head in it,
and he did it without deciding to, and he was up in the kitchen with the onions on the table
before he understood that he had come up knowing not one thing more than he had known going
down.

So he would have to go again. That was the entire result: six weeks getting ready to look,
and he had spent the looking on not looking, and the whole of it was still to do.

## What varied

**Seven things that are burned in, and not one of them is the act:**

- **The setting down** — and the decision inside it, which way it was turned. (1, 9)
- **His own sleeve**, from the inside, the way you see your own arm working. (2)
- **A dog barking a field away**, and himself standing still to let it finish. (10)
- **A weight**, and no picture whatever. (7)
- **Details he could not possibly have acquired**, because he went down without a light. (3)
- **The quarter turn the floor gave it**, which is still going round. (9)
- **Nothing yet** — the avoidance is the whole content and the memory never surfaces. (6, 8, 12)

**Six things the look does:**

- **It does not happen.** His eyes never leave the floor and he does not notice. (1)
- **It happens sideways**, in the window glass, reversed and the size of a walnut. (4)
- **It happens and takes no time at all**, and the brevity is the injury. (2)
- **It matches to the inch**, and he was never down there in daylight. (5)
- **It delivers one fact and no picture** — what twenty years of farming tells him six weeks
  ought to have done. (11)
- **He uses it up on not looking**, and has it all still to do. (12)

## Three things I refused, with the argument

1. **The act is never a sequence.** No version narrates it, and none supplies a why. A
   remembered act carries context and context is a motive's near neighbour (L-021), and it is
   the one thing in this story that explains itself by construction. Every piece gives one
   retained fragment and the fragments are deliberately the wrong ones — a cuff, a dog, a
   quarter turn — because that is what a man actually keeps.
2. **The comparison never resolves as a comparison.** *Worse than he remembered* and *not as
   bad* are both answers, and the second is a reveal. Where the look lands (2, 4, 5, 11) it
   delivers a fact, a size, or a piece of professional knowledge, and never a description.
3. **The head is not described anywhere in twelve pieces.** Same standing decision as the
   stairs batch, and it is holding up under more pressure here, because this is the scene
   built to demand it. Still reversible.

**The ordinary world does all the forcing.** Nine soft potatoes, an early frost, a storm sash
that goes up in October, a list, the lard in the wrong place, and a wife asking about a door
hook. Nothing pulls him down those stairs. Winter does.

**Not written, and available:** the version where somebody else goes down first and comes back
up perfectly ordinary; the version where he goes down at night on purpose so as not to see it
and it is a bright night; the version that is only the six weeks and never reaches the stairs
at all.
