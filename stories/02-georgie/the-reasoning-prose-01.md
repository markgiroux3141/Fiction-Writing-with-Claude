# The Reasoning — Twelve Renderings

Story 02. Scene supplied by Mark, 2026-09-11: *he loves his wife, and Georgie convinces him
to kill her. Bring the reader along and make them agree with the reasoning, rather than
taking the easy route of the protagonist going insane.*

Twelve versions. No plot, no mechanism, **no flags in the body**. Same placeholders: Ellis,
Verna, two daughters, a cellar. Chronologically this sits after `the-look-prose-01.md` and
after or alongside `stairs-prose-01.md`.

**In none of these does Georgie advocate.** Georgie does not mention her in eleven of the
twelve, and in the twelfth treats the thing as a piece of work and never touches whether.
The argument is Ellis's, it is entirely available to the reader without Georgie, and it is
sound. The reasons for that are at the end.

**In none of these is he unwell.** He eats, he sleeps, he does good work, and he is pleasant
company. The prose nowhere marks the reasoning as faulty.

## 1. The rail

He was three days on the cellar stair rail and it did not want three days.

He had told the girls in September that those stairs were rotten and it had been a lie when
he said it. In November he took the whole rail off and put a new one on in oak out of the
good pile, and plugged the screw holes, and dressed the two treads that had worn hollow in
the middle. When he was finished a woman could have come down them in the dark with a full
basket and both arms occupied.

Verna said it was about time. She said it pleasantly, going past.

He had the reasoning in order before he ever put a hand on that rail.

She was going to go down there. Not because she suspected anything — because it is a cellar,
and she is the woman of the house, and it is November. It would happen on an ordinary
afternoon while he was out at Pruitt's or in town, and he would come in at the back and she
would be standing at the bottom of those stairs, and he would have about as long to decide
what to do as it takes a man to cross his own kitchen.

He knew what he did with that much time. He had found that out in September.

So it was never a question of whether. It was a question of whether it happened in a doorway
in four seconds with his coat still on and his wife looking at him, or whether it happened
some way he had thought about and chosen, at an hour he had picked, with her not frightened.

He finished the rail on the Thursday. It was the best piece of work he did that year.

## 2. Forty-four

He got the tin down on a Sunday and went through the papers, which he did once a year, and
the once a year was January and this was November.

His father had gone at forty-four, in the field, between one row and the next. His
grandfather at forty-six, in a chair, in the afternoon. Ellis was thirty-nine, and he had a
wife of thirty-four who would in all likelihood see seventy, and he had a cellar.

That is the whole argument and it took him about a minute to have and six weeks to get out
from under.

She would find it. Not soon. But she would have thirty years in that house, and the thing
would still be down there in all of them, and there would be nobody to ask about it and
nobody to blame for it but a man in the ground who had been good to her for sixteen years.
She would have to decide by herself what to do. At sixty. With the girls married and their
husbands to think of.

He could not be there for it. That was the piece he could not get round, and he tried. A man
can do a great deal for his wife. He cannot do that one from where he would be.

He put the papers back in the tin and the tin back on the shelf, and then he sat in his own
kitchen a long while, being a man who had thought of something.

## 3. She had stopped asking

She had stopped asking.

That was the first of it, and he had taken it for tact. She asked twice about the preserves
in October and not once after, and there are a hundred reasons a woman stops asking her
husband about a thing.

Then in the last week of November she moved the hook back to the inside of the cellar door.
She did it herself, with his own screwdriver, while he was at Pruitt's, and she did not
mention it, and when he came in the tools were away and the supper was on the table.

That is a hard thing to hold. A woman does not take a hook off a door for no reason, and she
does not do it quietly.

He watched her for eleven days after that and she was exactly as she had always been. That
was the worst of it, because it meant she had not been working up to anything. She had
decided.

She was never going to tell. He knew that the way a man knows his own wife. She was going to
stay in that house and keep that door and go on being pleasant, and in a year she would be a
woman who had known for a year, and in five she would be something there is no word for, and
she would have got there by being loyal to him.

He had already done one thing to her in September, with her not in the room.

Whatever she was going to turn into, she was going to turn into it slowly, out of loyalty.
He could not have that.

## 4. The slope

He was doing the talking, the same as always.

He had come down after supper with the light to say one thing, which was that he had been at
Pruitt's and Pruitt's wife had asked after Verna twice inside an hour, and he wanted to hear
how it sounded out loud, and then go up.

That is not what happened. Talking has a slope in it. A man says a thing, and hears it, and
the hearing puts him a step further along than the saying did; and if there is nothing in
the room to stop him he will go the whole way down inside an hour, and the entire time it
will feel like thinking.

Georgie did not say one word about Verna. Georgie did not say one word.

Ellis went from Pruitt's wife to the winter, and from the winter to the cellar door, and
from the cellar door to what a woman does with thirty years, and he went in the plain order
with nothing skipped, and every step of it was sound.

There was one place he stopped. He would not afterward say where it was. He stopped, and he
waited, the way a man waits when he has put a thing into company that wants somebody to take
it up or put it down.

Nobody did.

He went on to the end of it. He was up in the kitchen before ten, and he slept.

## 5. The objections

He put it to Georgie plainly on the Sunday, because he wanted it argued with.

Georgie argued with it.

Not the way a priest would. Georgie went at it the way a man goes at a neighbour's plan to
sell off a piece of land — the ground, the season, the sister and what the sister was like,
who would be asking after her and when, whether the girls could be got out of the house and
for how long and on what pretext, and what a man says at the door on the day.

Ellis sat on the fourth stair and took the objections one at a time. There were fewer of
them than he had hoped for. He had an answer to every one, and some of the answers he had
had ready for a fortnight without knowing he had them ready, and by the end of the evening
the thing had been gone over the way you go over a piece of work the night before you start
it.

He had brought it down there to be argued with, and it had been.

Nobody had said a word about whether.

## 6. The order I had it in

I had it in this order, and I have never once found the place where it goes wrong.

She was going to know. Not by finding it — by living with me. You cannot keep a thing like
that off your own table for thirty years, and by November I could not keep it off it for a
week.

If she knew and stayed, she was in it, and every year she stayed she was further in.

If she knew and went, she was a woman who left her husband in the winter, and she would be
asked why for the rest of her life, by her sister and by the priest and by every woman in
that parish, and she cannot lie. She has never been able to. She goes red and she looks at
her hands.

If she told, they would hang me, and my girls would be the daughters of that, here, where
nothing is ever done with.

There is no fourth thing. I looked for a fourth thing for five weeks and I would take one
now.

So it came down to what was going to happen to her, and when, and by whose hand — and the
only one of those three I had any say in was the last.

I want it put down that I was not out of my mind. I was better in myself that November than
I had been since September. I ate. I slept the night through. I was easy with her and it was
not put on, and on the Tuesday she said to me that I seemed like myself again.

She was right. I have thought about that more than about anything else in this account.

## 7. Careful

In the second week of December she began being careful with him.

She was good at it. She had always been good at it — she had done two years of it with her
mother — and if he had not known what he knew he would never have caught it. He caught it in
how she came into a room he was in: slightly, from the side, with something in her hands to
be getting on with.

He watched her do it for nine days. She was kind to him nine days running and every day of
it cost her, and she did not let it show, and she was pleasant at supper, and once, going
past his chair, she put her hand on the back of his neck. He had to sit still for a while
after she went out.

There is no coming back from that and he worked at it for a fortnight. You cannot go to a
woman and tell her to stop being frightened of you, because the telling is the proof. You
cannot send her away, because then she is a woman whose husband put her out in December.
You cannot wait, because waiting is only more days of watching her do it.

What he arrived at, at the end of the fortnight, was that there was one thing still
available to him that would stop her being afraid of him.

## 8. The eighth

The ground goes hard about the middle of January in that country and holds till March.

Her sister came at Christmas and stayed nine days, and had done every year for eleven years.

The girls broke up from the school on the nineteenth.

He wrote none of that down. He did not need to. A man who farms carries a calendar in his
head the way he carries his own name, and Ellis had been carrying this one since the end of
November without once saying to himself what it was a calendar of.

That is how it is done, and he only understood it afterward. You never put the question to
yourself. You attend to the arrangements. You are a reasonable man thinking about weather
and timing and who will be in the house, and every hour you spend on the arrangements the
other thing settles a little further down and stops being a question, and when you next look
at it directly it has the standing of a fact you have known for some while.

He fixed on the eighth of December, which was a Tuesday.

He had never once decided to.

## 9. Sixteen years

He knew she would be at her sister's from about two on a Thursday. He knew she walked back
the field way when it was dry and the road way when it was not, and that on a wet Thursday
she came in at the front because of the boot scraper, and on a dry one she came round the
back and was in the kitchen before you heard her.

He knew which shoulder she slept on and how long after the lamp went out. He knew she did
not eat before ten and would tell you she had.

He had not gathered any of it. That is the part that took him a long time to sit with. Every
piece of it he had come by the ordinary way, across sixteen years, by being fond of her —
which is the whole of what being fond of somebody is, it is knowing where they are — and
none of it had ever been for anything. Now all of it was for something, and he had not had
to go and look up one single fact.

He was a long time on the stairs the night he understood that. Georgie said very little.
There was not much wanting saying.

A man who did not love his wife would have had to plan it.

## 10. Her cousin

She gave him the last of it herself, at supper, on a Wednesday, and she was talking about her
cousin.

The cousin's husband had gone off in the spring and left her the place and two boys, and the
parish had been good about it for a month and had not been since. Verna said what she said
about it every time it came up, which was that she would sooner be a widow.

She said it the way you say a thing you have said before. She was reaching for the bread.

Ellis said something back. He does not remember what. It will have been ordinary, because
she went on with her supper and asked him about the fence.

He had spent five weeks on the question of what to do about a woman who was going to find
out, and every answer he had turned up had been about him — hanging, or running, or being
left. What she had just handed him, not knowing she was handing him anything, was what she
would want. In her own voice. At her own table. With her hand out for the bread.

He knew it was not what she had meant. He knew that on the Wednesday and he knows it now.

It made no difference at all. Once a thing has been said in the room in her voice, there is
no getting it back out of the room.

## 11. Three doors

He tried three other things first, and he tried them properly.

He tried being rid of it. That is its own account and it does not belong here; it comes to
this, that the water was low the whole of that autumn, that there was a levee crew on the
near reach from September, and that a man cannot dig in his own yard in daylight in a parish
where everybody can see everybody.

He tried sending her. He raised her sister's twice at the end of October, and the second
time she stopped what she was doing and asked him what the matter was, and he had to stand
in his kitchen and say nothing was. That cost him more than the trip would have.

He tried going himself. He got as far as the price of the place, from Toussaint, who would
have paid it. Then he sat down with a pencil and worked out where he would be living in
March and who would be living here, and put the pencil down.

Three doors, and every one of them shut on ordinary grounds — the water, the season, a woman
who asks a straight question, and the plain fact that a farm cannot be walked away from in
November.

What is left when three doors have shut is not a choice, or it does not have the feel of
one. He never went looking for a fourth thing. The fourth thing was the room he found
himself standing in when the other three shut, and he did not have to reason his way into it
at all, which is what he would want said about it if there were anybody to say it to.

## 12. He decided against it

He decided against it on the fourth of December and it made no difference whatever.

That was the discovery. He had had the whole thing in order for a fortnight — the timing,
the arrangements, the reasons, every one of them sound — and on the fourth of December he
sat on the cellar stairs and went over it one last time and said no, and meant it, and went
up, and slept better than he had slept in a month.

What he had not understood is that a thing you have thought all the way through does not go
anywhere afterward. It is done. It is finished work. It sits where you put it and it is
available.

In January the youngest had the croup and Verna was up with her three nights running. On the
third night he came through the kitchen at two in the morning to put a kettle on, with his
wife's voice going low in the other room, and found he had the whole of it in his hands
again and had not gone to fetch it.

He put it back. He was to put it back a number of times.

## How the reader is brought along, since that was the ask

**Never argue the conclusion. Argue the schedule.** A reader handed *she should die* refuses
it in the same instant. A reader handed *not while her sister is here*, or *before the ground
goes hard*, or *by a decision rather than by a reflex*, is evaluating a timetable — and to
evaluate it they have to be holding the conclusion already. Pieces 1, 5, 8 and 9 argue
nothing but *when*, *how* and *by whose hand*. Piece 8 shows the technique working on Ellis
himself: he keeps a calendar for six weeks without admitting what it is a calendar of.

**The argument has to be about her, not about him.** *She will find out and I will hang* is a
coward, and nobody goes along with a coward. Every version here reasons about what happens to
Verna — thirty years alone with it (2), becoming an accessory out of loyalty (3), nine days
of being kind to a man she is afraid of (7), a kitchen doorway and four seconds (1). The
reader agrees because the concern is real. That is what makes it unbearable.

**Love is the instrument, not madness.** He is at his most lucid in every one of these. He
eats, he sleeps, he does the best joinery of his year, and he is pleasant company — and in
piece 6 his wife tells him he seems like himself again, and she is right. Piece 9 is the
whole thesis in one line: sixteen years of knowing where she is at any hour is what makes him
competent at this, and a man who did not love her would have had to plan it.

**Close the alternatives on ordinary grounds.** Piece 11 is L-019 run as reasoning rather than
as action — the water, the season, a straight question, a farm that cannot be left. Nothing
prevents him but the world, and the last option is not chosen, it is what is left standing.

**Never mark the reasoning as faulty.** No *he knew how it sounded*, no fever, no sleepless
nights, no narrator's cough. The one place a version admits the strangeness is piece 6, where
he says he was not out of his mind — and he is telling the truth, which is worse than a
denial.

## What Georgie does, and why so little

Georgie does not mention Verna in eleven of the twelve. The reasons are the doctrine, and
they are also just craft:

- **If Georgie makes the argument, the story has a mechanism** (L-016) — a thing with an
  appetite, a plan and a reach, which can be described in a line. And it has handed the worst
  fact in the story a motive (L-021), which `event-options.md` already ruled out.
- **Nobody agrees with a puppet.** The moment the reader can attribute the reasoning to the
  thing, they stop testing it, and the whole ask fails.
- **The two versions where Georgie is present are present as a shape, not a voice.** In 4 it
  is the slope of talking out loud with nothing in the room to stop you, and the horror is one
  pause where he waited to be contradicted. In 5 Georgie argues — about ground, season, the
  sister, the pretext — and never once about whether, which is the most persuasive thing
  anybody could do.

**This overrides `event-options.md` step 0b, guess 2** (*it talks him into another killing* —
banned as the escalation the premise most obviously offers). Your call and it stands, but the
ban is the reason for the handling above rather than a thing to be argued with: the modal
version of this scene is the one where the head does the talking, and none of these are it.
