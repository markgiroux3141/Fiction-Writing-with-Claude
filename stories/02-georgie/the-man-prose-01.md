# The Man — Twelve Renderings

Story 02. Asked by Mark, 2026-09-11: *what happened to the man Georgie was, and how he came
to be in the cellar — and how much of this do we explain?*

The answer to the second question is at the end of the file and it is a split rather than a
quantity. Twelve pieces. No flags in the body. Some contradict each other, particularly about
whether anybody comes looking.

**The dead man is Cleve Doak**, fifty-one, a place of thirty acres on the Sligo road over the
county line, out after a strayed mule at dusk on the ninth of September. He is the same man in
all twelve so the facts accumulate.

---

## 1. What they said about him

Cleve Doak was fifty-one and he had a place of about thirty acres on the Sligo road, over the
county line, which he had off his father and which was not enough and never had been. He
hired out in the fall. He was hired out that September, cutting for the Latimer crew, and it
was Latimer who first said out loud that he had not been seen.

Ellis got all of it standing in Toussaint's with a bag of nails, over about six weeks, off
four different people, without asking one question.

He hardly spoke. That is what everybody said, and they all said it the same way, as a thing
in his favour. He would sit out a whole sale and buy nothing and say nothing and go home, and
if you spoke to him he answered you and that was the end of it.

He shod his own horses and made a poor job of it.

He had a sister at Bethel, Ora, married to a man with a store. He had had a wife and she died
in ninety-one. There were no children, or there was one that did not live, and the two people
who told Ellis about it disagreed with each other and neither of them was uncertain.

He was owed eleven dollars by the Latimer crew and it was still owing.

Ellis took the whole of it in, over six weeks, and asked nothing, and none of it was any use
to him whatever, and he has it yet.

## 2. The mule

The mule came back on the ninth of October.

One of the Fenton boys brought it up the road on a lead and half the district saw it come,
because a strayed mule walking home three weeks later on the end of a rope is the best thing
that happens in a month. It was thin and it was sound and it had been somewhere with good
water. Four men had a view about where, and all four were confident.

What it settled, for the district, was Cleve Doak. He had gone out after that mule on a
Tuesday evening in September and the mule had come home and he had not, and after the ninth
of October nobody in that parish believed he was coming. The talk moved on to what he had
been thinking, going off after a mule at dark, at fifty-one, with the crew starting at six.

Ellis was at the fence when it went by. He was mending the fence and he had a mouthful of
staples, and he had to take them out to say what a person says.

The mule was sold in November to a man from Ripley for eight dollars, which was three dollars
short of what the Latimer crew still owed him.

## 3. The good cup

His sister came over from Bethel in the last week of October and called at eleven houses in
two days, and Ellis's was the eighth.

She sat in his kitchen. Verna gave her coffee in the good cup, and she took it and drank it
and did not once set it down, and she asked her questions in order off a sheet of paper
folded in four, because the sheriff's man at Sligo had asked her the same things and she had
worked out it goes faster written.

Had they seen a man of about so high on the road, on the Tuesday or the Wednesday, on foot,
after a mule.

They had not.

Would they ask their girls.

They would.

She was not distressed. She was a woman doing a piece of work that had to be done, doing it
well, and she thanked Verna for the coffee and said the cup was a nice one and asked where it
had come from, and Verna told her, and the two of them were a minute on that.

Ellis walked her out to the road, because that is what you do. At the gate she stopped and
said Cleve would not have gone off, and that she was aware the district had settled on it,
and that she meant to keep on a while yet.

He said that was the right way to go about it.

Then he came back in and finished his dinner. It had gone cold and he ate it.

## 4. Ten days

Nothing happened.

That is what he had not made himself ready for. He had spent the whole of September ready for
the other thing — a horse in the yard before light, a man at the door with his hat off, the
question — and then October came in fine and there was nothing.

No search. No notice at Toussaint's, and there is a board at Toussaint's and things do go up
on it. Nobody at Sligo took out a paper. There was talk for about ten days and it was not
much talk, and then the gin burned at Ripley, and after that there was the election.

A man of fifty-one went out of the world on the ninth of September and it cost the district
ten days of mild interest. What would have finished the interest sooner, if anybody had
thought to say it, is that he had nothing on him worth a nickel and everybody knew it.

Ellis thought about that a great deal more than he thought about the night. He had taken a
man nobody wanted. He had gone to enormous trouble over it — the hours, the state of his
hands, the shirt he burned in the yard, the thing in his cellar — and the world had taken the
whole of it in ten days and got on with the election.

He would have preferred the horse in the yard.

## 5. In the morning

Nobody ever decided to keep it.

That is the whole of it and it will satisfy nobody. He came in at about two in the morning by
the back, and he still had a good deal to do, and he was past the end of what a man has in
him. He put it on the boards in the cellar, because the cellar is where you put a thing you
are going to see to. Then he went up and washed to the elbow and lay down beside his wife.

He was going to see to it in the morning.

In the morning there was a crew on the levee. They had come Tuesday and he had known they
were coming. There were three men on the Fenton line all that day, and the water was low the
whole of that autumn, which everybody remarked on. So it was not the morning. It was going to
be the Friday, when the crew moved down, and on the Friday it rained, and a man does not
carry a thing over open ground in the rain and leave a track a child could follow.

There were a great many mornings. That is all that happened. Every one of them had something
ordinary in it, and by the middle of October the question had quietly stopped being *when*
and become a question he never once put to himself in words.

The first thing he ever said out loud down there was an apology for the delay.

## 6. The part that went right

The rest of it is the part that went right and there is not much to tell.

It took him from about eleven until two and most of that was walking. The place is in the
cane brake below the second field, where the ground has never been anything but wet and never
will be, and where nobody has run a plough since before the war because there is no sense in
it. He did not dig. You do not dig in a place like that. You find where the water is already
doing the work and you help it, and he had known that ground since he was nine years old and
had never had a use for it.

He went back twice in October to look. There is nothing to look at. There was nothing in
October and there is nothing now.

He can account for the whole of that part. He knows the hour he started and the hour he
finished. He knows what he carried and in what, and that he went back for the second load
because he had thought it out on the way down. He was tired, he was careful, and he made no
mistakes.

What he cannot account for is why he came back up to the house carrying anything at all.

## 7. The reason

There is a reason and it is a good one, and he has taken it out and looked at it a great many
times since.

The face is the part that gets a man named. Everything else is a size and a pair of boots. If
something comes up out of that brake in five years or in fifty, it comes up as a man of about
so high, and the parish has three men it could be and no way to choose between them. That is
not a small thing and it is not a mad thing. It is what any man would work out for himself
with three hours of dark left and a great deal to carry, and he only had to work it out once.

So it came up to the house, because the house was where he was going. And it went into the
cellar because he meant to burn it, and burning wants a fire, and a fire wants a morning when
nobody has any reason to be looking at your chimney.

The reason holds the whole way up to the cellar stairs and then it stops holding, because
there is nothing anywhere in it that says a man has to look at a thing before he puts it
down.

He looked at it. He has never known why he did that, and it is the only part of that night he
has ever gone back over and tried to change.

## 8. The Wednesday

He did everything on the Wednesday that he had meant to do on the Wednesday.

Up at five, which is his time. He took the wagon in and got the nails and the coal oil and
two pounds of coffee, stopped at Toussaint's, heard about the gin at Ripley, and was back
before noon. In the afternoon he and the girls got the pears in, the whole crop, which was a
job he had been putting off since the first week of the month.

He has gone over that day since, looking for the place in it where he was somebody else, and
it is not in there. He ate his dinner. He was short with the eldest about the ladder, and
then was sorry about it and told her so, and she said it was all right, and it was.

That evening Verna said it was the most he had got done in a month and that she was pleased
with him, and he sat out on the gallery afterward and there was nothing whatever the matter
with him.

The cellar door stayed shut all day. Nobody went near it, because nobody had any reason to.
That is the last day of his life about which that could be said.

## 9. The fence post

The law came on the eleventh of October in the person of a deputy called Sowell, who was
twenty-four and had been sent because there was nobody else to send.

He came up the lane at about ten in the morning and did not get down off his horse. He asked
whether they had seen a man on the road on the ninth or the tenth of September. He asked
whether they had lost any stock that month. He asked whether anybody had been by looking for
work.

Ellis stood in the yard with a bucket in each hand the whole time and set neither of them
down.

The deputy did not write anything. He had a book on him and never opened it. He said Doak's
sister was making a business of it, and that Doak had a sister-in-law at Ripley he had most
likely gone to, and that this was the ninth place he had been at that morning with four still
to go.

Then he turned the horse, and turned it back, and said one more thing, which was that if they
came across a hat they should hang it on the fence post where it could be seen from the road,
because that is how these generally turn up.

Ellis said they would.

He carried the two buckets into the barn and set them down. Then he came back out and looked
at the fence post for a while, which he had walked past four times a day for sixteen years.

## 10. All right

By November the district had Cleve Doak worked out and did not think much of him.

That was the thing Ellis had not seen coming. A man goes missing, and for two or three weeks
he is a man who is missing. Then he is a man who went off. And once he is a man who went off,
everybody who ever met him goes back through what they have of him and finds the parts that
fit.

He was odd. He had always been odd. He never had two words for anybody and there is a kind of
man that is. He had let his father's place go right down. He had not been near his sister
since the spring, whatever she says now. Somebody recalled that he had gone to Texas in
eighty-eight and come home again, and that got told twice more, and by the third telling he
had gone to Texas and come home twice.

Ellis heard the whole of it in Toussaint's on a Saturday, at the counter, with three other men
there, and one of them was Latimer, who was out eleven dollars and had a right to an opinion.

He said Doak had been all right.

That is all he said. He said it the way you say a mild thing, and it landed the way a mild
thing lands, and Latimer said well, maybe, and the talk went on to the state of the roads.

Walking out to the wagon, Ellis worked out that he was the only man in that store who knew
what he was talking about, and that he had just spent it on saying that a man had been all
right.

## 11. Saturday

It was February before he got the one that stayed.

He was at a sale over at Ripley, standing where you stand, and two men in front of him were
talking about a roof. One of them had had a roof to do in September and had only got it done
in January, badly, by his own account.

He said he had had Doak spoken for. Doak had said he would come on the Saturday, and Doak was
worth two men on a roof for all he was fifty, and then Doak went off, and he had been three
months finding anybody else, and that is the sort of thing that happens to a man.

Ellis stood and looked at a set of harness he had no use for until they had moved on.

He had gone out on a Tuesday evening after a mule with a Saturday in front of him. That is
what Ellis got, in February, at a sale, in the cold, with his hands in his pockets, and it is
the one that stayed.

## 12. Georgie

The name is the part he can give no account of at all, and he has tried, because it is the
part that would make him sound like a madman if it ever came out, and the rest of it would
only make him sound like what he is.

His name was Cleve. Cleve Doak. Ellis had it by the end of September out of Toussaint's, and
had known the man by sight for eleven years without once having had a conversation with him.

There is no George in it anywhere. There is no George in his own family and none in Verna's.
He has been through the whole district in his head, every George he ever knew of, and there
are four, and two of them are living and one was a horse.

He did not choose it. He was on the stairs in the second week and he said it, and it was not
strange while he was saying it. It got strange about an hour afterward, in the kitchen, with
the lamp lit.

He has never called it anything else since.

Nobody taught it to him and nobody has ever said it back.

## How much do we explain

**The man: completely. The killing: not at all. The logistics: in full. The keeping: never.**

Those are four different questions and the instinct is to give them one answer. They do not
take one.

**Explain the man, and be generous about it.** D-004 governs the haunting, not the biography,
and L-003 basis 3 settles the rest: *withholding a name is not withholding an explanation.*
Vagueness about Cleve Doak buys nothing and costs the reader the only thing that makes the
crime cost anything — that a person is gone. So: thirty acres he had off his father, eleven
dollars still owing from the Latimer crew, a sister with a sheet of paper folded in four, a
mule sold in November for eight dollars, and a roof he had said he would come and do on the
Saturday. He is worth more to this story as an inventory than as a mystery.

**The discipline that makes that free: every fact about him has to be unusable.** If he had
been a preacher, an auctioneer, a man who never shut up, a man with bad teeth — that is
provenance (L-016, basis 2) and the reader can answer *what was it* in a line off it. So the
one thing everybody says about Cleve Doak is that he hardly spoke, and nothing whatever
follows from it. Specify everything that cannot be used.

**Explain the killing not at all, and do not let him be sorry.** No version says why. L-021,
and `event-options.md`'s answer to it: *this story declines to let him be sorry.* Piece 8 is
where that gets tested — he is short with his eldest about a ladder and apologises for it, and
means it, and the story does not comment. He is capable of remorse. It is simply not available
for the other thing.

**Explain the logistics fully.** Piece 6 gives the hours, the ground, the two loads, and why
he did not dig, and the plainness of it is what buys the withholding everywhere else. A story
that is straight with the reader about the wet ground below the second field has earned the
right to say nothing about the cellar.

**Never explain the keeping.** *Why the head and not the rest* is the question that hands a
reader a one-line theory, so it is the one that stays shut. Piece 7 takes it as far as it can
honestly go — the face is the part that gets a man named, which is sound, and which any man
would arrive at once — and then shows the reason failing at the exact point it fails: nothing
in it says you have to look at a thing before you put it down. Piece 5 gives the true account,
which is that no decision was ever made. He was going to see to it in the morning, and there
were a great many mornings.

**And never explain the name.** L-016 already forbade provenance for it, and piece 12 is
built to make the absence load-bearing rather than coy: he goes looking, in his own head, and
the search is thorough and it comes up with four Georges, one of them a horse.

## Two things I would not do

- **Do not connect Doak's life to the haunting.** Not by trade, not by temperament, not by
  something he said once. The reader should finish knowing a great deal about the man and be
  unable to build a single bridge from any of it to what is in the cellar.
- **Do not stage a discovery of who he was.** No document, no letter found, no scene where
  Ellis learns the truth. Everything above arrives the way it would actually arrive — in a
  store, at a fence, at a sale, off men talking about a roof — and most of it arrives after it
  could have made any difference.

**Not written, and available:** the version where Ellis knew him properly and they had done
business; the version where the sister is still at it two years later and everybody is tired
of her; the version that is only the walk down to the brake and back, with nothing in it but
the work.
