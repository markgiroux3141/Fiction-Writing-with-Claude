## Fixed before generating

**POV:** the woman on the gallery, close third. Mark, 2026-09-08.

**Rule (off-page), the part the vignette already fixes:** it watches, it learns the
rule, it keeps the rule exactly, it goes on playing after the children have stopped,
and it comes in if it is called. **What it wants is deliberately still open** — that
clause gets fixed by whichever event is chosen, and then never appears in the prose
(L-008, D-004).

**Wound (D-004):** what it costs, not what it means. Filled once the event is chosen.

## Step 0b — the reader's three guesses, banned

1. It is a dead child of this house — a sibling who died, wanting to be counted or
   included. **Banned.** It is the modal reading and it is an explanation (D-004).
2. A child is taken, or swapped, and the count at the end is five-but-wrong.
   **Banned.** It is the guess the vignette's own last line invites.
3. She pulls the sheet off, and there is nothing under it / something under it.
   **Banned.** The reveal is the one move the whole book forbids.

Also banned outright as `ai-tells.md` §F modal events: a child's laughter with no
child, the photograph with an extra figure, footsteps stopping outside the door.

## ⚠ The one hard limit, flagged before it is broken

`craft-principles.md` §11: *"a child's suffering is never an engine for a scare on its
own. If a child is harmed in a story's past, that harm must be what the story is
actually about."* Mark's own suggestion — the children sicken, die, and reappear
outside in sheets — is child harm in the story's **present**, as the engine. Combined
with D-004 (nothing explains it) that is the exact configuration §11 refuses: harm with
no account of it.

**The reconciliation available**, and the reason several candidates below take the
illness route anyway: in 1878 a family losing three children in a fortnight is
period-normal (`reference/period/medicine-and-disease.md`), and no adult present has a
framework that could explain it — germ theory is not general until the 1880s. So *no
explanation* is the **historical condition**, not a withholding. The story is then about
a woman nursing children in shifts who cannot tell whether she is watching an epidemic
or a haunting, and who counts either way. The harm is what the story is about; the thing
is never accounted for. Both rules survive.

Mark's call whether that reconciliation is enough. It is his book and §11 is my
document, not his.

## The pool

Twenty-four candidates, seven categories chosen by **who is affected** (L-013), four
seeded from `reference/period/` without regard to whether the source was spooky.

### A — The children

**A1.** They sicken in the order it caught them. The Ledoux girl, whom it followed
twice and let get away, goes down last, and is the only one in the house who knows why
she was late. *(Mark's suggestion, given an order. The order is the game's.)*

**A2.** The sick children keep the rule in bed — slow, arms out to the sides — and when
a child lets her arms down they go back out. Nobody in the room does it.

**A3.** They stop screaming at it. By the third week they are polite to it, and they
save it a turn, and the politeness is worse than the screaming was.

**A4.** Being spared is the diagnosis. Whichever child it has not yet caught stays
well, so the household learns to read who is next from the game rather than from the
fever, and is never wrong.

**A5.** They bury one. The count of sheets in the linen press goes up by one.

### B — Her

**B1.** It teaches her the rule. It waits at the tree with its arms out and will not
move until she puts hers out, and it is patient, and it has all afternoon.

**B2.** She puts a sheet on herself, to show the children that it is only ever an adult
under a sheet. They scream at her properly. Afterward one of them asks which one she
was.

**B3.** She takes the game over to control it — sets the hours, sets who plays, calls
the turns — and finds she has not become the referee. She has been given a job.

**B4.** She can only see it while her hands are busy: the peas, the churn, the mending.
Stand up to look, and there are five.

### C — The household and other adults

**C1.** Hollis, who is not a man who plays, begins going slow across the field with his
arms out. He does not know he is doing it and is short with her for saying so.

**C2.** The doctor counts the children for his book, gets six, writes six, and puts the
charge on the crop lien. She cannot get the six changed to five without explaining why,
and explaining why is not available to her.
*(Seeded: `medicine-and-disease.md` — the doctor arrives hours late, charges, and the
charge goes on the ledger.)*

**C3.** The neighbour woman who lays out the body ties the jaw and folds the arms, and
comes back the next morning to ask, without much interest, why this family lays its
dead out with the arms straight out to the sides.
*(Seeded: `death-and-mourning.md` — no undertaker in a parish; a carpenter makes the box
and a neighbour woman lays out the body.)*

**C4.** No adult can be made to find it strange. Each one she tells supplies a
reasonable sixth child — a different child each time, a cousin, a hand's girl, someone
staying — and each is certain, and none of them agree.

### D — The game and its rules

**D1.** The rule changes, and the change is an improvement. What it teaches them is a
better game than the one they had, and they prefer it, and they will not go back.

**D2.** There is a part of the rules the children will not explain to her. Not because
it is a secret — because she is not a player, and it would not mean anything to her.

**D3.** It plays fair and she does not. It gives the turns, lets them get away, keeps
the count honestly. The only party breaking the rules all summer is her, and something
has started keeping score of that.

**D4.** The game acquires a way to be won. The children want to win it.

### E — The count and the arithmetic

**E1.** Twelve sheets in the press at the start of the summer. She counts them weekly.
The number is right every week, and there is still one out on the tree.
*(Seeded: `material-culture.md` — household linen as an inventory a woman is answerable
for, counted and valued.)*

**E2.** She begins writing the count down, and the flyleaf of the family Bible is where
a household's arithmetic goes, and there is room under the last death.
*(Seeded: `medicine-and-disease.md` — "a family Bible flyleaf with five births and three
deaths in it is not gothic; it is ordinary, and it is a document.")*

**E3.** She counts five at the table every night of the summer and is right every
night, and the counting takes longer each week.

**E4.** She stops being able to count her own children in one pass. She has to touch
them.

### F — Outside the house

**F1.** A household four miles off has the same game and the same rule, has had it
longer, is calm about it, and has adjusted the number of children it keeps.

**F2.** She finds out where it learned the game. It was not from her children.

**F3.** The game is not local to the house. She sees it played correctly, by children
she does not know, from the road, on the way back from the burying.

### G — Time

**G1.** It does not come back the next summer. The children ask after it. She keeps the
sheets out.

**G2.** It comes back the next summer, and the children who are left are too old for
the game, and it is prepared to wait.

**G3.** It has the whole summer and it uses all of it. Nothing accelerates: one turn a
day, correctly taken, until the household's calendar and the game's are the same
calendar.

## Step 0f — selection, resolved

**Mark chose, 2026-09-08. No `pick.py` draw needed.**

**Selected: A1, B4, C3.**

- **A1** — they sicken one by one.
- **B4** — it is not there when she looks for it. Mark: *"I like the idea of it being
  gone when she looks for it."*
- **C3** — the neighbour woman lays out the body and asks about the arms.

**Rejected wholesale: categories D, E, F, G — twelve candidates.** Mark: *"I think this
is getting too technical, the game doesn't matter, they're playing tag... If its a game
for kids the rules would be nonsense anyways, the whole point is just that there is a
strange entity out there with them, that we don't know until she counts."* And on F and
G: *"more of the same. Overthinking."* And the general finding: *"A horror story doesn't
need this level of internal consistency and plot coherency. This isn't a mystery novel
where you solve a mystery and in the end all the pieces come together."*

Logged as **L-016**. `craft-principles.md` §3 and §4 amended the same day; L-012's third
test and L-013's category quota both gained boundaries. The pool's own failure is
recorded in L-013: twenty-four candidates across seven categories, but only about four
distinct ideas, and the category structure hid that rather than revealing it.

**The count is one line, not a subplot.** *"Very simply... she sees six in the field and
counts 5 meaning one of them wasn't real... that's it."*

**The game is tag.** Not an invented game, no rules named, no correctness. The vignette's
*"because that was the rule"* survives only as throwaway texture and is never built on.

### The §11 ruling

Mark did not answer the child-harm flag directly; choosing A1 and C3 answers it. Children
sicken and at least one is laid out. I am treating the limit as **satisfied rather than
overruled** — §11 requires that the harm be what the story is actually about, and under
this spine it is the story rather than a decoration on it. Say so if that reads wrong.

### The spine, in Mark's words

> *"the woman notices a tall figure with the children, when they come in she notices it
> wasn't with the children, they get sick one by one, and it can even end mysteriously."*

Ending open, by his invitation.
