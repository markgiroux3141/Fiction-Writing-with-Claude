# Story 01 — Alternate endings, round 2

The written ending (`draft-01.md`) is intact and is candidate zero. This file is the
second pool, opened because Mark asked for a version that gives chills.

**No recommendation section, by rule (L-013).**

## Diagnosis of the standing ending

> *She took to carrying her dinner out to the middle of the yard in the good weather, out
> from under the tree, and standing there to eat it with her arms down at her sides. /
> Hollis called her in from the steps the first few times. / The sun does not come off
> that yard until five.*

**It is oblique, and obliquity is the one thing L-003 forbids.** The reader must recall
the sun from movement one, notice that Alma is standing where the figure stood, and infer
the resemblance. Three steps, none of them stated. Mark's own exemplar states the terrible
thing outright — *"in the end, he looked just like the creature."* Restraint became
withholding at the exact point the reader had earned payment.

**Second fault: it is inside her head, and it invites pity.** Pity and chills are
mutually exclusive. A last beat that asks the reader to feel sorry for Alma cannot
frighten them. The versions below that work hardest are the ones that ask the reader to
be **afraid of her** — which mostly means stepping outside her.

## The corniness problem, named

Mark's proposal — Alma spends her days walking round the tree with her arms out — fails
in three specific ways if written carelessly, and all three are avoidable:

1. **Naming the state.** *Mad, insane, lost her mind, never right again* — a label instead
   of a rendering. The story has not named an emotion in 4,000 words and must not start.
2. **The modal madwoman.** The raving widow is stock. The fix is that she is not raving.
   She is doing something **correctly**: going slow, arms out, because that was the rule.
   Competence is what makes it frightening; incoherence is what makes it corny.
3. **Pathos.** If the last image is a broken woman, the reader relaxes. If it is a tall
   figure in a yard going round a tree at the slow pace, they do not.

## The pool

### A — Alma takes the position (Mark's, variants)

**A1.** She keeps the game up in the afternoons, going round the fig at the slow pace with
her arms out. Nobody in the house calls it anything.
> *Somebody had to be it.*

**A2.** The same, and the last line is duration, flat, with a number in it.
> *She did that for nine years.*

**A3.** The same, restricted to the hours the children used to play.
> *She went out at two and she came in at five, which were the hours they had.*

**A4.** The same, and Hollis gives up. Prices it on him rather than on her.
> *By the spring he had stopped coming out to the steps.*

**A5.** She does it in winter too, when there is no reason to be outside at all.

### B — From outside her: the parish, and what it tells its children

**B1.** Final beat leaves Alma's head for the first time in the story and lands on a child
on another gallery, a few years on, watching something tall go round a tree at the slow
pace with its arms out. The reader is put exactly where Alma stood in movement one.

**B2.** The district's practical arrangement, with no explanation attached.
> *The children at the landing were told to keep off the Ivey place. They were not told
> why.*

**B3.** A child of the parish describes the tall one to another child, years later, and
the description is of a woman in a dress. Nobody listening knows who that is.
> *He said it wore a dress.*

**B4.** Mrs Rey — who laid out three children in that house and asked one question —
drives past the place years later and looks at the yard, and says nothing to anyone in
the cart.

### C — The next children

**C1.** Sam comes back grown, with children of his own, and they play in that yard.
Last beat is what he does about it, which is nothing.

**C2.** The Ledoux baby — the one that was not thriving, the reason Emmy stayed — grows
up and comes to the house to ask about her sister. Alma has to answer her in person, and
the letter is what she says again.

**C3.** A new family takes the place after the Iveys and their children invent the game
themselves, without being taught it.

### D — The record

**D1.** End on the letter. She wrote it, every word of it true, and she kept a copy, and
that copy is what survives of Emmy Ledoux.

**D2.** End on what the parish register does and does not have in it.

### E — The tree

**E1.** Present tense, plainly, and the reader is implicated because it is now.
> *The tree is still there. It still bears.*

**E2.** Hollis cuts it down in the end, and nothing whatever changes, and she goes on
going round the place where it was.

**E3.** The figs. Somebody eats them every year and nobody in the parish will.

### F — The direct statement (Mark's exemplar register)

**F1.** The plainest available version, with the resemblance said out loud rather than
inferred — the equivalent move to *"he looked just like the creature."*
> *By the end of it you could not have told her from the tall one, at that distance, in
> that light.*

**F2.** The same, delivered by a person rather than the narrator: Hollis, once, quietly,
to Mrs Rey or to his sister, saying what his wife looks like out there.

**F3.** The reader is told what she looked like from the gallery — from the exact spot she
sat shelling peas in the first sentence.

## Constraints any winner must clear

- **No explanation** (D-004). Nothing may tell the reader what it was or why. *"Somebody
  had to be it"* (A1) is the closest to the line: it implies a purpose. Permissible only
  as her unstated reason, never as a fact the story confirms.
- **No mechanism** (L-016). Nothing that makes the summer add up. The three who died and
  stayed dead are the guard.
- **Plain, short, flat, last** (L-017). The final sentence is the flattest in the story.
- **Not the accommodation shape** (L-006 / L-014). Rationed out; it has fired nine times
  in twelve.
- **No pity.** See above.
