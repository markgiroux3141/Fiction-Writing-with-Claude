# Revision Brief — "The Game"

A short literary-horror story set on a small Louisiana cane farm, late 19th c.
Current state: strong draft. Assessment below is from a blind read (no author
attribution, no context about how it was written).

**Do not rewrite the story. This is a targeted revision pass.** Most of the
draft is finished work. The notes below identify a specific set of problems and
leave everything else alone. When in doubt, change less.

This pass has two halves. §2 is almost entirely cuts. §3 is the one substantial
addition: a religious layer that the draft is currently missing and that, done
correctly, makes the story frightening in a way it presently is not. §3 is the
harder job. Read it before making any cuts, because two of the cuts interact
with it.

---

## 1. What is working — protect these

Do not alter, "improve," or clarify any of the following. If a proposed edit
weakens one of these, discard the edit.

- **The rule.** "Arms out, because that was the rule" is planted early as
  children's-game pedantry and pays off in the final line. The horror is that
  the tall one *complies* — it follows the Ledoux girl twice round and lets her
  get away, "which was correct." A monster that obeys the rules of tag is worse
  than one that doesn't.
- **The tall one is never explained.** No origin, no motive, no face, no lore,
  and after §3 is implemented, no theology either. Do not add a single sentence
  of explanation anywhere.
- **The dust.** The children raise dust to their knees; it does not.
- **The arms.** Nan's arms going back out to the sides twice, and Mrs Rey
  standing over her "a second longer than the flowers needed" and saying
  nothing. This motif becomes load-bearing twice over after §3. Do not touch
  the existing instances and do not add new ones.
- **Sam.** He plays out in the open, outside the ring of the tree's shade, and
  he is the one who lives, and the story says "nobody ever said why" and
  refuses to close the loop. Do not close the loop.
- **Emmy Ledoux.** The moral spine of the story: Alma keeps a child who isn't
  hers in a fever house, stops writing to the mother, turns the brother away
  with two true sentences. "The thought she had was that Emmy Ledoux was not
  hers, and she finished pinning up the sheet she was holding before she
  moved." Untouchable.
- **The final letter.** Every word true; the truth is a lie by omission.
- **The last four paragraphs**, from "He went back up the steps and sat down on
  the gallery" to the end. Hollis walked that yard for an hour with a lamp and
  a gun in August and now he sits down. Do not touch a word of this. See §3.6
  for the one thing that must remain true around it.
- **The miasma argument.** Period medicine as a plot mechanism: Alma has
  already spent her credibility screaming at nothing, so she must argue the
  fever theory she doesn't believe against three people who do, and lose.
- **The Mrs Rey / Delphine passage.** The most alive writing in the draft.
  See §5.

## 2. Cuts and pressure

### 2.1 Cut the sag in the middle (highest priority)

The sighting beats between Nan's death and Mattie's illness are deliberately
flattened, but there are too many of them and two are the same beat written
twice.

- **Cut the wash-pot sighting.** In the paragraph beginning "She saw it twice
  that week," the wash pot and the kitchen window are one beat doubled. Keep
  the onion and the knife; drop the wash pot. Rework the paragraph to a single
  sighting.
- **Cut the Friday sighting.** "She saw it on the Friday, and again on the
  Sunday" — reduce to the Sunday only. The wrung-dry shirt, the set fingers
  that have to be opened one at a time, and the stopped water are the whole
  reason that section exists. Everything before them is throat-clearing.

Net target: roughly 150–250 words out of the middle third. The §3 additions
will put some length back. That is acceptable, but the finished draft should
not be materially longer than the current one.

### 2.2 Delete the thesis statements

The story states its theme out loud in about three places. It has already shown
each of them. Cut, don't rephrase.

- **"She would rather it was out there. That was where she had got to by
  morning."** Cut. The bar across the door and the wash bench moved to the
  north end of the gallery already say it.
- **"Either there was a thing in the yard or there was not. If there was not,
  then the danger in that house was her."** Cut, or reduce to the first
  sentence alone. The reader has been running this calculation since the
  laying-out.
- Scan for a third. Likely candidate: any sentence in the Hollis-and-Ada
  material that explains why Alma refuses.

Rule of thumb: if a sentence tells the reader what the preceding scene meant,
it goes. This rule governs §3 absolutely.

### 2.3 Break the surface

Every sentence in the draft is load-bearing. Nothing is incidental. This
airlessness is the draft's most artificial quality — real fiction has slack in
it, and a story this controlled reads as engineered rather than observed.

Add **two or three details that go nowhere.** They must have no thematic job,
no echo later, no payoff. Candidates:

- A neighbour or a name mentioned once in passing and never again.
- A piece of business with the mule beyond the thrown shoe — something Hollis
  says about it that is only about the mule.
- Something Alma notices from the gallery that is simply a thing in the world:
  weather, a bird, the state of the road, a smell from somewhere else.
- A wrong turn in the burial logistics — a cost, an inconvenience, a person who
  arrives and is not useful.

These must be in the story's register (flat, concrete, unadorned) and must not
be spooky. If a reader could gloss one as foreshadowing, it has failed.

**Do not use the §3 material as this slack.** Everything religious in this
story is thematically loaded whether you intend it or not. The slack has to
come from somewhere genuinely inert.

### 2.4 Vary the syntax once or twice

The and-and-and parataxis never breaks, which makes the flatness read as a
setting rather than a voice. Two interventions, no more:

- **One long unspooling sentence** somewhere in the Wick section — the nine
  days, the false recovery, the figs picked in the dark. Let it run. Exhaustion
  is a plausible motive for the sentence.
- **One abrupt fragment** somewhere in the Emmy section, where the prose is
  currently at its most even.

Both should feel like the voice under pressure, not like a stylistic flourish.
Do not add more than two.

Also watch for the doubled-back construction, which recurs as a tic:
"she did not count it, and she thought about not counting it while she was
closing the door." One or two of these are good. There are more than two.
Reduce to the best.

## 3. The religious layer

### 3.1 Why the draft needs it

Three children die and nobody prays. There is no priest, no service, no
scripture, no heaven, and not one line of consolation across three burials.
Meanwhile the story is populated by Ledoux, Thibault, Rey, Bertrand and
Delphine, is set in a *parish*, and turns on a laying-out. This is Catholic
Louisiana with the Church deleted from it, and the deletion is currently the
most conspicuous artificial thing in the draft after the airlessness.

The reason to fix it is not accuracy. It is that the Church, handled correctly,
supplies three things the story is short of: a second kind of authority ranged
against Alma, a structural reason for her isolation, and — most importantly —
a rhyme with the thing in the yard.

### 3.2 The horror mechanism (read this twice)

**The story's monster is a rule-follower, and so is the Church.** That is the
whole idea. Everything else in §3 serves it.

The tall one is terrifying because it complies. It goes at the slow pace. It
keeps its arms out. It lets the quick one get away twice, which was correct. It
does not cheat. It is, in its way, devout.

Catholic practice as Alma encounters it is also a system of exact forms: the
words said in order, the gesture made correctly, the posture held, a thing done
wrong not counting. If the draft establishes that the parish runs on ritual
precision — and establishes it flatly, as ordinary life, without a syllable of
commentary — then two things happen that no amount of new incident could
achieve:

**(a) The arms become a desecration without anyone saying so.** A body is laid
out with its arms folded across the breast. The story already has Nan's arms go
straight out to the sides, twice, against Mrs Rey's hands. Straight out to the
sides is the shape of the cross. It is also the posture the priest takes at the
altar, and the posture a child takes playing blind tag. The draft has set this
up and never cashed it. **Plant the priest's extended-arms posture exactly once,
in passing, at a graveside or an altar, described as flatly as the doctor's
spoon.** Do not connect it to anything. Do not let any character notice. The
reader will make the join, and the join is the single coldest thing available
to this story.

**(b) Alma's final walk becomes a rite.** She cuts two holes in a sheet, does
it badly and refuses to correct it, goes out at the hour they had used, and
walks the tree at the slow pace with her arms held out, and waits to be got,
because that is the rule. That is already a liturgy: a vestment, an appointed
hour, a fixed circuit, a posture, a rule obeyed exactly. Nothing in that passage
needs changing. But if the reader has spent the story watching correct forms
performed over three dead children to no effect whatever, then the last scene
reads as Alma transferring her observance to the rite that is actually
answering. She is not raving. She is at worship. **This is the payoff of the
entire religious layer and it is achieved entirely by what comes before it. Do
not add one word to the final scene.**

### 3.3 What to add, in order of value

**1. The grace, and its absence.** The best and smallest addition in this
brief. Establish once, early — in the first section, before the tall one
appears — that Alma does some small ordinary devotional thing at the table:
grace said, or the children crossing themselves, or a bare phrase before eating.
One sentence, no emphasis, folded into a meal that is about something else. Then
let the meals after Nan's burial happen without it, and **never remark on it.**
No character notices. The narration does not notice. The gap is the whole
effect, and it is the story's own method turned on its most private subject.

**2. The priest, rendered as labour.** Handle him exactly as the draft handles
the doctor: a man with a schedule, a register, other people to get to. The
doctor looks at a throat with a spoon, leaves a paper of something, writes the
visit in his book to put against the crop, and goes on to the Thibaults, who
have two down. The priest should be that man. He has buried a great many
children this summer. He is neither fervent nor doubting; he is *behind*. Give
him a fee or a plot or a sexton or a distance to travel. One appearance, two at
most. He should be no warmer than the doctor and no colder.

**3. Alma as the wrong kind of person here.** Her mother's people were from
Alabama, which in this parish almost certainly means Protestant, married in.
Mrs Rey's "Is that a way in your people?" is already circling this without
knowing it. Give the fact one hard edge somewhere — a thing she does not do the
way the parish does it, noted once by someone without malice — and her isolation
stops being temperamental and becomes structural. Everyone has known she was
foreign to the place since long before the fever. This also makes her defeat in
the miasma argument (§1) considerably more plausible.

**4. The fourth voice.** Currently three people hold one theory: Hollis, Mrs
Rey, and the doctor all say it is the air. A priest arrives at the same
instruction from a completely incompatible direction — do not look for a cause,
this is not given to you to understand, submit. Two authorities who agree on
nothing except that Alma should stop. That closes the trap. He is not wrong
within his own frame, and the story must not suggest he is.

**5. The unburied and the unbaptised.** Emmy has no body, no requiem, no
consecrated ground, and no stone. In this parish in this decade that is a
specific and severe thing, and it does not need explaining to be felt. The
Ledoux baby, four weeks old and not thriving, sits in the draft as pure plot
machinery; the period terror around a child dying unbaptised would give that
thread a second floor at almost no cost. Use a light hand. One clause in a
letter would do it.

**6. The prohibition.** Optional, and strong if you take it. Let the priest
caution Alma once, generally and wearily, against what grief drives people in
the country to — the old women, the practices, the things done at crossroads and
trees. He is not talking about the tall one; he has no idea it exists. He is
categorising *her*, not it. Then she does it anyway, in the last scene, in a cut
sheet at two in the afternoon. A prohibition planted and fulfilled, at the cost
of three sentences.

### 3.4 Free gains — change nothing

Two things in the draft get worse (better) the moment the religious frame
exists, without a word being altered. Be aware of them so you don't disturb
them:

- **Mrs Rey's silence about the arms.** She has laid out better than a hundred
  bodies and never seen it, she asks Alma one careful question about it, and
  then she never raises it again and asks not to be sent for. Inside a Catholic
  frame, her not naming what the posture resembles becomes a refusal, not an
  absence. Leave every word of that exchange exactly as it stands.
- **The linen press.** It holds the sheets the children play under, the shroud
  Alma eventually cuts, and Emmy's boots. It is shut after Nan is buried and
  opened once at the end. It is already a sacristy. Do not point at it.

### 3.5 Method — how all of this must be written

- **Flat, concrete, and logistical.** Fees, distances, schedules, the state of
  the road, who has to be sent for and how long he takes. The story's register
  is inventory. Religion enters it as work, cost, and obligation, never as
  atmosphere.
- **No one theorises.** Not the priest, not Alma, not the narration. The instant
  any character discusses suffering, providence, or evil, the story deflates.
  There is no conversation about why God permits this. There is a man who has
  three more of these to do before Sunday.
- **No consolation lands.** If the priest offers the standard comforts, they
  should be reported the way the story reports the doctor's paper of something.
  He said the things he says. That is colder than his absence.

### 3.6 Hard limits

- **Nothing is blessed, censed, sprinkled, or exorcised**, and no one attempts
  it on the tree, the yard, the house, or the children. Any rite aimed at the
  tall one implies the tall one has a category, and a category is an
  explanation. This is the most important limit in the brief.
- **No deathbed prayer scene.** Three of them would be unendurable and the
  first would misrepresent what this story is.
- **No crisis of faith rendered as a crisis.** If Alma's faith goes, it goes the
  way the grace goes: silently, in a gap, unremarked.
- **No priest at the end.** He is not summoned, not present, and not mentioned
  in the final section.
- **Hollis's paralysis must survive the addition.** Once a priest exists in the
  world of the story, the last scene has to hold against the obvious question of
  why nobody sends for him. It must remain true, and be felt, that Hollis sits
  down on the gallery and does not send for anyone either. Do not answer the
  question. Do not raise it. Just make sure the passage still bears the weight —
  this is the one place the religious layer could break something that currently
  works, so check it last and check it hard.
- **Still no stone for Emmy Ledoux.**

## 4. Mechanical notes

- **"Mrs Rey"** is set British-style with no period. If the setting is
  American, this should be "Mrs." throughout. Decide and be consistent.
- The scene breaks are currently `---`. Fine for a working file; if this is
  going anywhere as a manuscript, use a standard section break.
- Check that the timeline of the deaths (Nan, Mattie, Wick, then Emmy on the
  second of September) holds against the days of the week named in the text.
  The story leans hard on named weekdays and the crop calendar, and an error
  there would be costly. **Note that §3 adds Sundays to this problem** — once
  the Church exists, every Sunday in the story acquires an obligation, and the
  draft currently has people doing farm and sickroom work on several of them.
  Either the dates absorb it or a line has to acknowledge it. This is a real
  continuity risk, not a pedantic one.

---

## 5. Do not "fix" the Delphine passage

Mrs Rey learned laying-out from "a woman called Delphine on the Bertrand
place," and the passage handles this with deliberate obliquity: no dialect, no
explanation, no editorialising. It also wanders — the hair, the three years of
watching from the door, the count that isn't kept and then is, the eleven in
the year of the water said in order. That wandering is exactly the slack §2.3
is asking for elsewhere. Leave it long. Leave it oblique. If anything, this
passage is the model for the tone of the whole revision, and specifically for
how §3 should sound.

## 7. Standing constraints

- No explanation of the tall one, in any form, at any point, including
  theological.
- The religious layer exists to supply pressure, rhyme, and isolation. It
  supplies no comfort to anyone at any point, and the story never says so.
- No stone for Emmy Ledoux.
- The story's power is in what it withholds. Every proposed addition should be
  interrogated for whether it withholds less.
