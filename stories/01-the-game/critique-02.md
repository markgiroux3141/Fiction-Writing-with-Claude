## Tier 1 — Act. Cheap, surgical, no risk to anything that works.

### 1a. "in the world" — the author leaning in
> *"the only thing in the world she fought him for"*

Reviewer: *"the 'in the world' is the author leaning in."* Correct and unarguable. It is
emphasis supplied by the narrator for a beat that is already the strongest in the
passage. **Cut three words.**

### 1b. The gloss on Sam's shoulder
> *"If there was not, then the danger in that house was her, and Hollis had got to that on
> the Saturday standing at the bottom of the steps with his hand on Sam's shoulder, and
> had been decent enough not to say it."*

Reviewer: *"you already gave the reader that with Sam's shoulder. Spelling it out is the
story checking that the reader kept up."*

**Partly right, and the fix is narrower than the note.** The reviewer wants the whole
proposition gone; that cannot go, because Mark asked twice for Alma's reasoning to be
visible and it is what makes her inaction credible. But the **back-reference** must go —
it explains a gesture the reader has already understood, which is precisely `ai-tells.md`
§E item 12: *any sentence after an impossible fact whose job is to make the fact hold up.*

Keep: *"Either there was a thing in the yard or there was not. If there was not, then the
danger in that house was her."* Cut the clause about Sam's shoulder.

## Tier 2 — The real one: follow Emmy through.

> *"You have one devastating sentence — 'the thought she had was that Emmy Ledoux was not
> hers'... That's the nastiest thing in the story. Alma keeps writing letters that keep a
> child in a fever house, and she notices that the thing prefers that child, and she keeps
> her anyway. The story touches this and then flinches."*

**This is the best note in the review and it is right.** As drafted, the letters run the
*exculpating* direction: Alma tries twice to send Emmy home and is refused both times, so
the child's presence is nobody's fault. That is a choice that protects her, and it wastes
the story's own worst thought.

**It is also the answer to the symmetry note** (Tier 3a below), because it is the one
place the story can be given asymmetric weight without restructuring anything.

Minimal version — roughly sixty words, no new scenes:

1. **She stops writing.** After the *not hers* thought, the letters end. One sentence
   noting the absence, unremarked. The reader watches her stop trying.
2. **She answers the mother's request immediately.** Currently *"Alma wrote back that she
   could manage it."* Make it same-day, before Hollis is home, before she has thought
   about it. The speed is the confession.
3. **The final letter.** The line *"every word of it was true"* stays — it is the best
   thing in the ending — but what she withholds should be more than the figure.

**Guard:** no emotion may be named, so *glad* and its family are unavailable. This has to
be carried by what she does and how fast she does it.

## Tier 3 — Real, but not a draft-02 edit.

### 3a. The architecture is too visible
> *"Three deaths, three laying-out scenes with a small variation each, a countdown of
> children that lands exactly on the number the game requires, section breaks at even
> intervals... Everything here is sanded to the same grain. No line is bad and no line is
> a risk."*

**This is L-014's rubric tell, diagnosed from outside by a reader who has never seen the
craft folder.** That independent confirmation is worth more than the note itself.

But *"break the symmetry somewhere on purpose"* is not a line edit, and a scheduled
asymmetry is the same failure Mark already killed once (L-014: a mandatory deviation is
still a rubric item). **The only honest way to take it is Tier 2** — let the Emmy thread
run long and lopsided because it deserves to, not because the story owes the reader a
lump.

### 3b. The setting declines the obligations it borrows
> *"There's a labor force in that field the narration never looks at once, and Mrs Rey,
> who lays out for most of the district, is drawn with conspicuous care not to specify
> anything... it's a question about whether the workflow chose this setting for its
> history or for its mood."*

**The most serious note in the review, and it is not fixable by a paragraph.** It also is
not story 01's problem alone: it will recur in every period story, so it needs a standing
decision from Mark rather than a patch. Proposed as **D-005** if he wants it.

There is, however, a **cheap fix that is also a genuine improvement**, independent of the
larger decision. The draft is internally inconsistent about scale: it implies a
plantation-sized operation (*"somebody's hand walking through"*, *"a girl up from the
landing"*) while Hollis personally does sixteen hours a day of manual labour and cannot
be spared to cut down one tree. **Those two things cannot both be true.** Making the
place explicitly small — a smallholder working his own cane, one hand at most — resolves
the contradiction, explains why he is never available, and removes the invisible
workforce rather than eliding it. Roughly a dozen words.

The wider question — whether the book engages Reconstruction-era labour or sets its
stories where that is not the subject — is Mark's, and belongs in `book-bible.md`.

## Declined, with reasons.

### The disappearance behind the fig
> *"That scene commits, and it commits in a narration that hasn't established itself as
> unreliable about physical events — so it either breaks the ambiguity or retroactively
> demands a reading the prose hasn't set up. I'd have made it smaller."*

**Recommend against.** The dual reading is a *by-product* of the period research, not the
story's contract. D-004 is explicit that the thing must be *"unexplainable enough to be
supernatural, and it has an effect"* — a fully deniable version is a psychological story,
and this is a ghost story collection. Mark also wrote this ending himself and it is the
beat that made the story work for him; shrinking it would undo four rounds. The reviewer
is describing a different and legitimate story, not a fault in this one.

### The McCarthy charge
> *"close enough that 'pastiche' is a fair charge, though I'd call it competent
> inhabitation rather than imitation."*

**Note, do not act.** The reviewer's own defence — the idea underneath is not
McCarthy's — is the right one, and the register was arrived at partly through Mark's
notes. It is a **collection-level** risk rather than a story-level one: if all twelve
stories sound like this, `craft-principles.md` §12 has a problem. Worth watching in story
02 by deliberately varying voice, not by revising story 01.
