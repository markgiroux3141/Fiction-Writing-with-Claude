This is a real story, and I'd be surprised if it didn't place in a decent horror venue.

The thing your workflow got right is subtraction. The prose has been starved on purpose and it works: "She shelled peas. She got through most of the pan." The details are load-bearing rather than decorative — the doctor writing the visit against the crop, ninety-five inside the shut house, the mule throwing a shoe so Hollis happens to be home for the second episode. Almost nothing is there for texture alone.

The conceit is genuinely good, not borrowed. Tag as a folk-horror engine, with the rule of arms out as the hinge, lets you do two things at once: the corpse posture becomes the first hard evidence, and the ending inverts the game so Alma takes the role of the one waiting to be got. That's a real structural idea, and the last line earns itself.

The dual reading is held with discipline for most of the length. Everything supernatural has a naturalistic twin — diphtheria in the parish, the sore throat and the smell and the false rally before death, a mother in shock. And the best beat in the piece isn't the ghost at all, it's Hollis at the bottom of the steps with his hand on Sam's shoulder, not looking at the tree. That's the moment the horror relocates from the yard into the house, and it's done in one gesture with no commentary.

What's still detectably machine-made:

The architecture is too visible. Three deaths, three laying-out scenes with a small variation each, a countdown of children that lands exactly on the number the game requires, section breaks at even intervals. It's elegant, and elegance of that kind reads as designed. Human stories have lumps — a passage the writer kept because they loved it even though it doesn't quite fit, a section that runs long. Everything here is sanded to the same grain. No line is bad and no line is a risk.

Two places state the thesis the rest of the story is careful not to state. "Either there was a thing in the yard or there was not. If there was not, then the danger in that house was her" — you already gave the reader that with Sam's shoulder. Spelling it out is the story checking that the reader kept up. Same with "the only thing in the world she fought him for": the "in the world" is the author leaning in.

The disappearance behind the fig is the seam. Up to that point everything is deniable. That scene commits, and it commits in a narration that hasn't established itself as unreliable about physical events — so it either breaks the ambiguity or retroactively demands a reading the prose hasn't set up. Some commitment was probably needed; I'd have made it smaller.

And the real missed opportunity: Emmy Ledoux. You have one devastating sentence — "the thought she had was that Emmy Ledoux was not hers, and she finished pinning up the sheet she was holding before she moved." That's the nastiest thing in the story. Alma keeps writing letters that keep a child in a fever house, and she notices that the thing prefers that child, and she keeps her anyway. The story touches this and then flinches, resolving it into a letter Alma writes truthfully and a secret she keeps. The horror you built is available and you left it on the table.

One other thing worth flagging, less craft than judgment. A Louisiana cane parish, a "landing," hired hands, sixteen hours in the cane — the setting is doing heavy atmospheric work, and the story declines every obligation that setting carries. There's a labor force in that field the narration never looks at once, and Mrs Rey, who lays out for most of the district, is drawn with conspicuous care not to specify anything. A reader who knows the period will feel the vacancy. That's not fixed by adding a paragraph; it's a question about whether the workflow chose this setting for its history or for its mood.

The voice is close to McCarthy's early novels — close enough that "pastiche" is a fair charge, though I'd call it competent inhabitation rather than imitation, since the idea underneath isn't his.

If you want a sharper version: cut the two thesis lines, break the symmetry somewhere on purpose, and follow Emmy through. The workflow is clearly working. What it's producing now is a very good student of the form. What it isn't yet doing is making a choice a careful writer would call a mistake and keep anyway.
