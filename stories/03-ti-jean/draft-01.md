## 1

Elmire had the loose brick out of the coping and the old mortar off it before eight.

It was the first Saturday in May and the ground was dry enough to kneel on. Her father
had laid that coping the year she was married, and he had put too much sand in the
mortar at the north corner, which she knew because she had carried the bucket for him
and watched him do it. She had been picking that corner out and setting it back every
few years since. She used less sand. Alcide said brick was brick, and Alcide had never
in his life had a trowel in his hand.

The ground ran up from the gate in six rows with the shell path up the middle, inside a
wall low enough to sit on, which people did. There were two crosses of any size, the
Thibodeaux one at the top and the iron one. Three plots down from her there was a marble
lamb over a Melançon child, and the lamb was the only thing in there that a child ever
asked to see, so it earned its keep.

Their own plot was in the north row. The plot on the far side of theirs had never had
anything done to it: no coping, nothing planted, no stone with a name. It had a figure
standing on it, on two courses of brick, about the height of a tall man. A woman, with
wings. The head was down and turned to one side and the hair was loose over the face, so
there was more hair than face. The wings were out full. The drapery came down to the
ground and gathered on the ground, and where the leg came through, the folds lay flat
against it. Grey where the rain took it and paler up under the wings.

Three families were said to own that plot. Elmire had heard all three names and had never
once heard any of the three say it.

Tante Azélie came in at the gate at about nine with Ti-Jean behind her carrying the
bucket, and with her own two grandchildren, who went straight over the wall. He had his
hat on. His aunt had made that hat out of a child's hat — got the band out of it, cut it
down, worked it round again, an evening's work she had never made anything of — and it was
a good hat, and everybody in the parish had said something nice about it at one time or
another. It was a good place to put your eyes and it was always there. His shoes had to be
sent for. His coat was a man's coat, because a coat does not have to fit.

He was nineteen that spring. His mother had been Léonide, Elmire's cousin, twenty-two: a
long birth and a bad one, and the doctor came out from town on the second morning and
found nothing to do that had not been done, and she lived four days after that. There was
a way of telling it in the family in which the child had done it to her. Nobody had ever
said it out loud where Elmire could hear. Everybody in that house had heard it. He went to
Azélie because Azélie had the room.

Two weeks before, in Hébert's, he had asked out loud for a peppermint, and Ovide had leaned
over the counter and asked his aunt whether the boy would want one. He was at her elbow
while it was said. Azélie said he would, and Ovide gave him the peppermint and would not
take anything for it, and that was the whole of it, and Elmire had thought about it twice
since.

The women worked their own plots and talked across the rows without stopping work, which is
how anything in that parish gets said. Azélie had a daughter down with something and a
sister-in-law who would not help and had said so plainly, which Azélie reported without
heat, the way you report weather. Elmire's own boy, Numa, was up at the top row with the
water can, doing very little with it. The lime came off the brush and dried on her wrists
and cracked when she turned her hand over.

She had the corner brick back in and was working the joint with her thumb when she heard
the bucket go down.

He was four steps off the row by then, going up the middle of the path at no great pace,
the way a child goes at a dog. Her hand was on his shoulder before she had thought about it
and she turned him and said come on now, Ti-Jean, come see the lamb, and he came. He was
easy, and he always came. He did that every time he was brought out there and every time
somebody took him off it, and it was never the same person twice, because it was whoever
happened to be nearest.

He did not look back at it. She noticed that and did nothing with it.

A child hauled off a dog looks back.

She put his hand on the lamb's ear, which is the part worn smooth, and he was pleased with
it and stood with his hand on it longer than she needed him to. Then she found she still
had hold of his shoulder, and had had for a while, and that her heart was going for no
reason she would have given. She got her hand off him. Numa called something down from the top row
and she answered it without hearing what it was.

The mortar had gone off in the pan while she stood there. She knocked it out and made it
again, which put her better than half an hour behind for the day.
