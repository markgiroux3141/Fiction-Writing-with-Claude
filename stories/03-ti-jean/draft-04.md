## 1

Elmire had one side of the coping scrubbed and was waiting on it to dry before eight.

It was the first Saturday in May and the ground was dry enough to kneel on. A coping wants
washing twice a year, and whitewash after that, and the shell inside it raked over. That
work was hers, and her mother's before her. The north corner was hers in a different way.
Her father had laid that coping the year she was married and had put too much sand in the
mortar at that corner. She knew because she had carried the bucket for him and watched him
do it, and she had been picking it out and setting it back every few years since. She used
less sand. Alcide said brick was brick. Alcide had never in his life had a trowel in his
hand.

The shell path went up from the gate through the middle of the ground to the back wall,
where it stopped. The rows ran off it on both sides and hers was the fifth on the north
side. The wall round the whole of it was low enough to sit on, which people did. There
were only two crosses of any size in that ground, the Thibodeaux one and the iron one, and
the rakes lean on those; the buckets go on the copings, a coping being flat and at the
right height. Three plots down from hers, a marble lamb over a Melançon child, which was
the only thing in there a child ever asked to see, so it earned its keep. The shell was
packed hard as far as the third row. Past that it was loose and went over your shoe.

The plot at the top of the path had never had anything done to it: no coping, nothing
planted, no stone with a name. It had a figure standing on it, on two courses of brick,
about the height of a tall man. A woman, with wings. The head was down and turned to one
side and the hair was loose over the face, so there was more hair than face. The wings
were out full. The drapery came down to the ground and gathered on the ground, and where
the leg came through, the folds lay flat against it. Grey where the rain took it, and
paler up under the wings. Elmire had come up that path most of her life and could not have
told anybody what the mouth was like.

Three families were said to own that plot. Elmire had heard all three names and had never
once heard any of the three say it.

She did the north face of her coping first in the mornings, because a north face dries
last and wants the start of the day. That put the path behind her.

Tante Azélie came in at the gate at about nine with her two grandchildren, who went
straight over the wall, and Ti-Jean behind her.

He was nineteen that spring, and he was simple. Azélie gave him the bucket and sent him to
the cistern by the gate, the long way round by the wall, and he went the long way round by
the wall.

His aunt had made his hat out of a child's hat — got the band out of it, cut it down,
worked it round again, an evening's work she had never made anything of — and it was a
good hat, and everybody in the parish had said something nice about it at one time or
another. It was a good place to put your eyes and it was always there. His shoes were a
child's shoes and had to be sent for. His coat was a man's coat, because a coat does not
have to fit.

He said good morning to Elmire on his way down to the cistern and she said it back to his
hands. There was whitewash on her own knuckles and she looked at that. Then she made
herself bring her eyes up and say it again properly, the way you would to anybody, and she
got it out, and it was worse with her eyes up, and she was back on his hands before the
sentence was done.

His mother had been Léonide, Elmire's cousin, twenty-two: a long birth and a bad one, and
the doctor came out from town on the second morning and found nothing to do that had not
been done, and she lived four days after that. There was a way of telling it in the family
in which the child had done it to her. Nobody had ever said it out loud where Elmire could
hear. Everybody in that house had heard it. He went to Azélie because Azélie had the room.

The women worked their own plots and talked across the rows without stopping work. Azélie
had a daughter down with something and reported it without heat, the way you report
weather. She told Elmire to get a man to do that corner. Elmire did not answer, and Azélie
had not expected her to. Her boy Numa was two rows down with the water can, doing very
little with it. He had a bird's egg in his pocket that he had been carrying about for
three days and had shown to everybody twice. The lime dried on Elmire's wrists and cracked
when she turned her hand over, and she would taste it at dinner.

She had the corner brick back in and was working the joint with her thumb when she heard
the bucket go down.

He was up out of the row and going up the middle of the path, going for the figure at the
top of it, at no great pace, the way a child goes at a dog. The shell went over his shoes.
Her hand was on his shoulder before she had thought about it, and she turned him and said
come on now, Ti-Jean, come see the lamb, and he came. He was easy, and he always came. He
did that every time he was brought out there, and somebody turned him every time, and it
was never the same person twice, because it was whoever happened to be nearest.

He did not look back at the figure.

A child hauled off a dog looks back.

She put his hand on the lamb's ear, which is the part worn smooth, and he was pleased with
it and kept his hand on it longer than she needed him to. Then she found she still had
hold of his shoulder, and had had for a while, and that her heart was going for no reason
she would have given. She got her hand off him. Numa called something up the row and she
answered it without hearing what it was.

The mortar had gone off in the pan while she stood there. She knocked it out and made it
again. That was half an hour gone.

## 2

Everybody going to the store came in at the front gate, up the path as far as the third
row, and out over the wall by the china tree, which took a quarter mile off the road.
Elmire had walked it that way since she was a girl and Numa walked it that way now.

Her own plot was further up than the third row, so to get to it she had to keep on up the
path past where everybody else turned off.

There was a stretch in there where she walked faster.

She knew where it began. She could have put her hand on the piece of shell it began at.
She had never once slowed down going through there and was not going to start, because
slowing down would have been a thing she had decided to do, and walking fast is not a
thing anybody decides. The footing was bad in there. She had a great deal to get through
in a day. Her mother had walked fast.

Nobody could tell you when the figure came, which is not the same thing as nobody knowing.
She asked sideways twice that summer, once at a wedding and once at a burial, and got the
same shape of answer both times: that it had been there when they were children, and then
a pause, and then the weather.

Madame Dugas was ninety-one and could give the price of hogs in any year you named. She
was on her gallery with a pan of butter beans in her lap and she began to say when she
believed it had gone up. Then she sat a while with her hands not moving in the pan, and
she never did finish the sentence. She said she did not know why she had started. She
asked after Elmire's boy.

Ovide Hébert kept the store, and he had taken the short way through to it his whole life,
and he went round by the road now. In between there had been a good while when he still
cut through and walked it faster than he used to, and if anybody had asked him he would
have said the shell was bad in there.

A drummer up from Franklin wanted the Guidry place and asked Elmire which of the boys
about the store was Guidry's, so he could send word out.

She said he would be the small one, and he would have a hat on.

She was most of the way home before it came to her that there was a thing she could have
said that would have told him at once, and that she had gone round it without slowing
down, the way you go round a hole in a road you have known about for years.

She had Numa out there with her on a Sunday in June, and she told him the angel was
watching over his grandmother, because that is what you say to a child in a burying
ground. He said it wasn't watching over her. He said it was watching at her.

Elmire let that go, the way you let a child's grammar go.

He was quiet the whole way down the shell path, and at the gate he wanted to know whether
his grandmother could see it back.

Ti-Jean called the figure maman. He called Father Ancelet's housekeeper maman and she was
seventy and she liked it. He called Elmire's mother maman. He called the mare maman, and
Elmire's brother thought that was the finest thing he had ever heard and used to get him
to do it in front of company. So it was nothing. It was a word Ti-Jean had, and he put it
on what he was fond of.

One afternoon in July Elmire was at her own plot cutting back the crape myrtle, which
wants doing before it goes woody, and she came to standing up with the shears open and
nothing in them. Her neck was stiff on the left side. It was stiff because she had her
head turned so the path stayed where she could see it without looking at it, and it had
been turned that way long enough to set.

She had been listening.

There was nothing on the path, and there had not been anybody in the ground but her since
she came in at the gate. She cut the crape myrtle too hard on that side and then had to
take the other side down to match it.

In August she dreamed about the figure, and there was nothing bad in the dream. She went
up the middle of the path at her own pace, not hurrying, on a bright morning with the
shell white and her hands empty. She put her hand on the face. It was easy.

She was glad.

She came to sitting straight up in the bed with her arm out in front of her and Alcide
saying what, what, what. She told him a dog. She had never in her life dreamed about a
dog.

## 3

Elmire went over to Azélie's on a Tuesday in September with a dish of rice and stayed to
do the ironing, because the daughter was still down and the day had got away from that
house.

Ti-Jean was out at the woodpile where they could see him from the window, at a job that
took him a long while and that nobody needed done that afternoon.

Elmire came at it sideways, over the iron, by talking about something else first for as
long as it took. She got to it eventually. The boy wanted watching out in that ground, she
said. There was the road on the one side of it and the water at the back of it, and he was
out there any time anybody had work to do, and a woman could be down at a coping with her
back turned for the best part of an hour.

Azélie agreed with her. She agreed with all of it, and then she went further than Elmire
had gone: he wanted watching in the road here, and in the yard, and at the stove; she had
four in the house and one of them down; and last winter he had walked to Hébert's on his
own after dark and been brought back by a man she did not know. She said it all in the
same voice, and she said Elmire was good to think of it.

Elmire ironed two aprons and the girl's nightgown, drank a cup of coffee she did not want,
and went home.

That week she told Numa to quit cutting through and go round by the road. He was nine and
he asked why, and she said because she said so, which worked. She did not mention it at
Azélie's.

They buried Sosthène Dupuis in the second week of October, in the second row, and
afterward Elmire got Father Ancelet by himself at the gate.

She asked him about the plot at the top of the path. Whose it was. Whether the church had
any claim on it. Whether there ought not to be a stone on it with something written on the
stone. She had those three ready and they came out well enough. Then she heard herself
starting the fourth one, and the fourth one began with the boy, and she stopped it.

What she would have had to say was that Ti-Jean Guidry ought to be kept out of the burying
ground on account of a statue. A sentence like that is at Hébert's by supper and in her
own kitchen by Sunday, and Alcide would have been decent about it, which was the worst of
the ways it could go.

Father Ancelet said the church had no claim on that plot and never had. He said there were
two families in the parish with nobody buried at all, and he would rather have their
business than a stone on a plot that was doing nobody any harm. He was kind about it. He
answered the question she had asked him.

Ti-Jean came up while they were still standing there. Father Ancelet turned and asked him
after his aunt's daughter, and looked at him while he asked it, and did not stop looking
at him until the answer was done. Straight at him. The way you look at anybody at all.

Elmire found that her back teeth were together and had been for some time. She was angry.
She turned it over the whole way home and could not find one person to be angry at.

She had never seen anybody put anything against the figure. Not a rake, not a coat, not a
bucket. There was no rule about it, and nobody had ever mentioned it to her, and she had
never mentioned it to anybody.

She took her rake up there on the Thursday and stood it against the plinth, the way she
would have stood it against a coping, to see whether she could. She did not once look above
the second course of brick.

She could. It stood there like a rake standing against anything. She had it back in her
hand before she had finished thinking about it, and she went down the row and did the
lamb, which did not want doing.

Anatole Broussard had had that ground thirty-one years. She asked him who had set the
figure, and she asked plainly, standing in the door of his shed, because there was no
sideways way to put it. He said nothing went into that ground that he did not dig the hole
for, and he had never dug that one. She asked him what was in the plot. He said there was
nothing in it. Nobody had ever been put there.

She did not ask him anything else.

Three days after that it had not rained, and the whole ground was drying. The copings were
going pale from the edges in, because the edges are thin.

The drapery was dark with water from the knee down, the way a hem goes dark, and where it
had always lain flat against the leg it was off the leg and hanging.

Stone does not hang.

Elmire had been up and down that path her whole life and she could not have said, standing
where she was standing, whether the top of it was ten steps off or forty. She got her
hands going again. She took the can down to the cistern and filled it, and it had been
full when she picked it up.

She said nothing about it to anybody. Not that day and not after.

## 4

By nine on the Saturday before All Saints' there were forty people in that ground with
buckets and brushes, and every one of them had their own dead to see to.

It was the first cool morning of the year and a good one, with the light off the shell
hard enough to squint at. By nine every monument in that ground was being used: rakes
against the crosses, a coat over the arm of the iron cross, somebody's bucket on the
Guidry coping and somebody's dinner set down beside it, and the two youngest Landry boys
sitting on their grandfather with their legs hanging. Elmire worked the far side of hers,
which put the path behind her, and she had been at it since seven and had the whitewash on
and drying.

There was a good deal of noise for a burying ground. Somebody's dog had come in with
somebody and was going up and down the rows being sent away from things. The Thibodeaux
men had brought a barrow of sand and were doing three copings at once and doing them fast,
because they had the church fence to see to as well. Children were being sent to the
cistern and coming back with the cans half empty from the swinging. Two rows over a woman
Elmire had known since they were girls was telling somebody at length about a hip.

Azélie was three rows down with her sister-in-law, who had come after all. Elmire had seen
Ti-Jean at about eight, at the gate end with the Landry boys, carrying a can for them with
both hands.

She straightened up at about ten to get the ache out of her back, and saw him go by on the
shell path, going up the middle of it at his own pace with his hat on.

She had the brush in her hand. Her arm kept going.

She had his name in her mouth. She had the breath up under it and her mouth open and the
next thing did not happen, and it did not happen for about as long as it takes to say a
name, and then her mouth was shut and her arm was still going.

His aunt was in there and Ovide was in there and Father Ancelet was in there, and the
granddaughter of hers who was eleven was in there somewhere with the cans.

She turned round and finished the coping. It took her the best part of an hour and she did
it properly, and afterward she could not have said one thing about doing it.

## 5

Ovide found Ti-Jean at about eleven and Théo Landry was there. Neither of those two men
ever said what they saw, then or after, and both of them were asked by people with more
right to ask than Elmire had.

She went over that evening and Ovide was in his own yard looking at his fence, which had
wanted a rail since spring, and he went on looking at it while she stood there. She would
not go until he gave her something about the boy. He gave her one thing.

They could not get his hand down.

She thanked him for it. Then she was in the road with the gate shut behind her, and she
went home the long way.

She never asked either of them another question about it.

The box was closed at the house and it stayed closed at the church. For a boy of that age,
dead out of doors on a clear morning, with no cart in it and no water and no machinery
anywhere about, that is a thing a parish notices. Nobody asked. His aunt said, to two or
three people, that he had never liked being looked at.

There were more people in that house than had ever been in it. They had chairs in from
three houses and the good ones went to the old women, and the coffee went round until
midnight and then went round again, and a child that nobody claimed slept the whole night
on a coat in the back room. Azélie sat where she was put and thanked everybody who came,
by name, and got the names right. Her sister-in-law did the kitchen and did not sit down
once, and the two of them did not speak all evening, and there was nothing in that either.

Somebody said, late on, that nobody had seen him go up. Two or three people agreed that
nobody had. Elmire had been up in the fifth row at her own coping the whole of that
morning, which was true, and she said so.

The sexton gave her the other thing, out on the steps, because it was the only thing he
had that was worth anything to anybody. He had seen the boy get there twice, years before,
on days when nobody was minding, and had come out of the shed and led him off himself.

He said the boy went up and set his hat on the plinth first, the way a man sets his hat
down coming into a house, and then went on.

His hat was on the plinth when they found him.

So he had not been in a hurry. He had gone up there and put his hat down, which takes a
moment, and which nobody does if there is anything at all the matter.

That was the part Elmire could not put anywhere. Not what had been wrong with him
afterward.

That he had been comfortable.

Azélie had no money to speak of, and the Guidry ground was full — there were nine in it
and the last two had gone in on top of somebody. The plot at the top of the path had never
cost anybody anything and it was there. The parish made it easy without one person
proposing it. The sexton dug where he was shown. Father Ancelet said what wanted saying
and said it well, and afterward stood at the gate for a long while with his hands behind
him. Two of the Thibodeaux men set a coping on it in the spring for the price of the
brick, and made a good job of it, and would not be paid for the work.

Nobody objected. To object you would have had to say why.

Elmire kept it after that. There was no coping on it the first winter and the shell would
not stay where it was put, so she carried shell up from the road in a bucket twice and

went into the basket out on the road, and she never cut anything with them. She did not
take Numa in there with her any more, and when he was old enough to go on his own he went
round by the road.

On All Saints' the next year the ground was full from seven. A small boy got away from a
group down at the second row and went up the middle of the path.

Elmire's hand was on his shoulder before she had thought about it. She turned him and said
come on now.
