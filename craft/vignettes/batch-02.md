# Vignette Batch 02

Five new levers, chosen to stress the rules rather than to repeat what worked. Every
lesson in `lessons-learned.md` came from batch 01, and eight of the eleven came from a
single paragraph — so this batch is the test of whether they generalise.

Scoring as before: *did anything land physically / which sentence did the work / which
sentence is a lie.*

**What each one is testing, beyond its own lever:**

| # | New lever | Rule under test |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Delayed recognition + the document | L-009 on a frame that wants withholding |
| 2 | **Dialogue** — untested in the whole project | L-011 (construction), style-guide § Dialogue |
| 3 | Lost time — **temporal register** | L-007 on a register never yet used |
| 4 | Wear, and a **non-accommodation ending** | Whether L-006 is a rule or a crutch |
| 5 | **Modern**, and horror at a remove | D-002, and L-010 with the whole story as approach |

## 1 — The day-book  *(C1: delayed recognition; the document as vector)*

> **Rule (off-page):** It wanted a portrait and it paid for one, like anybody else. The
> ledger is the only part of the transaction that stayed honest.

On Saturday nights Thibodaux did the week's book, which took an hour and put him in a
good humour, the book being the only part of the trade that behaved.

Eleven sittings. Two families, a christening, the Rey girl twice because the first
plate fogged. He carried the totals into the margin and came to a twelfth entry.

*Gentleman, seated, one plate, paid.*

He read it twice and admired it a little, because it was a good clean hand and it was
his own, down to the way he crossed a double t. Then he sat back.

There was a dollar in the drawer he did not know, an old one, thin at the edge and worn
smooth on the face. He put it in his waistcoat pocket instead of in the drawer.

He carried it there until he died, and had it on him in the coffin, because the woman
who laid him out went through his pockets and put everything back where she found it.

## 2 — The laundress  *(J1: speech; the witness who is not frightened)*

> **Rule (off-page):** It goes out at night wearing her. It has been doing it long
> enough that the road has begun to know her by it.

The Fontenot woman came for the wash on Monday and stayed in the yard talking, the way
she did, about her boy's arm and the price of lye.

Then she said, "I seen you out Tuesday. Late."

Alma said she had not been out Tuesday.

"On the road by the Meaux place. I said to my husband, that's Miss Alma, and he said
what would she be doing out at that hour, and I said I don't know, but that's her." She
shifted the basket onto her hip. "You want to be careful of that road at night, is all.
It ain't the walking. It's the ditch."

Alma said she would be careful.

The Fontenot woman went off down the lane with the basket. Alma stood in the yard for a
while. Then she went in and got out the shoes she kept for church and turned them over
and looked at the soles of them.

She kept those shoes in the top of the press after that, wrapped, where she would have
to stand on a chair to reach them.

## 3 — The wake  *(K1: lost time; the temporal register)*

> **Rule (off-page):** It takes three nights for the dead to finish leaving, and
> something attends to them. It would rather not be watched, and it is patient about
> arranging that.

They sat up with Emile in the front room in two-hour turns, and on the second night the
turn that came to her was two until four, which is the worst of them.

She had a lamp, a chair, and her sister's shawl. Somebody had to be in the room with
him. Nobody had ever said why, and she had not thought to ask while there was still
somebody to ask.

At some point the lamp wanted filling and she thought about getting up to see to it.

The next thing was the hall clock going four. Her neck was stiff against the chair
back, the lamp was out, and the window had gone grey.

Somebody had combed his hair.

It was parted on the right, which was not the side he parted it, and it had been done
wet, and there was no water in the room, and there had been no water in the room when
she sat down.

## 4 — The angel's feet  *(F2: wear; Plate IV; deliberately not an accommodation ending)*

> **Rule (off-page):** She stood for the sculptor and never came out of the pose. The
> stone was taken from her; she was not made from it. She can still move, but only
> slowly, and only when the light is not on her.

The Villere family wanted the monument photographed before the ivy took it, and wanted
it in the morning light, so Boudreaux was in the cemetery at half past six with the
camera and the dark tent.

The angel stood over the plot with its wings out. Good work, and expensive: the drapery
had a fall to it that most of them never got, and the face was finished, which was rare.
Most of them are blank, or near enough.

He set up, sighted, and went in under the cloth to see it upside down, which is where
you see a thing properly.

The leaves at the foot of the plinth were pressed flat. Not blown and not swept —
pressed, in two ovals, the way grass goes under something that has stood on it a long
while.

He made the exposure. He made a second because the first had felt short.

In the second plate the leaves are pressed about a foot to the left. That is the one
the family hung in the hall.

## 5 — The remote eye  *(J2: modern; horror at a remove)*

> **Rule (off-page):** It stands behind whoever is in that house, and it only carries
> at a remove — in glass, in a lens, in a picture. The person in the room never has it.

Her mother could not make the drive, so they did the house on the phone: Jess walking
the rooms with the camera turned around, her mother's face in the corner of the screen
saying keep, keep, that's nothing, keep the tureen.

They got through the kitchen and the front room. In the hall her mother said keep the
barometer, and then stopped talking.

"Mama."

"I'm here."

"You went quiet."

"I'm looking."

They did the two bedrooms. Her mother said keep about four things and nothing about the
rest, and twice more she stopped in the middle of a word. Each time her eyes went up and
slightly to the left of the screen, which is behind and above whoever is holding the
phone.

Jess said, "You want me to turn it round?"

"No."

They finished the house. On the drive back her mother rang again and said the connection
had been poor, that was all, and asked whether Jess had locked the door. She asked again
about ten minutes later.

After that she rang every evening at six.
