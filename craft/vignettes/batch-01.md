## 1 — The staircase  *(A1: presence with nothing shown; no impossible thing)*

The stairs came down into the hall at an angle that put the top of them behind you
before you reached the front door, which was a fault in the building and not in her.
She had gone through twice that morning with the wash water and thought nothing. The
third time she was carrying the mending basket on her hip, and halfway across she
knew that someone was standing on the landing with their hands at their sides.

She did not look. She had the sense that looking would be a kind of answer.

She went on to the door and unlatched it and set the basket out on the step for the
Boudreaux girl to collect, and she did all of it in the right order, and when she
came back through she went around by the pantry, which added eleven steps.

## 2 — The extra setting  *(B1: dread as arithmetic and decision)*

There were seven at Mrs. Ivey's that spring and she laid for seven, and had laid for
seven since the Pardue boy went home in February, and she did not have to count to do
it any more. It was the hands that counted, going around. So when she came back in
with the bread she saw the eighth place before she had any thought about it: fork,
knife, spoon, the plate squared to the table edge, the napkin folded the way she
folded them and nobody else in the parish did.

She stood there with the bread. The men would be down in about four minutes.

To take it up meant she had set it. To leave it meant she had not. She turned that
over a while and then she left it, and poured the water, and when they came in Mr.
Dell sat down in it without a word, because there was nothing wrong with it, and she
watched him eat off it for an hour.

## 3 — The handover  *(D1: touch; the impossible as courtesy)*

He lost the knife off the gunwale in about three feet of water, which was nothing,
except that the bottom there was silt and it would be gone in a minute. He went over
the side. The water came to his chest and was the temperature of a bath somebody had
got out of an hour ago, and he stood in it and put his arm down and swept it along
the bottom in the flat way you do, wrist loose, palm up.

He found the handle. He had it.

And then it was pressed into his hand, closed, and the fingers folded over his own to
be sure of it, the way you would give a man his change.

He got back into the skiff with the knife. He did not look at the water. He sat with
his knees drawn up and the knife in his fist and the boat turning slowly in the middle
of the pond, and he was still sitting like that when the light started to go.

## 4 — The game  *(I1: tonal inversion; daylight)*

It was the fourth day of the heat and the children had got into the linen press,
which she would deal with later. From the gallery she could see them going around the
fig tree — two under sheets, three not, and the ones under sheets went slow with their
arms out because that was the rule.

She shelled peas and watched a third sheet come around the far side of the tree.

It was taller. It went the same slow way with its arms out, and the children screamed
at it properly, and it followed the Ledoux girl twice around and let her get away,
which was correct, and she thought Hollis had come up from the field to play, though
Hollis was not a man who played.

She called them in at noon. Four children came, and then the fifth, pulling the sheet
off her head as she came up the steps, and the sixth stayed out by the fig tree with
its arms down at its sides, in the full sun, where she could see it from the kitchen
all through dinner.

## 5 — Trimming  *(G1: explicit vegetal physical, flat delivery)*

Sunday he did it at the window, because the light was better there than at the
washstand, with the scissors and the enamel bowl and a square of newspaper on his
knees.

It came in faster in the wet months. He took the outside first, the long threads that
had got down past the second button and gone pale at the ends the way a thing does
when it has run out of something. Those cut easy. Closer in it was tougher and had a
grain to it, so you went across rather than straight on, and there was a wetness at
the root end that came off on his fingers and smelled of the underside of a boat.

He worked up under the jaw by feel. Some of it was through the skin and some of it was
only against it, and after eleven years he could tell the difference in the dark, and
he did not pull on the ones that were through, because of what pulling on them did.

He folded the newspaper over and put it in the stove.

## 6 — The worn chair  *(F1: patience evidenced by wear)*

The house had been shut since the old woman went into her daughter's in 1889, and Rey
had the key and a list, and until the chair there was nothing on the list he had not
written a hundred times. Cypress press, poor. Six chairs, cane, four sound.

The seventh was in the north bedroom facing the window, and the cane of it was worn
through in the seat to a hole you could put a fist in, and the arms were worn down to
the pale wood, and the whole of it had the polish that comes off a person and cannot
be put on with anything.

He wrote it down as bad and stood looking at it.

The boards in front of the chair were worn too. Not scuffed the way boards go by a
door, where the traffic swings and spreads. Two patches, side by side, about a foot
apart, each of them the size and shape of the sole of a shoe, going down into the wood
a good sixteenth, and they had not moved in any direction at all.
