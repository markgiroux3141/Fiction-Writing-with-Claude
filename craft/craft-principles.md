# Craft Principles — Story-Level

How a ghost story is built. Prose-level rules live in `style-guide.md`; things never
to do live in `ai-tells.md`. This file is about architecture.

---

## 1. The mundane buys the impossible

A ghost story is a credit arrangement. Every page of specific, verifiable, boring
reality is deposited so that one impossible thing can be withdrawn. The reader must
believe in the drainage ditch, the tax assessment, the smell of a cold woodstove,
the way a screen door is hung — *before* they will believe in the figure standing
in the water.

Rule of thumb: the more impossible the event, the more ordinary the sentence that
delivers it.

## 2. One impossible thing

Pick a single violation of the world's rules and hold to it with absolute
discipline. Everything else in the story obeys physics. A story with a wrong
reflection *and* a talking crow *and* a room that wasn't there is not three times
as frightening; it is a cartoon. Fear needs a stable floor to crack.

## 3. The haunt has rules — and you never state them

The ghost must operate by a consistent internal logic that the reader can *almost*
infer. It appears in still water but not running water. It cannot cross the porch
line. It only takes what is offered. The reader should finish the story able to
half-articulate the rule, and be wrong in an interesting way.

Never let a character explain the rule aloud. The moment a haunting is
explicated, it becomes a mechanism, and mechanisms are not frightening — they are
solvable.

**Withheld is not the same as undecided.** This principle assumes you have written the
rule down somewhere the reader will never see it. A rule that exists and is concealed
produces prose thick with consequences that imply a cause; no rule at all produces
gestures that imply nothing, and the reader feels the difference immediately. The
symptom is the reader asking *what is actually happening?* - which is never a request
for exposition, but a report that the pieces do not add up.

State it in one sentence in the story's `notes.md`, under **Rule (off-page)**: what it
wants, and what it does. If you cannot, do not draft. See `lessons-learned.md` L-008.

## 4. The haunt must mean something

A ghost that is only a ghost is a jump scare with a longer runway. The apparition
should be the shape of something the living character will not look at directly:
grief, guilt, complicity, inheritance, a debt, a thing done to someone poor by
someone comfortable. The supernatural is the return of what was buried — literally
and otherwise.

Test: if you removed the ghost, would there still be a wound? If no, the story is
hollow.

## 5. Dread is structural, not adjectival

Dread comes from *arrangement*, not from words like "eerie" and "sinister." You
build it by:

- **Withholding.** The reader knows something the character does not, or the
  character knows something the reader does not. Never let both be equally informed
  for long.
- **Repetition with variation.** The same detail returns, slightly wrong each time.
  Three sightings of the same thing, each closer.
- **Delay.** Put a promise early (a locked door, a sound under the floor) and do not
  pay it for pages.
- **Constriction.** The character's available options should narrow, page by page.

## 6. Escalation: notice → intrude → implicate

A reliable three-beat spine:

1. **Notice.** Something is slightly wrong and can be explained away. The character
   explains it away. The reader does not.
2. **Intrude.** The wrongness enters the character's safe space and cannot be
   explained. The character begins to accommodate it — which is the true horror:
   people adapt.
3. **Implicate.** The character discovers they are not a witness but a participant.
   They were always part of it. The haunting was addressed to them.

The turn from witness to participant is where most ghost stories live or die.

## 7. Endings

The worst ending explains. The second worst resolves. Good endings do one of these:

- **Recontextualization.** A final detail makes the reader re-read the whole story
  in their head. Not a twist — a *key*. It should have been visible all along.
- **The door left open.** The threat is not defeated, merely postponed, and the
  character now lives inside the arrangement.
- **The quiet horror of continuance.** Life goes on, altered. The last image is
  domestic and wrong.

End on an image or an action, not a summary and not a moral. Cut the last paragraph
you wrote; check whether the story is better. It usually is.

### How continuance is executed: price the fear

The three shapes above name endings without saying how to build one. The mechanism for
the second and third is **cost**. See `lessons-learned.md` L-006 for the full rule.

Give the character a permanent, quantified, trivial inconvenience that she now pays to
avoid the thing — and never state what she is avoiding. *"She used the kitchen door for
the rest of the summer, which added eleven steps each way."* The fear is never
described; the reader sizes it from the price, and sizes it larger than any description
would have.

Keep the price **small**. Abandoning the house is proportionate to a fright and
therefore tells the reader nothing. Eleven steps a day forever means she has neither
fought it nor fled — she has made an arrangement with it, which means it is a permanent
condition of the place and she knows it.

Withdraw interiority for the last beat. Once the character has stopped having a crisis
about the haunting, the prose should stop having one too.

## 8. Point of view

First person and close third both work. Pick the one where the character's *not
looking at something* is most productive. The narrator should be reliable about
facts and unreliable about meaning — they report accurately and interpret wrongly.
The gap between those is where the reader lives.

## 9. Length and shape

Target 2,500–5,000 words for the core stories, with room for a few 1,000-word
short pieces used as interstitials between sections. A ghost story has one movement.
If it needs two, it is a novella; cut it back or split it.

## 10. Period and register

The book is **mostly period, some modern**. Its imaginative center is c. 1850–1920,
matching the wet-plate photographs, and most stories sit there. One or two are set
in the present day and inherit a nineteenth-century object, house, or photograph —
which gets both registers at once and keeps the collection from reading as pastiche.

A modern story must earn the modern setting: it should be frightening in a way a
period story could not be. Cell reception, digitised records, a house sold at
auction, an image that can be enlarged.

## 11. The physical is permitted — and it is expensive

This book is willing to get physical. Body horror is a legitimate instrument here,
not a last resort. But it is expensive, and the cost is paid in credibility: every
explicit image spends some of the reader's willingness to believe the next one.

How to spend well:

- **Precision over volume.** One exactly-observed wrongness beats a paragraph of
  damage. The wrong number of knuckles. Hair rooting from the wrong side of the
  scalp. Something that has been in water long enough to have gone soft in a way
  that is specific and correct.
- **Late and once.** Place the explicit image after the reader has been made to
  wait for it. A story that opens physical has nowhere to go.
- **Keep the prose calm.** The sentence delivering the worst thing should be the
  flattest sentence in the story. Horror rises out of the gap between what is being
  described and how evenly it is described.
- **Vegetal before visceral.** The plates point at roots, fibre, waterlogged tissue,
  growth through the body rather than injury to it. That register is this book's
  own; blood and wounds are generic. When in doubt, make it grow.
- **The body as evidence, not spectacle.** The physical detail should tell the
  reader something true about the haunting that no one in the story will say aloud.

Across the collection, expect roughly a third of the stories to go genuinely
physical, a third to threaten it, and a third to stay wholly at the edge of the
frame. Variety is the point — a book where every story delivers the same escalating
grotesque is as monotone as one where nothing is ever shown.

The one hard limit stands: a child's suffering is never an engine for a scare on its
own. If a child is harmed in a story's past, that harm must be what the story is
actually about.

## 12. The collection is a machine too

The stories are **fully standalone** — no shared geography, family, object, or
continuity, and no story depends on another having been read. Each is judged
entirely on its own.

That makes variety-tracking more important, not less, because rhyme is the only
thing holding the book together and rhyme curdles fast into repetition. Track across
the collection: haunt-logics used, ending types, POVs, decades, and how physical
each story got. Two stories ending on a wrong reflection is one story too many.

The ledger in `bible/book-bible.md` is the instrument. Fill the row before drafting.
